Clinical Application of Near Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

Alshorman, Altahla, Gao, Liu | 2024

Keywords: Near-infrared light, Blood oxygen saturation, Cervical spinal cord injury, Laminectomy, Durotomy

Final Summary

Title: Clinical Application of Near Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring (NIRS) in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

Research Question: Can NIRS be used as a non-invasive, real-time method for monitoring spinal cord blood perfusion during cervical spine surgery to predict neurological risks and optimize surgical strategies for patients with cervical spinal cord injury?

Methods: A total of 37 traumatic CSCI (Cervical Spinal Cord Injury) patients were analyzed. NIRS was used after laminectomy to assess regional spinal cord blood oxygen saturation (rSO2) levels and assess the need for further durotomy or extensive laminectomy based on SO2 values.

Key Findings: Significant correlations between rSO2 levels and intraoperative changes such as positioning, decompression, and instrumentation were found. Reduced rSO2 levels were associated with a higher risk of neurological complications post-surgery.

Conclusions: NIRS offers potential benefits for monitoring cervical spinal cord injury patients during surgery, predicting neurological risks, and optimizing surgical strategies to improve patient outcomes. The technology demonstrated safety, feasibility, and real-time assessment of the improvement of spinal cord blood supply. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and determine optimal use for various types of spinal cord injuries.

PREAMBLE

Title: Clinical Application of Near Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

Journal: Research Article, Volume 6 • Issue 4, 100

Key Findings: This study explores the clinical application of near-infrared blood oxygen monitoring (NIRS) in managing cervical spinal cord injury.

Methods: The research involved a non-invasive, continuous NIRS system to monitor regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) levels during cervical spine surgery.

Data Analysis: A total of 40 patients with cervical spinal cord injury undergoing anterior cervical discectomy and fusion were included. rSO2 levels were measured at four critical stages of the surgical procedure.

Results: The study found significant correlations between rSO2 levels and intraoperative changes, such as positioning, decompression, and instrumentation. Reduced rSO2 levels were associated with higher risk of neurological complications post-surgery.

Conclusion: The clinical application of NIRS offers potential benefits for monitoring cervical spinal cord injury patients during surgery, predicting neurological risks, and optimizing surgical strategies to improve patient outcomes.

ABSTRACT

This study explores the feasibility of using Near Infrared Light-Oxygen saturation (NIR-SO2) monitoring for cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI). A total of 37 patients were analyzed, with an improvement in ASIA grades during follow-up. The NIR-SO2 technology demonstrated safety and feasibility in reflecting the improvement of spinal cord blood supply. It was found that NIR-SO2 could monitor regional SO2 of CSCI patients and assess spinal cord perfusion and compression in real time, with significant improvements observed after laminectomy and durotomy. The study was conducted at the Department of Orthopedics, The Second Affiliated Hospital, Hubei University of Science and Technology, and Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China. The corresponding author is Jamal Alshorman from the Orthopedic department of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Clinical Medical College, Hubei University of Science and Technology, China. The research was published in the Journal of Spine Research and Surgery in December 2024. Keywords associated with the study include Near-infrared light, Blood oxygen saturation, Cervical spinal cord injury, Laminectomy, Durotomy.

INTRODUCTION

The paper discusses challenges in diagnosing and treating Cervical Spinal Cord Injury (CSCI), highlighting the need for early and adequate decompression. However, there's no uniform standard on whether further spinal canal decompression or durotomy is necessary following fracture reduction and internal fixation.

The authors propose the use of Near Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring (NIR) as a potential solution to assess the blood supply to the spinal cord non-invasively, which could help in determining the degree of CSCI, selecting treatment modalities, and evaluating outcomes. They note that while other methods such as optical devices and Doppler ultrasound have limitations, NIR has not been previously investigated for its use in guiding treatment for CSCI.

The paper also discusses the role of laminectomies and durotomy-duroplasty in improving spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP) and treating spinal cord compartment syndrome (SCCS). While some surgeons prefer laminectomy alone, others advocate for durotomy due to its ability to restore cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation and decrease intradural pressure.

In conclusion, the paper aims to explore the potential of NIR as a non-invasive, real-time method for monitoring spinal cord blood perfusion in CSCI patients, thereby helping to guide treatment decisions and predict prognosis. The paper also reiterates the need for safe and simple techniques to monitor blood flow at the injury site during surgery.

METHOD

In this study, the non-invasive NIR-SO2 technique was used to assess spinal cord blood perfusion after laminectomy for traumatic CSCI patients. Patients with edema, hematoma, or contusion, and ASIA grades A, B, and C were included, while those without fracture or dislocation, <18 years old, with normal MRI and no neurological symptoms, with spinal tumors, or ASIA D or E were excluded. After laminectomy, the dural pulse was checked to confirm blood reperfusion, and an incision was made when the dura was thick (dark) to confirm hematoma presence. The probe was inserted to determine local blood SO2 and microvascular blood flow. The NIR-SO2 levels between 58%~68% indicated no need for further durotomy or extensive laminectomy, while SO2 <58% required a durotomy with duroplasty. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 22, and the working principle of NIR-SO2 monitoring is based on the absorption of light in the NIR range by hemoglobin, which allows for the detection of blood oxygen parameters at a specific site.

RESULTS

The study, published in the Journal of Spine Research and Surgery in 2024, involved 37 patients with spinal cord injury due to fall, road traffic accidents (RTA), or falling objects. The mean age of the patients was 51.4 years, with a slightly higher number of males (62.2%).

The time from admission to operation ranged from 38 to 73 hours, with an average of 51.9 hours. Most patients underwent laminectomy (n=22), and near-infrared blood oxygen saturation (SO2) monitoring was performed in all cases.

Before laminectomy, the SO2 was 53.7%, which increased to 65.1% after the procedure for 22 patients (59.5%). For the remaining 15 patients (40.5%), the SO2 remained below 50% and required durotomy and duroplasty. The SO2 values before laminectomy, after laminectomy, and after durotomy were 39.7%, 42.3%, and 62.7%, respectively (P-value <0.01).

Neurological assessment showed that on the last follow-up (average time is 21 months), the ASIA grade changed for some patients: from A to B in one patient, A to C in five, and remained the same for ten. For patients with grade B, improvements to grade C occurred in four and seven, while turning to grade A was seen in one. Overall, 53.7% of patients showed improved neurological function after surgery.

The study suggests that near-infrared blood oxygen monitoring can be a useful tool in cervical spinal cord injury cases, as it did not cause any trauma or pain during the operation and provided real-time information on SO2 levels. However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and determine the optimal use of this technology for different types of spinal cord injuries.

DISCUSSION

The discussion section of the paper emphasizes the importance of detecting blood flow at an injury site during spinal cord injuries (CSCI) for effective surgical treatment. The study investigates the use of Near-Infrared Spectroscopy for the measurement of regional SpO2 (NIR-SO2) in CSCI, a method previously unexplored for traumatic CSCI.

The key findings suggest that monitoring spine blood reperfusion during surgery using NIR is advantageous due to its ability to measure the SO2 levels of bone, skin, and fat. The study includes 37 patients with varying injury intensities, types, operation times, and treatment methods. After laminectomy, SO2 was found to be more than 58% in 22 patients, indicating improved blood flow and a significant improvement in the final ASIA score. However, those with values below 50% required durotomy with duroplasty to achieve better outcomes.

The paper also highlights the risks associated with traditional durotomy procedures, necessitating their use only when necessary to evacuate hematomas and complete spinal cord decompression. Maintaining an appropriate mean arterial pressure (MAP) between 85 mmHg and 90 mmHg for seven days can help establish an appropriate spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP), but this method does not work for all patients.

The study concludes that NIR-SO2 is a valuable technique for determining whether a laminectomy is sufficient to restore blood flow or if further procedures are required, indirectly improving the injury-to-spinal cord pressure ratio and SCPP. The paper discusses the importance of ensuring the accuracy of the NIR-SO2 technique during surgery and provides guidance on monitoring SO2 values for making decisions regarding durotomy.

The study supports the benefits of NIR-SO2, suggesting its potential to provide early detection of hypoxia and ischemia in spinal tissues, evaluating short-term prognosis after SCI, and assessing the safety and significance of treatment methods. The technique offers advantages such as ease of use, high accuracy, low cost, real-time monitoring, non-invasiveness, and applicability across various departments. However, factors such as nutrition, weight, height, fitness, aging, gender, and growth scale can affect NIR-SO2 values among patients.

CONCLUSION

The conclusion of the paper highlights that Decompression in CSCI (Cervical Spinal Cord Injury) can lead to significant improvement, but not always. A unique technique like NIR-SO2 is suggested for monitoring blood flow during surgery, as it aids in determining the necessity of extensive laminectomy or durotomy with duroplasty.

Intraoperative Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) offers more information about blood reperfusion at the injury site and is a quantitative, real-time, non-invasive method for assessing spinal cord ischemia and hypoxia. When SO2 (the oxygen saturation of hemoglobin in tissues being measured by NIRS) ranges between 58%~68%, better recovery and prognosis can be expected without a durotomy. However, if SO2 is less than 58%, a durotomy may be required to achieve a significant outcome.

NIRS is also beneficial for evaluating the severity of SCI, selecting treatment modalities, determining their efficacy, and predicting patient prognosis. The procedure is safe, feasible, and without complications.
