Galvanin (TMEM154) is an electric-field sensor for directed cell migration

Belliveau, Footer, Platenkamp, Rodriguez, Kim, Prinz, van Loon, Lin, Eustis, Chan, Cohen, Theriot | 2026

Final Summary

Title: Galvanin identified as a direct electric-field sensor for cell migration in rapidly moving cells

In a study published in Cell (2026), Belliveau et al. discover that Galvanin, a membrane protein, acts as an essential electric-field sensor guiding directed cell migration in immune and epithelial cells. The research was conducted by a collaborative team from the University of Colorado Boulder, University of Washington, Princeton University, and Princeton's Omenn-Darling Bioengineering Institute.

The study aimed to identify proteins involved in electric-field sensing that support rapid electrotaxis responses. Galvanin was found to accumulate at the anodal side of cells in response to electric fields, triggering changes in protrusion and retraction patterns. The intracellular domain of Galvanin is crucial for directing subsequent migration responses, while a mutant protein without this domain failed to restore directional migration.

The research suggests that Galvanin plays a role in processes like skin injury and wound healing due to its involvement in electrotaxis. It functions upstream of intracellular signaling pathways, providing a physical mechanism for electric field sensing that leads to polarized signaling responses. The study also highlights differences in electrotaxis behavior between solitary motile cells and collective cell migration.

Limitations of the study include potential biases from gene knockdowns affecting cell-substrate adhesion, indirect changes in glycosylation due to UXS1 and GNPNAT1 regulation, underestimation of Galvanin's net charge, differences in Galvanin's effective diffusivity, reduced fluorescence, increased intracellular signal, and potential limitations in the ability to detect effects of positively charged ectodomain variants. The authors have filed a patent application for their methodology used in this study.

Additional resources and sequencing data can be found at specified links, with processed data files available on GitHub. The complete study is accessible upon request from Nathan Belliveau (nathan.bellieve@colorado.edu).

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Belliveau et al., published in Cell (2026), identifies Galvanin (TMEM154) as an electric-field sensor essential for directing cell migration in rapidly moving immune and epithelial cells. The researchers found that Galvanin relocalizes to the anodal side of cells in response to electric fields, triggering changes in protrusion and retraction patterns. These findings suggest that Galvanin acts as a direct sensor of electrical cues, linking electrical information with intracellular mechanisms for directed cell migration. The study was conducted by researchers from various institutions, including the University of Colorado Boulder, University of Washington, Princeton University, and Princeton's Omenn-Darling Bioengineering Institute.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the scientific paper discusses the significance of directed cell migration in various biological processes, focusing on mammals where it plays crucial roles in development, immune system function, and tissue regeneration. While much is known about chemotaxis mediated by transmembrane receptors, less is understood about how cells sense and respond to spatial information such as temperature, pH, or matrix stiffness gradients.

The paper then highlights the unique challenge of directed cell migration in response to acute injury, which generates new spatial information that guides cells towards the wound. Tissue injury creates endogenous electric fields with magnitudes ranging from 50-500 mV/mm, and many cell types, including epithelial cells and tissue-resident immune cells, have been found to migrate directionally in response to these electrical cues (electrotaxis or galvanotaxis).

The molecular mechanism of electric-field detection remains unclear. Although several intracellular signaling components known to be involved in chemotaxis have also been shown to contribute to electrotaxis, it is unlikely that the relatively weak wound-induced electric fields directly affect the localization or activity of intracellular signaling components due to electrostatic shielding and poor conductance across the plasma membrane.

The paper aims to identify cell-surface proteins that act as direct electric-field sensors in various cell types, focusing on rapidly moving cells such as immune cells and skin epidermal cells. The research will use an unbiased functional genomics approach to isolate human neutrophil-like cells based on their electrotaxis behavior and identify proteins involved in electric-field sensing, with the goal of discovering a dedicated electric-field sensor protein that supports rapid electrotaxis responses.

RESULTS

The study investigates the role of the protein Galvanin in electrotaxis, which is the movement of cells toward an electric field. Galvanin, with a high mobility and net negative charge, acts as an electric-field sensor, playing a crucial role in directing various cell types such as HL-60 neutrophils, EL4 T cells, and fish keratocytes towards the cathode. The intracellular domain of Galvanin is vital for regulating the subsequent directed movement response following spatial redistribution. A mutant protein without the intracellular domain failed to restore directional migration in a collagen assay. The net negative charge of the ectodomain was confirmed as essential for Galvanin's sensory function and electrotaxis, with highly negatively charged engineered ectodomains rescuing directed migration in knockout cell lines, whereas weakly positively charged ones did not. The study suggests that Galvanin could contribute to processes like skin injury and wound healing due to its role in electrotaxis.

DISCUSSION

The study identifies Galvanin, a membrane protein, as an electric-field sensor that mediates cathode-directed cell migration. Loss of Galvanin expression disrupts directed migration in response to an electric field. Galvanin accumulates at the anodal side of individual cells and may steer cell migration by locally activating retraction or inhibiting protrusion. The electrophoretic response of Galvanin is rapid, enabling rapid repolarization within minutes of exposure to an electric field. This study suggests that Galvanin functions upstream of intracellular signaling pathways and provides a physical mechanism for electric field sensing that is translated into polarized signaling responses. The work also highlights differences in electrotaxis behavior between solitary motile cells, such as immune cells, and collective cell migration.

The study used a screen to identify Galvanin as a key player in electrotaxis, a methodology for which the authors have filed a patent application. The research was supported by grants from the National Institutes of Health and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, among others. The authors thank members of the Theriot lab, Alex Mogilner, Sean Collins, Griffin Chure, Alexander Leydon, Jennifer Nemhauser, Takato Imaizumi, and Tom Daniel for their contributions.

Limitations of the study include potential biases in the screen due to gene knockdowns affecting cell-substrate adhesion, ineffective individual sgRNAs resulting in false negatives, indirect changes in cellular glycosylation due to UXS1 and GNPNAT1 regulation of glycan biosynthesis, underestimation of the inferred net charge due to lack of modeling of electroosmotic fluid flow at the cell surface, differences in Galvanin's effective diffusivity in migrating cells compared to latrunculin A-treated cells if it interacts with the actin cytoskeleton, and reduced fluorescence and increased intracellular signal in the construct used for biophysical characterization potentially limiting the ability to detect effects of positively charged ectodomain variants.
