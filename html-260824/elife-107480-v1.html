Linking spinal circuit reorganization to recovery after thoracic spinal cord injury

Shevtsova, Lockhart, Rybak, Magnuson, Danner | 2025

Final Summary

The study by Shevtsova et al. (eLife 2025;14:RP107480) investigates the reorganization of spinal locomotor circuits in rats following lateral hemisection and contusion injuries using a computational model. The research question focuses on understanding how these circuits can recover function after injury, with findings suggesting two distinct routes: recovery after lateral hemisection involves the formation of detour pathways; while recovery after contusion primarily relies on reorganization of sublesional networks and altered cervical circuit control.

The study's methods involve adapting a model from previous work on mouse spinal locomotor circuitry, simulating pre-injury and post-recovery gait expression for both injury types. Sensitivity analysis revealed that reactivated sublesional lumbar rhythm-generating circuits and balanced lumbar commissural connectivity are key determinants of post-injury gait expression.

The study's key findings suggest that injury symmetry influences the cord's reorganization, with effective recovery in both cases depending on engaging these lumbar networks, which could be potential targets for therapeutic intervention. However, limitations include not incorporating afferent feedback or body and limb biomechanics.

The conclusions emphasize the distinct deficits in interlimb coordination and gait expression following spinal cord injuries, with recovery routes varying between injury models. Clinical approaches aimed at restoring sublesional lumbar RG and commissural function may be more effective when tailored to injury symmetry. The study anticipates further development of computational models to better characterize injury profiles and assist in the development of therapeutic strategies.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Shevtsova et al. (eLife 2025;14:RP107480) presents a computational model of the rat spinal locomotor circuits, exploring how these circuits can reorganize following lateral hemisection or contusion injuries to mimic observed gaits in vivo. The simulation results closely match experimental findings, suggesting the emergence of detour circuits after lateral hemisection and plasticity of left-right and sensory inputs below the injury after a midline contusion.

The model, adapted from previous work on mouse spinal locomotor circuitry, reproduces pre-injury and post-recovery gait expression for both lateral hemisection and contusion injuries. The research suggests two distinct routes to restored function: recovery after lateral hemisection involves functional restoration of damaged descending drive and long propriospinal connections (implying compensatory plasticity through the formation of detour pathways); while recovery after a moderate midline contusion primarily relies on reorganization of spared sublesional networks and altered control of supralesional cervical circuits.

However, sensitivity analysis reveals that activated sublesional lumbar rhythm-generating circuits and appropriately balanced lumbar commissural connectivity are the key determinants of post-injury gait expression, implying that injury symmetry shapes the cord's reorganization, but effective recovery in both cases depends on engaging these lumbar networks, which could be potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction section of this scientific paper summarizes research on the recovery of locomotor function following incomplete spinal cord injuries. It highlights the reorganization of spared and severed descending pathways and spinal circuitry, but the specific plastic changes underlying functional recovery remain unclear. The study focuses on the spinal circuitry that controls speed-dependent interlimb coordination and gait expression, primarily through rhythm generators located in the lumbar and cervical enlargements. These rhythm generators are interconnected by commissural (left–right) and long propriospinal (lumbar–cervical) neuronal pathways, which define interlimb coordination. Thoracic spinal cord injuries disrupt these descending pathways and inter-enlargement connections, but the ability for speed-dependent gait expression following post-injury recovery can provide insights into the reorganization of these pathways. The study aims to investigate potential reorganization of neural connectivity underlying locomotor control following recovery from symmetrical contusion and asymmetrical hemisection injuries using computational models of spinal circuitry.

RESULTS

The study by Shevtsova et al., published in eLife (2025), investigates factors influencing frequency-dependent gait expression in spinal cord injury models. Two models are discussed: hemisection and contusion.

  1. Hemisection model: A left-sided spinal cord injury affecting the left hindlimb and lumbar region reveals that the drive to the ipsilesional (same side) hind/lumbar flexor rhythm generator center is the most important determinant of locomotor outcomes, with significant impact within a certain range.

  2. Contusion model: A symmetrical moderate thoracic contusion injury shows that the drive to the sublesional (below the injury site) lumbar flexor RG centers dominates sensitivity profiles. The balance between V0 and V3 commissural interneuron connectivity is crucial for maintaining post-injury gait expression.

Both models suggest that plasticity within the local circuitry linking left and right rhythm generators, as well as long propriospinal pathways and cervical CINs, have negligible effects on post-injury locomotor behavior. The study emphasizes restoring activation of sublesional lumbar rhythm-generating circuits and strengthened commissural pathways to shape post-injury locomotor patterns.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific paper focuses on the role of long propriospinal interneurons (LPNs) in interlimb coordination and gait expression in animals with spinal cord injuries, specifically hemisection and contusion. The research reveals that LPNs disrupt interlimb coordination post-injury but improve it in intact animals, suggesting reorganization or altered modulation of intra-enlargement commissural interneurons during recovery to strengthen left–right alternating influences.

The model has limitations, such as not incorporating afferent feedback or body and limb biomechanics, which could impact long propriospinal connection strengths necessary for reproducing experimental interlimb coordination. Future work will address these shortcomings and further explore the role of LPNs in spinal cord injury recovery.

The paper also discusses various connections like descending pathways and afferent feedback's roles in both pre- and post-injury locomotion. The model proposes that some connections might be mediated by afferent feedback, such as ascending LPNs receiving afferent feedback and commissural interneurons integrating input from various modalities. However, the model does not model reorganization of afferent feedback connectivity or phasic afferent feedback.

Future work should concentrate on incorporating phasic afferent feedback, its reorganization after injury, and its interaction with body and limb biomechanics into neuromechanical models. Experimental characterization of post-injury reorganization in lumbar circuits is needed to distinguish circuit reorganizations promoting locomotor recovery from those limiting it.

CONCLUSION

The study by Danner et al. (2023) reveals distinct deficits in interlimb coordination and gait expression following two spinal cord injury models during overground locomotion. These deficits differ between the injury models and are not limited to walk-trot speeds. A computational model of spinal locomotor circuitry replicated these behavioral changes, identifying principles of circuit reorganization supporting post-injury locomotor recovery.

After lateral hemisection injuries, recovered drive to the hindlimb was achieved by new or strengthened pathways that bypass the lesion. In contrast, symmetrical contusion injuries involved local left–right circuitry strengthening within each enlargement. The study highlights that injury symmetry influences the location and type of plasticity supporting recovery, with a shared requirement for reactivated sublesional lumbar rhythm-generating circuits and balanced lumbar commisural connectivity being crucial for post-injury gait expression. Clinical approaches aimed at restoring sublesional lumbar RG and commissural function may be more effective when tailored to injury symmetry. The study anticipates further development of computational models to better characterize injury profiles and assist in the development of therapeutic strategies.

METHODS

The scientific paper presents a method for calculating the distance between a point in a three-dimensional phase space (represented by phase differences LR, HL, and diag.) and idealized gaits (one-, two-, three-, and four-beat gaits). The distance is calculated using an equation that squares the argument of the difference between the non-normalized phase differences of the point and the idealized gait.

The study focuses on post-injury gait analysis, where the alpha (α) value was incrementally increased from 0.075 to 1.1, then decreased back, using a step size of 0.001, with each simulation running for 10 seconds at each step. If the normalized circular standard deviation met the criteria within 100 seconds, the mean phase differences were considered stable solutions. Initial conditions were randomized, and multiple runs were evaluated to ensure model stability. A weak noisy current was added to account for unstable trajectories.

A sensitivity analysis of injured models was also conducted, where parameter variations were quantified to understand their impact on locomotor behavior relative to the baseline parameter set. The EMD (Rubner et al., 1998) was computed as a summary outcome in a seven-dimensional feature space defined by the six interlimb phase differences and locomotor frequency.

Model parameters were perturbed multiplicatively on the logarithmic scale, and Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo training samples of 213 perturbations were generated to train an XGBoost gradient-boosted tree model as a surrogate for approximating the mapping of the perturbed parameters to the EMD. The surrogate's accuracy was assessed on an independent validation set, and Sobol sensitivity indices were estimated using the surrogate model with Saltelli sampling. First-order and total-order Sobol indices were reported with 95% confidence intervals, which should be interpreted as variance contributions within the local neighborhood based on the perturbation band [80%,125%], not as global sensitivities across the entire parameter space.
