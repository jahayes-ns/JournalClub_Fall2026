Expert-level probabilistic breathing event detector informs phenotyping of sleep apnea

Kjaer, Hanif, Brink-Kjaer, Olsen, Sum-Ping, Carrillo, Sands, Redline, Stone, Jennum, Mignot | 2026

Final Summary

The study aimed to develop an advanced probabilistic breathing event detector, named ABED (Apneic Breathing Event Detector), for the automated diagnosis of obstructive and central apneas, hypopneas, and isolated respiratory events in sleep studies. Trained on 5,456 polysomnographies and tested on 1,099 from six cohorts, ABED demonstrated a strong correlation (r² = 0.84) with expert annotations and achieved an F1 score of 0.78 across event types, outperforming human experts in two independent datasets. The model offers a probabilistic output, termed "apnotyping," providing insights into sleep disordered breathing etiology, which may enhance diagnostic accuracy and support personalized treatment strategies. The study used convolutional neural networks with batch normalization, ReLU activations, and bi-directional LSTM for temporal processing to achieve these results. Despite limitations in mild apnea range detection and computational efficiency, ABED offers new directions for characterizing sleep disordered breathing events.

ABSTRACT

The paper presents an automatic apneic breathing event detector developed to localize and classify obstructive apneas, central apneas, hypopneas, and isolated respiratory events in sleep studies, reducing the need for manual annotation. Trained on 5,456 polysomnographies and tested on 1,099 from six cohorts, the model demonstrates a strong correlation with expert annotations (r² = 0.84) and achieves an F1 score of 0.78 across event types. The model performs comparably or better than individual expert raters in two independent datasets. A probabilistic output, termed "apnotyping," offers insights into sleep disordered breathing etiology and may enhance diagnostic accuracy and support personalized treatment strategies, potentially improving patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction discusses sleep-related respiratory diseases (SDB), including central sleep apnea (CSA) and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The paper's focus is on the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), a metric used to measure the severity of these conditions, with apneas defined as complete cessations of breathing lasting at least 10 seconds. Central apneas (CA) are characterized by a lack of respiratory effort, while obstructive apneas (OA) involve upper airway obstruction. Hypopneas (HYPO), partial events, can be difficult to differentiate.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) has established clear definitions for these events. Single sleep apnea events can have both central and obstructive components and are known as "mixed apnea" events. Scoring inconsistencies and evolving HYPO definitions over the last 20 years contribute to discrepancies in OSA prevalence across studies, leading to complications in the epidemiological literature.

The article highlights the need for revising SDB event definitions, particularly differentiating central versus obstructive events, distinguishing events with and without desaturations/arousals, and basing definitions on pathophysiology and outcome variables. The hypoxic burden, a measure of oxygen desaturation, has been suggested as a potentially better measure of SDB severity. However, it does not capture SBD events without significant drops in blood oxygen levels, which can still cause sleep fragmentation and daytime symptoms.

The paper also addresses the inter- and intra-scorer variability in expert annotations of SDB events, contributing to scoring inconsistencies. This study aims to develop a deep learning model, the Apneic Breathing Event Detector (ABED), for detecting different types of SDB, providing insights into their pathophysiology, and potentially improving the reliability of sleep apnea diagnosis.

RESULTS

The 'results (combined)' section of the scientific paper introduces ABED, an Automated Breathing Event Detector designed to diagnose various sleep-disordered breathing events such as obstructive apneas, central apneas, hypopneas, and isolated respiratory events without arousals or desaturations.

Trained and tested using data from multiple studies involving over 1,000 participants, ABED demonstrated high accuracy in detecting sleep-disordered breathing events with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.84 between its predicted AHI (Apnea Hypopnea Index) and expert-annotated AHI. The model outperformed human experts in speed, taking only 14 seconds per average PSG (Polysomnography), potentially increasing diagnosis rates and alleviating the economic burden of undiagnosed sleep apnea.

ABED is resilient to variations in signal quality but has limitations with EEG and thoracic modalities. The model accurately identified signal quality and isolated respiratory events, as shown by its latent space visualization. ABED also provides a probabilistic view of apnea scoring that improves sleep apnea endotyping by offering more accurate predictions of loop gain, pharyngeal muscle compensation, and arousal threshold compared to manual annotations.

However, the model has limitations, including lower diagnostic accuracy in the none-to-mild apnea range, the need for training on additional datasets, and reduced computational efficiency due to resampling input signals to 8 Hz. Despite these limitations, ABED offers new directions for characterizing the full spectrum of sleep disordered breathing events.

METHODS

The study utilized data from seven cohorts: MESA, MrOS, WSC, CFS, DREEM, Alliance, and NSRR. A total of 6,555 full night polysomnography (PSG) recordings were used to train and evaluate ABED, a convolutional neural network for the detection and classification of sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) events such as obstructive apneas (OA), central apneas (CA), hypopneas (HYPO), and isolated respiratory events without arousals or desaturations (IRE).

The preprocessing and detection pipeline involved bandpass filtering, standardization, and resampling of breathing channels. ABED processes 240-second windows with 50% overlap, using a modified ResNet50 with batch normalization and ReLU activations, and bi-directional LSTM for temporal processing. The network architecture was chosen to capture both local morphological patterns and long-range dependencies in respiratory signals.

Training involved balanced sampling across event classes, optimized both event classification and localization, and employed loss functions based on cross entropy and Huber loss with Adam optimization and carefully tuned detection thresholds based on F1 score for each event type. Evaluation was performed using precision, recall, F1-score, and IOU metrics.

The endotype-apnotyping analysis involved examining the probability of each event type within each predicted event to evaluate ABED's ability to predict sleep apnea endotypes such as loop gain (Lgn), arousal threshold (ArT), and pharyngeal muscle compensation (Vcomp). The study used a stepwise linear regression approach for this analysis, and the effectiveness of the models was assessed using the adjusted R-squared coefficient.

The data and code for this project are available under restricted access due to ethical and legal constraints, while the DREEM dataset is publicly available via Zenodo. The custom source code, software packages, and versions used for this project can be found at https://github.com/RuudeResearch/ABED.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

This text appears to be a scientific article about a new deep learning model called the Apneic Breathing Event Detector (ABED). The ABED is designed to automatically detect apneas during sleep studies, and it achieves human-expert level accuracy across different cohorts. The model uses seven input modalities: nasal flow, oral thermistor, abdominal belt, thoracic belt, SpO2 (blood oxygen saturation), electromyography (EMG), electroencephalography (EEG), and electrooculography (EOG).

The ABED architecture involves channel mixing, channel amplification, 16 bottleneck blocks with residual connections, two bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) layers for temporal processing, two 1x60 kernel layers for determining 146 object class probabilities and positions, non-maximum suppression to remove overlapping predictions, and a multimodal arousal detector (MAD) to compute arousal and wake probability.

The authors also present the results of apnotyping, which involves predicting the cause of sleep apnea, such as loop gain (Lgn), arousal threshold (ArT), and pharyngeal muscle compensation (Vcomp). They use linear stepwise regression models to make these predictions based on summary statistics from true annotations, predicted annotations, and apnotyping from ABED.

The article includes several figures that illustrate the ABED model's architecture, sleep disordered breathing detection results, and apnotyping and apnea endotype predictions. The results show that the ABED model outperforms individual human experts in terms of F1-scores on two validation datasets (Alliance and DREEM).

Overall, this article presents a novel deep learning model for automatic sleep apnea detection and cause prediction, which could potentially reduce the need for labor-intensive manual scoring of breathing events during sleep studies.
