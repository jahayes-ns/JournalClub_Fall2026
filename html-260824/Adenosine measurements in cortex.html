Regulation of sleep homeostasis mediator adenosine by basal forebrain glutamatergic neurons

Peng, Wu, Song*, Zhang, Li, Xu†

Final Summary

The study, published in Nature Neuroscience by Peng et al., aims to investigate the role of basal forebrain (BF) glutamatergic neurons in regulating sleep and wakefulness. The research builds upon two separate studies: one developing a new sensor (Ado1.0 or GRABAdo) to detect adenosine levels, revealing higher amounts during different sleep stages; the other focusing on the role of BF VGLUT2+ neurons in sleep homeostasis, finding that their ablation results in increased wakefulness and decreased recovery sleep after prolonged wakefulness.

In this study, optogenetic activation of BF glutamatergic neurons during wakefulness increases arousal, while inhibition leads to drowsiness. The study identifies key brain regions involved in the BF's regulation of sleep and wakefulness, including the thalamus, cortex, and nucleus basalis magnocellularis (NBM). Further findings suggest that BF glutamatergic neurons are activated during natural sleep and wake periods, are modulated by adenosine, and play a role in sleep homeostasis through activity-dependent adenosine release.

The research provides valuable insights into the neural circuits involved in sleep regulation and offers potential therapeutic targets for sleep disorders. The study acknowledges collaborators and funding sources. In summary, the combined findings of these studies suggest that both adenosine signaling and BF VGLUT2+ neurons play crucial roles in regulating the sleep-wake cycle and have significant implications for understanding and treating various neurological disorders related to sleep regulation.

PREAMBLE

The combined preamble of the scientific paper discusses two separate studies.

The first study developed a new sensor (Ado1.0 or GRABAdo) to detect rapid changes in extracellular adenosine levels with high sensitivity and specificity. It was found that there is significantly higher amounts of extracellular adenosine during wakefulness, REM sleep, and non-REM sleep compared to each other. The study aimed to investigate the role of the basal forebrain (BF) in regulating the sleep-wake cycle by examining the activity of different neuronal types in BF and their contribution to adenosine dynamics during the sleep-wake cycle, but the results of this investigation were not discussed.

The second study focused on the role of basal forebrain glutamatergic neurons (VGLUT2+) in controlling sleep homeostasis. Researchers used mice with bilateral ablation of VGLUT2+ neurons in the BF and compared their sleep-wake behavior to littermate controls. The study found that mice with ablated BF VGLUT2+ neurons spent significantly more time in wakefulness, particularly during the active period (nighttime). There was no apparent difference in the quality of sleep or wakefulness between the groups as measured by EEG slow-wave activity during NREM sleep or theta activity during active wakefulness.

The study also examined recovery sleep after prolonged wakefulness and found that the loss of VGLUT2+ neurons in the BF affected recovery sleep, with significantly less NREM sleep in the lesion group during specific time periods (ZT 12–16). This implies that these glutamatergic neurons play a role in regulating sleep homeostasis.

In summary, both studies provide insights into the role of adenosine signaling and BF VGLUT2+ neurons in regulating the sleep-wake cycle and could have potential applications in understanding and treating various neurological disorders related to sleep regulation. The second study suggests that the loss of BF VGLUT2+ neurons impairs sleep homeostasis, leading to increased wakefulness and decreased recovery sleep after prolonged wakefulness.

DISCUSSION

The 2016 study published in Nature Neuroscience by Peng et al. explores the role of basal forebrain (BF) glutamatergic neurons in regulating sleep and wakefulness. Optogenetic activation of these neurons during wakefulness increased arousal, while inhibition led to drowsiness. The study also identified brain regions involved in the BF's regulation of sleep and wakefulness, including the thalamus, cortex, and nucleus basalis magnocellularis (NBM).

The research further indicates that BF glutamatergic neurons are activated during natural sleep and wake periods and are modulated by adenosine, a neurotransmitter known to promote sleep. Optogenetic stimulation of these neurons elicited activity-dependent adenosine release, suggesting they play a role in sleep homeostasis.

The study expands upon previous research on sleep homeostasis mediators and acknowledges collaborators and funding sources. The findings provide valuable insights into the neural circuits involved in sleep regulation and offer potential therapeutic targets for sleep disorders.
