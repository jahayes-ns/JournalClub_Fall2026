Exercise and the organ-brain axis: Regulation of neurological disorders by emerging exerkines

Dai, Dou, Nie, Sun, Chen, Fu, Zhang, Chu, Gao, Ge, Lin | 2021

Keywords: Exercise, Organ-Brain Axis, Neurological disorders, Peripheral organs, Crosstalk

Final Summary

The scientific paper explores the organ-brain axis, investigating how various peripheral organ diseases affect brain health and vice versa, focusing on skeletal muscle, heart, liver, adipose tissue, spleen, and their impact on neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and depression. Key findings include:

  1. Skeletal muscle neurofascial degeneration in sarcopenia causes reduced grip strength and memory decline in elderly females; heme release from atrophied muscles accelerates cognitive decline in AD.
  2. Heart failure leads to cognitive impairment, reduced gray matter density, and epigenetic modifications, while congenital heart disease causes cognitive deficits due to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion.
  3. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) compromises the blood-brain barrier, induces neuroinflammation, and disrupts oxidative stress homeostasis; hepatic metabolic dysfunction in mouse models activates NLRP3 inflammasomes in the brain.
  4. Dysfunctional adipose tissue alters adipokine secretion impacting cerebral energy metabolism in stroke, while adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells exhibit neuroprotective effects in traumatic brain injury.
  5. The spleen regulates immune responses and Aβ clearance from peripheral circulation; splenectomy accelerates AD progression.

Brain diseases can also influence skeletal muscle and the cardiovascular system, with AD linked to Aβ deposition within muscle tissue, activation of TGF-β-mediated atrophic signaling pathways, and mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction in early stages of the disease.

Exercise improves various organ-brain axes and can impact neurological disorders through mechanisms such as improving cerebral blood flow, regulating bioactive factor secretion, or modulating the autonomic nervous system and chronic inflammation. Exerkines, signaling molecules produced during exercise that cross the blood-brain barrier, play a significant role in these connections between organs. Irisin and lactate have been identified as key exerkines with neuroprotective effects.

Research is still needed to fully understand the mechanisms by which these exerkines act in brain diseases and to identify potential therapeutic targets for exercise-based interventions. Larger-scale, systematic clinical research is also required to fully understand lactate's role in regulating brain health and neurological disorders.

PREAMBLE

The 'preamble (combined)' section of the scientific paper summarizes the organ-brain axis, focusing on how peripheral organ diseases influence brain health and vice versa. Key areas discussed include skeletal muscle, heart, liver, adipose tissue, and spleen.

  1. Skeletal muscle: Neurofascial degeneration in sarcopenia is linked to reduced grip strength and memory decline in elderly females. Heme release from atrophied muscles accelerates cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease (AD).

  2. Heart: Heart failure leads to cognitive impairment, reduced gray matter density, and epigenetic modifications. Congenital heart disease causes cognitive deficits due to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion.

  3. Liver: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) compromises the blood-brain barrier, induces neuroinflammation, and disrupts oxidative stress homeostasis. Hepatic metabolic dysfunction in mouse models activates NLRP3 inflammasomes in the brain, promoting Aβ-related inflammatory factor secretion and accelerating AD progression.

  4. Adipose tissue: Dysfunctional adipose tissue alters adipokine secretion impacting cerebral energy metabolism in stroke. Adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells exhibit neuroprotective effects in traumatic brain injury.

  5. Spleen: The spleen regulates immune responses and Aβ clearance from peripheral circulation. Splenectomy accelerates AD progression, characterized by increased neuroinflammation, cerebral Aβ deposition, and tau hyperphosphorylation.

Brain diseases can also influence skeletal muscle and the cardiovascular system. AD is linked to Aβ deposition within muscle tissue, activation of TGF-β-mediated atrophic signaling pathways, and mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction in early stages of the disease.

Exercise improves various organ-brain axes and can impact neurological disorders such as AD and depression by improving cerebral blood flow, regulating bioactive factor secretion, or modulating the autonomic nervous system and chronic inflammation.

Exerkines, signaling molecules produced during exercise that cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), play a significant role in these connections between organs. Irisin, a myokine produced by skeletal muscle during exercise, enhances neural plasticity by upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and exhibiting broad anti-inflammatory properties.

Lactate, another exerkine, promotes neuroplasticity and cognitive function within the brain by activating the SIRT1–PGC-1α–FNDC5 signaling pathway, leading to increased levels of BDNF and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the hippocampus.

The text also discusses the spleen-brain axis in the context of stroke, where exercise interventions have been shown to modulate the spleen–brain axis and mitigate stroke-related inflammatory processes. However, research is still needed to fully understand the mechanisms by which these exerkines act in brain diseases and to identify potential therapeutic targets for exercise-based interventions.

Lastly, lactate's role in regulating brain health and neurological disorders remains limited in human studies, with preliminary findings suggesting that lactate infusion can increase serum BDNF levels and improve cognitive performance in some studies. Larger-scale, systematic clinical research is needed to fully understand its effects.
