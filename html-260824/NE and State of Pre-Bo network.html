Noradrenergic neuromodulation produces a NMDAR-dependent network state of respiratory rhythmogenesis in the preBötzinger Complex

Tryba, Viemari, Wang, Garcia | 2026

Final Summary

The research 'NE Modulation of Inspiratory Drive through Preservation of Neuronal Synchronization via NMDAR Activation' and 'Characterizing Synchrony in Neuronal Populations Using a Novel Statistical Approach' explore the effects of norepinephrine (NE) and NMDA receptors (NMDAR) on the preBotzinger Complex (preBötC), a crucial area for respiratory rhythm generation. The studies used electrophysiological techniques, pharmacology, in silico modeling, and data analysis to investigate how NE modulation impacts neuronal excitability, unit fidelity, and network synchrony.

Key findings include: 1. NE enhances preBötC network burst amplitude and frequency, with NMDAR playing a key role in mediating these changes. 2. Blocking NMDAR reduces burst amplitude but typically increases burst frequency and decreases overall network synchrony. 3. NE modulation leads to changes in unit fidelity, with some units becoming more or less synchronized under different conditions. 4. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) differently affects preBOTC neurons, leading to changes in bursting patterns and synchronization within the network.

These findings provide insights into the complexity of the respiratory rhythm-generating network and may contribute to understanding breathing disorders such as sleep apnea and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study 'NE Modulation of Inspiratory Drive through Preservation of Neuronal Synchronization via NMDAR Activation' explores the role of Noradrenaline (NE) in influencing inspiratory drive during fictive inspiration by focusing on the preBötzinger complex (preBötC). Experimental methods involved electrophysiological techniques, pharmacology, in silico modeling, and data analysis.

The research demonstrates that NE modulation enhances NMDAR activity, preserving neuronal synchronization during inspiratory bursts while increasing preBötC activity frequency. These findings suggest a novel role for NE in stabilizing and amplifying the inspiratory network rhythm, potentially contributing to future research on respiratory disorders and therapeutic interventions targeting the noradrenergic system.

In another study, 'Characterizing Synchrony in Neuronal Populations Using a Novel Statistical Approach,' a custom-written C++ simulation software with exponential Euler method for numerical integration was used to analyze neuron synchronization in various scenarios. The overall synchrony of the population was characterized using an adapted statistic calculating an average synchrony score (χ) based on spike count variations across neurons and time.

RESULTS

This scientific research focuses on the impact of norepinephrine (NE) and NMDA receptors (NMDAR) on the neural network in the preBotanical area (preBötC), essential for respiratory rhythm generation.

The study used multi-electrode array recordings to observe changes in neuronal excitability under baseline conditions, during NE modulation, and after subsequent blockade of NMDAR with APV. Key findings include:

  1. NE has a heterogeneous impact on neuronal excitability, reducing average action potential number during network bursts but increasing action potential frequency.
  2. APV application further reduced both action potential number and suppressed the NE-driven increase in action potential frequency.
  3. NE shifted spike timing closer to or ahead of the peak of the network burst, with APV not affecting this shift.
  4. NE recruited five previously silent units into participating in preBötC network bursts and increased firing frequency in the majority of inspiratory units.
  5. The study also investigated the effects of NE on interneuronal dynamics using computer simulations (CaN model network) and found that NMDAR play a key role in mediating changes to network activity.

Furthermore, the study explored the effects of NE on unit fidelity, revealing that NE modulation led to changes in unit fidelity with four low-frequency (LF) units becoming high-frequency (HF) and five HF units becoming LF. After applying APV to block NMDAR activity, inspiratory unit fidelity was reduced, causing 15 HF units to become LF and silencing three LF units.

The research also found that blocking NMDAR activity decreased the percentage of preBötC network burst cycles inspiratory units participate in, increased the probability of cycle skipping, and reduced the synchrony score of the networks, supporting the role of NMDAR in synchrony as predicted by an in silico model. The study was a preprint, not yet certified by peer review, available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

DISCUSSION

The combined discussion from the scientific papers revolves around the effects of norepinephrine (NE) and NMDA receptor blockade on the pre-Botzinger Complex (preBötC), a crucial area responsible for respiratory rhythm generation.

  1. NE modulation enhances preBötC network's burst amplitude and frequency, with an increase in large amplitude fictive sigh burst frequency observed in heatmaps. Blocking NMDAR, however, doesn't prevent transmission to trigger XII motor output, indicating the preBötC's ability to drive bursting in the hypoglossal (XII) motor pool even after blocking NMDAR. Blocking NMDAR reduces burst amplitude but typically enhances burst frequency, as shown in later heatmaps.

  2. The second paper focuses on the effects of NMDA receptor blockade with MK-801 on breathing in adult rats. It reveals that blockade reduces the overall synchrony score of the preBOTC network, decreases the percentage of network bursts that inspiratory units participate in, and increases the number of network burst cycles they skip.

  3. The third paper investigates the impact of chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) on inspiratory activity at the level of the preBötzinger Complex. CIH differentially affects neurons within the preBOTC, with some becoming more synchronized under hypoxic conditions and others less so, due to changes in excitatory and inhibitory synaptic connections between preBOTC neurons and other brain regions, as well as alterations in intrinsic properties of the neurons. The study also explores the role of NMDA receptors in mediating these effects, suggesting that CIH alters their function, leading to changes in bursting patterns and synchronization within the preBOTC network.

These findings offer insights into the complexity of the respiratory rhythm-generating network and contribute to understanding breathing disorders such as sleep apnea and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
