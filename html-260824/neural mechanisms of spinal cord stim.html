Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation

Balaguer, Prat-Ortega, Ostrowski, Borda, Verma, Yadav, Sorensen, de Freitas, Ensel, Donadio, Liang, Ho, Damiani, Grigsby, Fields, Gonzalez-Martinez, Gerszten, Fisher, Weber, Pirondini, Capogrosso | 2025

Final Summary

In the study 'Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation', Balaguer et al. challenge a previous hypothesis regarding the integration of artificial (spinal cord stimulation) and natural neural inputs to restore motor function post-paralysis, such as from spinal cord injury or stroke. The researchers used a model to show that residual supraspinal excitatory drive modulates motoneuron membrane potentials, enabling subthreshold SCS pulses to trigger action potentials and entrain motoneuron activity to the stimulation interpulse interval.

The key findings include: 1) highly entrained motor units exhibiting peaks at multiples of SCS interpulse intervals while still allowing force modulation; 2) increased stimulation amplitude leading to involuntary responses in participants with severe lesions; 3) subthreshold SCS efficacy decreasing with lesion severity; 4) adding inhibitory neurons expanding the voluntary movement regime and improving fine motor control; and 5) stroke patients' ability to suppress unwanted reflexes induced by SCS.

The study suggests that residual supraspinal inputs play a crucial role in restoring voluntary control during spinal cord stimulation, offering an alternative explanation for the observation of facilitated voluntary movement. The research has implications for improving spinal cord stimulation technologies for stroke and provides insights into the neural mechanisms underlying voluntary motor control restoration after paralysis.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In the study 'Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation', Josep-Maria Balaguer et al. present findings that challenge a previous hypothesis regarding how spinal cord stimulation (SCS) restores motor control after paralysis. The researchers discovered that residual brain inputs do not facilitate SCS to motoneurons, but instead modulate their membrane potential to convert subthreshold SCS pulses into suprathreshold action potentials, entraining motoneuron activity to SCS. However, these residual brain inputs can control firing rates by modulating the number of transformed subthreshold pulses. The study also predicts that residual inhibitory drive could silence unwanted motor neuron activity, expanding the functional set of SCS parameters. The research also indicates an inherent limitation of SCS in cases of severe injury due to a restriction in the set of functional stimulation parameters.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction section of the scientific paper discusses the use of epidural electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in improving motor control for individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Clinical trials have observed that during SCS, paralyzed limbs can appear controllable, suggesting that SCS facilitates volitional movement rather than generating it.

However, previous studies focused on the neural pathways directly recruited by SCS or the involvement of spinal circuits such as reflexes and central pattern generators, but they overlooked the contribution of residual supraspinal fibers in explaining the facilitation of 'voluntary' movements. The leading hypothesis is that SCS increases the excitability of spinal circuits, particularly motoneurons, bringing their membrane potential closer to the firing threshold. However, it is challenging to conceive how pulsatile stimulation could lead to such membrane modulation given the stimulation frequencies and membrane time constants at play.

The study presented in this paper aims to understand how residual supraspinal excitatory drive and SCS pulses are integrated in motoneurons to enable volitional motor control. The researchers built a model of the motoneuron membrane potential, integrated excitatory inputs from sensory afferents recruited by SCS, and residual supraspinal fibers. The study found that in the presence of residual supraspinal inputs, motoneuron action potentials are triggered by otherwise subthreshold SCS pulses, becoming effectively entrained to the stimulation interpulse interval. The research refutes the hypothesis that SCS facilitates residual supraspinal inputs by tonically increasing motoneuron excitability and provides an alternative explanation for the observation that SCS facilitates voluntary movement.

RESULTS

The study investigates the modulation of force during Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) and its relation to lesion severity, supraspinal excitatory and inhibitory drive. Key findings include:

  1. Highly entrained motor units in SCS showed clear peaks at multiples of SCS interpulse intervals for all force levels, yet participants could still modulate firing to follow the sinusoidal profile. Higher force levels led to less pulse skipping by shifting the Inter-Stimulus Interval (ISI) histogram toward smaller values.

  2. Subthreshold SCS efficacy may depend on lesion severity, with increased stimulation amplitude leading to involuntary motor responses in participants with severe lesions. A biophysical model demonstrated that increasing lesion severity reduces the size of the voluntary movement regime.

  3. Experiments on monkey Mk-JC showed that low SCS amplitudes did not potentiate muscle activity produced by ICS, while higher subthreshold SCS amplitudes did, indicating a shift into the voluntary movement regime. The number of SCS parameters in the voluntary regime shrunk with lesion severity.

  4. Adding a population of inhibitory neurons expanded the voluntary regime by enlarging it into the involuntary suprathreshold movement regime, although lesion severity still reduced the size of the voluntary regime. Inhibition also increased the ability to modulate motoneuron firing rate and execute fine motor control tasks.

  5. Two participants with stroke from a clinical trial were able to inhibit SCS pulses, thereby suppressing unwanted flexor reflexes induced by SCS, demonstrating their ability to volitionally inhibit suprathreshold SCS.

DISCUSSION

The research article "Neural mechanisms underlying voluntary control during spinal cord stimulation," published in Neuron on November 19, 2025, investigates the integration of artificially induced activity (spinal cord stimulation) to restore voluntary control over motoneuron firing. Key findings show that neural firing can be modulated by natural activity even when highly entrained to pulsatile artificial stimulation.
