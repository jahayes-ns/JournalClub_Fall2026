Final Summary

The research paper titled "A Projection Atlas of Excitatory and Inhibitory Inputs to the preBötzinger Complex" investigates the neural circuitry underlying breathing regulation by mapping the connections from various brain regions to the preBötzinger complex (preBötC), a crucial region for breath rhythm generation. The study utilized transgenic mice, viral tracer injections, and immunohistochemistry to identify both excitatory (glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) input neurons in multiple brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, periaqueductal gray, midbrain reticular nucleus, red nucleus, parasubthalamic nucleus, dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus, central amygdala, and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis.

The findings suggest that the preBötC acts as a hub for multimodal respiratory control, receiving inputs from diverse brain regions involved in homeostatic chemosensory and mechanosensory pathways, emotional processing, volitional motor planning, and arousal and defensive behaviors. The study's results reveal widespread, bilateral, and functionally diverse excitatory cortical inputs to the preBötC, providing valuable insights into the complex interplay between higher brain regions and respiratory control. Further research is required to understand the specific functional roles of these connections in breathing regulation and other physiological processes. The study's conclusions emphasize that the preBötC plays a central role in repurposing a core rhythmogenic network to support diverse respiratory modes, integrating brainstem reflex circuits, local ventral respiratory column networks, nociceptive and defensive pathways, pontine and midbrain hubs, and forebrain and limbic systems.

Title: A Projection Atlas of Excitatory and Inhibitory Inputs to the preBötzinger Complex

Authors: Elora K. Reilly, Aleeza Kadri, Joseph W. Arthurs, Jonathan R. Sedano, Allison N. Brezinski, Nathan A. Baertsch (Seattle Children’s Research Institute, Seattle, Washington, United States; University of Washington, Seattle, Washington, United States)

Summary: This research article presents a detailed atlas highlighting the pathways of both excitatory and inhibitory neural projections to the preBötzinger complex (preBötC), a key region controlling rhythmic breathing. The study used various techniques including viral tracer injections, immunohistochemistry, and confocal microscopy on rodent brains. The data reveals numerous connections from diverse brain regions to the preBötC, suggesting that it acts as a hub for multimodal respiratory control. These findings provide valuable insights into the neural circuitry underpinning breathing regulation.

ABSTRACT

The study presents an atlas of excitatory (glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) brain regions that project to the preBötzinger complex, a key area responsible for breath rhythm generation. Using a dual recombinase-dependent retrograde tracing approach, the research identifies various mouse brain areas contributing to these inputs, establishing the preBötzinger complex as a hub integrating multimodal signals to regulate rhythm generation and patterning. The findings highlight the preBötzinger complex's function as being shaped by diverse long-range excitatory and inhibitory influences, with this atlas serving as a foundation for further investigations into the roles of higher-order brain regions in shaping breathing across physiological, behavioral, and pathological contexts. The study aims to systematically map the sources of inputs to the preBötzinger complex, thus providing insights into how sensory signals, behavior, and brain state influence breathing rhythms.

INTRODUCTION

The paper introduces a study aimed at understanding the complex mechanisms controlling breathing, which is integrated with various brain functions beyond its basic homeostatic role. Unlike other automatic functions, breathing adapts to behaviors such as speech, adopts volitional demands, and is strongly influenced by state. The brainstem's ventrolateral medulla, specifically the ventral respiratory column (VRC), contains the primary rhythm-generating circuits for breathing, with the preB€otzinger complex (preB€otC) as the principal inspiration rhythmogenic site.

The preB€otC receives inputs from various brain regions, including homeostatic chemosensory and mechanosensory pathways, limbic structures involved in emotional processing, cortical areas implicated in volitional motor planning, and midbrain circuits associated with arousal and defensive behaviors. The study uses an efficient dual-recombinase tracing strategy to identify the molecular identity and prevalence of projecting populations, aiming to provide an anatomical framework for understanding multimodal breathing regulation. The research reinforces the preB€otC as an embedded hub within a broadly distributed network, linking survival-driven homeostatic control with volitional, behavioral, and affective modulation.

METHODS

The study conducted methods approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Transgenic mouse lines were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and crossed to produce heterozygous offspring for two genetic crosses: Vglut2-ires-Cre and Vgat-ires-Cre. At 6 weeks of age, these mice received a unilateral stereotaxic injection of rgAAV-EF1a-FlpO virus mixed with AAV1-EF1a-eYFP in the preB€otC region. Three weeks after surgery, mice were perfused, brains sectioned coronally, and sections imaged to verify injection site and visualize labeled projections. The images were processed to enhance neuron visibility, perform cell counts, and generate composite projection maps using QuPath and ImageJ software. The sex of the selected mice was 3 female for Vglut2;Ai65D, and 1 male and 2 females for Vgat;Ai65D. A representative mouse from each genotype was chosen to illustrate the distribution of labeled neurons.

RESULTS

The study explores neural connections to the preBötzinger complex (preBötC), a vital region for respiratory rhythm generation. Using genetic labeling, it identified both excitatory (glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) input neurons in various brain regions.

Excitatory input neurons were found in the periaqueductal gray (PAG), midbrain reticular nucleus (mRt), red nucleus (RN), pontine reticular nucleus (PNc), lateral hypothalamic area (LH), parasubthalamic nucleus (PSTH), dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus (DM), and cortex, with the most extensive labeling in layer 5. Inhibitory input neurons were concentrated within the central amygdala (CeA) and a cluster in the ipsilateral bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST).

The study reveals widespread, bilateral, and functionally diverse excitatory cortical inputs to the preBötC. The most caudal clusters were located in the temporal association area and ventral auditory cortex, expanding rostrally into primary and dorsal auditory regions, with labeled neurons becoming increasingly prominent contralaterally. Excitatory input neurons subsequently moved dorsally from auditory cortex into motor regions, also found within the insula, infralimbic area, and anterior cingulate cortex.

This study offers insights into the complex interplay between higher brain regions and respiratory control; however, further research is needed to understand the specific functional roles of these connections in breathing regulation and other physiological processes.

DISCUSSION

The 'discussion' section of the scientific paper outlines various brain regions connected to the preBötzinger complex (preBöC), a key region for respiratory rhythm generation.

  1. Cerebral cortex, spinal trigeminal nucleus (Sp5c), parabrachial complex (PB), rostral ventral medulla (RVM), and pontine and midbrain regions were identified as key connections to the preBöC. These connections suggest a network for rapid modulation of inspiratory rhythm in response to various stimuli, including aversive stimuli, changes in arousal or vigilance, and pharmacological states.

  2. Emotional and threat-linked control: Affective and threat-processing circuits such as the extended amygdala, midbrain defensive networks, and cortical interoceptive networks can influence inspiratory rhythm through direct projections to the preBötC. These connections may be linked to fear or anxiety-associated respiratory changes.

  3. Volitional and behavioral integration: Breathing must coordinate with behaviors that share motor effectors with respiration. Orofacial sensorimotor signals, cortical inputs, and pontine reticular inputs provide routes for cortical and behavioral signals to influence breathing.

The paper concludes by stating that the preBötC acts as a central nexus where convergent excitatory and inhibitory signals repurpose a core rhythmogenic network to support diverse respiratory modes, with brainstem reflex circuits stabilizing eupnea (normal breathing), local ventral respiratory column networks sustaining rhythm and pattern coupling, nociceptive and defensive pathways enabling rapid suppression or reconfiguration, pontine and midbrain hubs implementing state-dependent mode switching, and forebrain and limbic systems providing direct access for volitional and affective modulation.
