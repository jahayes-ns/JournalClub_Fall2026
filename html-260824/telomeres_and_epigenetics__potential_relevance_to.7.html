Topical review: Telomeres and epigenetics: Potential relevance to chronic pain

Sibille, Witek-Janusek, Mathews, Fillingim

Final Summary

This scientific paper investigates the influence of epigenetics on pain perception and management, focusing on how stress or pain experiences could result in molecular changes like accelerated cellular aging, reduced telomere length, or gene expression alterations. The research examines key epigenetic processes such as DNA methylation, histone modification, and microRNA regulation, emphasizing the impact of genetic makeup, past experiences, and environment on individual pain tolerance.

Research examples include links between epigenetics and nociceptive responses in rodents and human chronic conditions like headaches, migraines, cluster headaches, chronic bladder pain syndrome, endometriosis, age-associated disk degeneration, and chronic back pain. The paper proposes that manipulating specific epigenetic receptors could potentially be used for pain management.

Further research is recommended to ascertain the significance of these molecular responses in chronic pain, explore potential clinical implications, and investigate epigenetic, telomere, and telomerase changes with pain treatments.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper explores the role of epigenetics in pain perception and management. It presents a model linking environmental factors, internal factors, and genome functioning, suggesting that stress or pain experiences could lead to molecular changes such as accelerated cellular aging, reduced telomere length, or epigenetic modifications affecting gene expression.

Key epigenetic processes discussed include DNA methylation, histone modification, and microRNA regulation. The paper highlights the interplay between genetic makeup, past experiences, and environment in determining individual variability in pain experience.

Research examples are provided linking epigenetics to nociceptive responses in rodents and human chronic pain conditions like primary headaches, migraines, cluster headaches, chronic bladder pain syndrome, endometriosis, age-associated disk degeneration, and chronic back pain.

The paper suggests that manipulating certain epigenetic receptors could be a potential therapeutic strategy for pain management. The authors emphasize the growing body of evidence linking epigenetics to pain perception and offer potential avenues for future research and clinical applications. However, they declare a conflict of interest as one author is a stockholder in Algynomics.

Further research is needed to determine the significance of these molecular responses in chronic pain, explore potential clinical implications, and investigate epigenetic, telomere, and telomerase changes with pain treatments.
