Final Summary

In the study by Zholudeva et al., published in Science Translational Medicine (2026), researchers engineered human cervical V2a-enriched spinal interneurons from induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) and transplanted them into a rat model with cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). The key findings were that these engineered spinal interneurons exhibited functional integration into the injured host's phrenic network, establishing synaptic connections and improving diaphragm function after SCI. They also gave rise to glial cells and new neuronal populations post-transplantation. The study suggests a promising avenue for cell therapy in spinal cord injuries, particularly using V2a interneuron-enriched donor cells for spinal cord injury repair. However, limitations include potential risks of ectopic migration, variability in cell survival after transplantation, and influence of local injury environment on differentiation or maturation of transplanted cells. Further investigations are necessary to confirm these findings and explore long-term effects and scalability of the cell production processes.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In the study by Zholudeva et al., published in Science Translational Medicine (2026), researchers engineered human spinal interneurons enriched with channelrhodopsin-2 from an induced pluripotent stem cell line. The team evaluated these V2a-enriched spinal interneurons (SpINs) following transplantation into a rat model of cervical spinal cord injury (SCI).

In vitro multielectrode array recordings showed that the engineered SpINs exhibited spontaneous and light-evoked activity, as well as functional connectivity with spinal motor neurons. Post-transplantation, pseudorabies virus tracing from the diaphragm revealed donor neurons synaptically connected with the injured phrenic motor network.

Using optogenetics and multiunit electrophysiology, the researchers demonstrated that transplanted human SpINs could both receive and provide functional synaptic connections with injured motor networks, reflecting a functional neuronal relay. Single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic analyses confirmed the maturation of transplants into various excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations.

The study suggests that human cervical V2a-enriched SpINs can establish functional host-transplant connectivity and potentially improve motor recovery post-SCI, paving the way for potential reparative treatments for SCI.

INTRODUCTION

The paper discusses the lack of effective treatments for spinal cord injury (SCI), despite current treatments like rehabilitation and neuromodulation improving quality of life. The authors propose a new approach that combines regenerative medicine with intrinsic neuroplasticity. Specifically, they focus on VSX2-expressing V2a interneurons, which are critical for locomotor rhythm, respiratory circuits, and plasticity after injury or disease.

The authors differentiated VSX2-positive V2a-enriched spinal interneurons (SpINs) from optogenetically engineered human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). They then demonstrated the functional integration of these V2a-enriched SpINs with injured spinal cords, focusing on their ability to form synaptic connectivity within the cervical spinal respiratory network, specifically the phrenic circuit. This study aims to pave the way for targeted restoration and recovery after SCI.
