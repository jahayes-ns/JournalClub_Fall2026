Hypothalamic Glutamatergic/Orexinergic Neurons Attenuate Fentanyl-Induced Respiratory Depression via Medullary Pre-BöTzinger Complex Pathways

Arakawa, Hiroyuki, Arakawa, Keiko, Levitt, Erica S. | Not specified

Keywords: breathing, chemogenetics, hypothalamus, opioids, orexin, respiratory depression

Final Summary

This scientific paper investigates the role of hypothalamic orexinergic and glutamatergic neurons in counteracting fentanyl-induced respiratory depression (FIRD) in mice. The research question focuses on understanding how these neurons modulate opioid-induced respiratory depression, particularly those in the lateral hypothalamus (LH), which project to the pre-Boetzinger complex (preBötC) in the medulla and stimulate ventilation.

The methods used include immunohistochemistry and Whole-Body Plethysmography (WBP) on mice to observe changes in neurotransmitters like oxytocin (OXT), vasopressin (AVP), and orexin A/B (ORX) under various conditions. The findings suggest that activation of LH–orexin/glutamatergic projections to the medulla drive respiratory activity, and this could be used as potential therapeutics for opioid-induced respiratory depression.

However, the study also found that PVN glutamatergic neurons modulate respiratory function but may not play a significant role in attenuating opioid-induced respiratory depression under hypercapnia conditions. Further research is needed to fully understand these mechanisms and their therapeutic applications.

The key findings include the robust projection of LH Orexin neurons to the preBötC, activation of which stimulates breathing and attenuates FIRD, an effect blocked by ORX antagonists. PVN glutamatergic neurons have distinct projections to various regions and stimulate breathing in room air but not in hypercapnia.

The study highlights the importance of understanding the circuitry of Orexin signaling in modulating opioid effects and respiratory control networks, referencing previous studies on oxytocin (OX1R) pathways in breathing control. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study investigates the role of hypothalamic orexinergic and glutamatergic neurons in counteracting fentanyl-induced respiratory depression (FIRD) in mice. Hypothalamic neurons, particularly those in the lateral hypothalamus (LH), are found to project to the pre-Boetzinger complex (preBötC) in the medulla and stimulate ventilation. Hypercapnia activates orexin neurons in the LH but not in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN). Pharmacological blockade of orexin signaling exacerbated FIRD, while activation of dorsal hypothalamic neurons projecting to the preBötC increased respiratory rate and attenuated FIRD. Activation of LH–glutamatergic neurons successfully reduced FIRD, whereas activation of PVN glutamatergic neurons had minimal effects on respiratory parameters. The findings suggest that LH–orexin/glutamatergic projections to the medulla drive respiratory activity and may be used as potential therapeutics for opioid-induced respiratory depression.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper explores the role of hypothalamic neurons in modulating Fentanyl-induced respiratory depression (FIRD). The study used immunohistochemistry and Whole-Body Plethysmography (WBP) on mice to observe changes in neurotransmitters like oxytocin (OXT), vasopressin (AVP), and orexin A/B (ORX) in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and lateral hypothalamus (LH).

Hypercapnia exposure increased activation of these neurons, while Fentanyl induced respiratory depression. An OXT antagonist significantly reduced this effect, suggesting a crucial role for OXT in modulating FIRD. The study used two-tailed Student's t-tests, Welch's t-tests, and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni’s correction to analyze respiratory parameters and locomotor movements under various conditions.

The findings could have implications for understanding and developing treatments for opioid-induced respiratory depression. However, further studies are needed to fully explore these mechanisms and their therapeutic applications.

RESULTS

The study investigates the role of glutamatergic neurons in the Paraventricular Nucleus (PVN) and their impact on respiratory activity.

Key findings include: 1. PVN projections are primarily located in the medullary raphe/Reticular Tegmental Nucleus (RTN), with sparse presence in other regions like BötC/preBötC and NTS. 2. Activating PVN glutamatergic neurons increased respiratory rate in normal conditions but not under hypercapnia. This effect persisted even after an orexin receptor antagonist, although it was reduced in the presence of fentanyl or hypercapnia. 3. Activation of these neurons increased peak inspiratory and expiratory flows in normal conditions but had no effect on locomotive movements. 4. Activating PVN neurons projecting to preBötC had little impact on respiratory rate, peak flows, but induced decreases in time spent immobile, suggesting a role in non-locomotive activity.

The study suggests that PVN glutamatergic neurons modulate respiratory function and may be involved in attenuating opioid-induced respiratory depression under normal conditions, although further research is needed to fully understand their roles and potential therapeutic applications.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific article focuses on the role of Orexin neurons, specifically those in the lateral hypothalamic area (LH) and paraventricular nucleus (PVN), in modulating opioid-induced respiratory depression.

Key findings include: 1. LH Orexin neurons project robustly to the preBötC, a key respiratory center, and their activation stimulates breathing and attenuates FIRD, an effect blocked by ORX antagonists. 2. PVN glutamatergic neurons have distinct projections to LC/peri-LC, RTN, and medullary raphe regions and stimulate breathing in room air but not in hypercapnia, suggesting these circuits are already activated by hypercapnia.

The paper highlights the importance of understanding the circuitry of Orexin signaling in modulating opioid effects and respiratory control networks. It references previous studies, such as Young et al., (2005) and Musa et al., (2024), to further elucidate the role of oxytocin (OX1R) pathways in breathing control.
