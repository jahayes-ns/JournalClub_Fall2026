Epidural Electrical Stimulation: A Review of Plasticity Mechanisms That Are Hypothesized to Underlie Enhanced Recovery From Spinal Cord Injury With Stimulation

Eisdorfer, Smit, Keefe, Lemay, Smith, Spence | 2020

Keywords: plasticity, electrical epidural stimulation, propriospinal detours, monosynaptic connections, internal motor copy, efferent motor copy, DREADDs, afferent stimulation

Final Summary

This scientific review, published in Frontiers in Molecular Neuroscience in September 2020, explores the use of Electrical Epidural Stimulation (EES) for enhancing recovery from spinal cord injuries (SCI), specifically focusing on its impact on voluntary mobility. The research question centers around understanding how EES improves muscle activity and coordination through potential mechanisms such as changes in local lumbar circuitry, propriospinal interneurons, and the internal motor model.

The study utilized a combination of theoretical analysis and existing research findings to propose that EES could aid in recovery by boosting muscle recruitment, reorganizing propriospinal circuitry, enhancing descending motor control pathways, modulating interneuronal networks, and spatio-temporal integration of the internal model with peripheral afferent input within interneuronal networks to aid learning of correct motor output.

Key findings suggest that EES may improve muscle activity and coordination for individuals with SCI, particularly in cases of incomplete lesions where the propriospinal network and descending reticulospinal command appear to play crucial roles, and in complete lesions where local lumbar circuit plasticity induced by afferent input might drive recovery. The study also highlights the importance of dI3 spinal interneurons in driving locomotion following transection and underscores the potential of chemogenetic technology for uncovering mechanisms of enhanced recovery after SCI with EES.

In conclusion, EES is proposed as an effective therapy for enhancing sensorimotor recovery following SCI. However, more research is needed to fully understand its mechanisms and optimize EES protocols. The authors emphasized the role of alpha-motoneurons as comparators between motor commands and outputs and explored the potential of genetic tools for afferent modulation in expediting our understanding of these plasticity mechanisms.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific review, published in Frontiers in Molecular Neuroscience in September 2020, focuses on the use of epidural electrical stimulation (EES) to enhance recovery from spinal cord injury (SCI). The authors discuss how EES improves voluntary mobility in SCI patients and propose several potential mechanisms driving these improvements, including changes in local lumbar circuitry, propriospinal interneurons, and the internal motor model. The review also mentions genetic tools for afferent modulation as a promising method to further investigate the mechanisms of action underlying EES-induced recovery. Keywords include plasticity, electrical epidural stimulation, propriospinal detours, monosynaptic connections, internal motor copy, efferent motor copy, and designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs).

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper presents two hypotheses on how Electrical Stimulation (EES) could aid individuals with spinal cord injuries in regaining control of their limbs.

  1. EES can boost muscle recruitment by stimulating Ia muscle spindle afferents, activating agonist muscles and inhibiting antagonist muscles, increasing overall force generated by affected muscles. It also induces growth-promoting factors that encourage axonal sprouting and new synapse formation between group Ia afferents and motoneurons or inhibitory interneurons, further enhancing muscle activity.
  2. EES can reorganize propriospinal circuitry around the lesion site and within the lumbar central pattern generator (CPG) to promote rhythmic activity and hindlimb coordination, facilitating tasks like balance and motor synergies by modulating the activities of propriospinal neurons (PNs).

The paper suggests that EES could improve muscle activity and coordination for individuals with spinal cord injuries, but more research is needed to fully understand its mechanisms and optimize EES protocols. The discussion also covers three hypotheses for how Electrical Stimulation of the Spinal Cord (ESC) or Electrically-Enabled Rehabilitation (EER) may aid in recovery from spinal cord injuries: enhanced descending motor control pathways, modulation of interneuronal networks, and spatial-temporal integration of the internal model with peripheral afferent input within interneuronal networks to aid learning of correct motor output.

The paper emphasizes the role of alpha-motoneurons as comparators between motor commands and outputs, suggesting that the cerebellum is not the only area in the central nervous system where forward models are expressed. Proprioceptive feedback contributes to intrinsic spinal cord circuitry, aiding in constructing an internal motor command during severed descending pathways.

The study by Takeoka (2020) highlights the importance of dI3 spinal interneurons in driving locomotion following transection, as their absence led to reduced locomotor activity during recovery compared to control mice. The paper also explores the potential of chemogenetic technology in uncovering mechanisms of enhanced recovery after Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) with Electrical Stimulation (EES), offering insights into molecular and circuit mechanisms of EES-induced recovery from SCI.

CONCLUSION

The conclusion of the scientific paper suggests that Electrically Enabled Stimulation (EES) could be an effective therapy for enhancing sensorimotor recovery following Spinal Cord Injury (SCI). While the exact mechanisms leading to this recovery are not fully understood, several plasticity mechanisms might be triggered by EES through activation of peripheral afferents. The recovery is thought to result from a synergistic interaction among local lumbar, propriospinal, and internal models. For incomplete lesions, the propriospinal network and descending reticulospinal command appear to play crucial roles, while recovery from complete lesions may be driven by local lumbar circuit plasticity induced by afferent input. The understanding of these mechanisms of plasticity could potentially be expedited using genetic tools for afferent modulation.
