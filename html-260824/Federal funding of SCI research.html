Funding Distributions, Trends, Gaps, and Policy Implications for Spinal Cord Injury Research: A Systematic Analysis of U.S. Federal Funds

Tucker, Gillespie, Buxton, Kondiles, Leal-Garcia, Pacheco, Tran, Vo, Abu, Barr, Barretto, Biundo, Duenwald, Evans, Friedman, Gadaleta, Ganesh, Gottschall, Liu, Malik, Nava, Sorani, Tandon, Thomas, Thomas, Barr, Burkhart, McCreedy, Nowell, Blackmon, Rabchevsky, Rodreick, Torres-Espín, Dulin | Not specified in the provided text

Final Summary

This scientific paper provides an analysis of U.S. federal funding distributions, trends, gaps, and policy implications for spinal cord injury (SCI) research between 2008-2023, focusing on the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Department of Defense (DoD) Congressionally Directed Medical Research Program (CDMRP) Spinal Cord Injury Research Program (SCIRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA).

The research question focuses on understanding funding patterns, priorities, and focus areas for each agency in the SCI research field. The methods involved data collection from publicly accessible databases and a three-axis classification system to categorize individual awards based on problem, approach/intervention, and readiness level of the study. Key findings reveal distinct roles for each agency: NIH supports basic and early-stage research, CDMRP focuses on translational and clinical readiness (particularly pain and bladder function), while VA research concentrates on rehabilitation, muscle and bone health, and implementation studies.

However, the study highlights critical funding gaps in areas such as bowel/gastrointestinal health, cardiovascular function, and mental health. Potential reductions in NIH budget may lead to a 50% decline in federal SCI research funding by 2026, posing a significant risk to the field's progress. To address these issues, strategies such as improving standardization and transparency in grant awards, dedicating funding streams for underrepresented high-impact secondary complications, and forming working groups focusing on critical losses in SCI research funding are proposed.

The study concludes by suggesting a centralized, living research dashboard to promote efficiency, collaboration, and progress towards improving quality of life for individuals with spinal cord injuries. The authors declare potential conflicts of interest and state that the study was funded by various organizations and previously published as a preprint on MedRxiv.org.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper provides a systematic analysis of U.S. federal funding distributions, trends, gaps, and policy implications for spinal cord injury (SCI) research. The study adheres to current standards for transparency and reproducibility in data-driven research and follows PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Data was collected from publicly accessible databases such as NIH RePORT, NIH RePORTER, the CDMRP/DTIC portal, and the VA Office of Research and Development.

The study categorizes federal SCI research awards and identifies trends, gaps, and policy implications. The classification schema was developed in consultation with federal and private program staff and SCI experts. Statistical analyses were conducted to identify significant findings. The study also provides comprehensive supplementary tables containing all award-level data and category assignments used in the analysis for reproducibility purposes.

The paper was a collaborative effort involving researchers from various institutions, including Texas A&M University, University of British Columbia, San Francisco State University, and others. The authors contributed to the concept development, data classification, award classification supervision, data analysis, transparency, rigor, and reproducibility statement, and manuscript preparation.

ABSTRACT

The study evaluates the distribution of federal funding for spinal cord injury (SCI) research in the US, focusing on the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Department of Defense (DoD) Congressionally Directed Medical Research Program (CDMRP) Spinal Cord Injury Research Program (SCIRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). The analysis of 1,589 awards from these agencies between 2008-2023 reveals that:

  1. NIH predominantly funds basic and early-stage translational research focused on SCI pathology, regeneration, and motor functional recovery.
  2. CDMRP funding is concentrated on applied and clinical research, particularly in the areas of pain, bladder function, and neuromodulatory device development.
  3. The VA primarily invests in rehabilitation-focused studies aimed at improving musculoskeletal and functional health outcomes.
  4. Critical funding gaps were identified for high-priority areas such as bowel/gastrointestinal health, cardiovascular function, and mental health.
  5. Potential reductions in NIH budget may lead to a 50% decline in federal SCI research funding by 2026, posing a significant risk to the field's progress.
  6. The study emphasizes the need for coordinated, data-driven funding strategies and proposes a "living dashboard" for enhancing transparency, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration, and guiding strategic investment in SCI research moving forward.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper highlights the significant impact of spinal cord injury (SCI) on approximately 300,000 individuals in the United States, with around 18,000 new cases annually. SCI leads to permanent motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction, causing a lifetime of health complications, loss of independence, and substantial barriers to full societal participation. The socioeconomic burden of SCI is high, totaling $7.74 billion in 1997 and likely higher today.

The National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Department of Defense (DoD) Congressionally Directed Medical Research Program (CDMRP) Spinal Cord Injury Research Program (SCIRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) are the primary funders of SCI research in the U.S., with distinct goals: NIH focuses on fundamental knowledge about living systems, CDMRP aims to improve health and well-being, and VA seeks to enhance veterans' health and well-being.

The paper addresses a critical knowledge gap by curating and analyzing 1,589 federally funded SCI research awards from 2008–2025 across these three agencies. The findings aim to provide insights into funding trends, agency priorities, and gaps in research that may impede scientific and clinical progress, and serve as a foundation for developing a centralized, publicly accessible SCI research funding dashboard to support a more efficient, collaborative research ecosystem.

METHODS

In this study, a team of researchers and individuals with lived SCI experience analyzed funding data for spinal cord injury (SCI) research from three primary US federal agencies: the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Department of Defense's Congressionally Directed Medical Research Programs (CDMRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). The methods involved curating NIH data using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. They downloaded NIH data from fiscal years 2008–2023, aligned it with more detailed data from NIH RePORTER, and resolved discrepancies to create a final dataset of 1,167 unique projects. For CDMRP and VA data, they obtained unique awards datasets through internal sources and the respective agency's websites.

The researchers developed a three-axis classification system to categorize individual awards based on the problem being addressed, approach/intervention used, and readiness level of the study. Each award was reviewed by at least three team members, and those not relevant to SCI were excluded from further analysis. Data analysis involved pairwise correlation analyses using Microsoft Excel and network analyses of combinatorial awards.

RESULTS

The study analyzes funding patterns, priorities, and focus areas of three major spinal cord injury (SCI) research funders: the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Congressionally Directed Medical Research Programs (CDMRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). Key findings include:

  1. The NIH is the largest funder, allocating $506 million over 7 years, followed by CDMRP and VA with approximately $130 million combined.
  2. The NIH focuses on mechanistic regeneration/repair studies, while the VA invests in secondary health conditions, and CDMRP focuses on bladder health and SCI-related pain.
  3. The NIH primarily funds in vivo approaches, VA focuses on cell/tissue therapies, and CDMRP invests more in noninvasive devices.
  4. CDMRP's funding priorities have varied over time.
  5. A small percentage of NIH awards involve combinations of multiple interventions (13.9%).

The study also reveals a structured pattern of SCI research award pairing, with an emphasis on cell/tissue therapies and device-based interventions combined with exercise/rehabilitation. Nine intervention pairs occur more frequently than expected under the independence model.

In terms of funding distribution across distinct readiness categories from 2017 to 2023, in vitro and animal studies received $617M, while clinical studies got $292M. CDMRP allocated a significant portion of its budget to Phase I/II clinical trials and clinical feasibility studies compared to NIH and VA.

The research points to specific areas where synergistic interventions are being prioritized in the federal funding landscape, highlighting a growing trend toward integrative approaches in SCI research.

DISCUSSION

This scientific paper analyzes 1,589 SCI research awards from NIH, CDMRP, and VA, revealing distinct roles for each agency: NIH supports basic and early-stage research, CDMRP focuses on translational and clinical readiness (particularly pain and bladder function), while VA research concentrates on rehabilitation, muscle and bone health, and implementation studies. From 2017 to 2923, the total federal investment in SCI research was $952 million, yet the economic burden significantly exceeds this due to high treatment costs and lost employment. Clinical trials for SCI treatments are expensive ($3.4M-$21.4M), challenging current funding levels to support late-stage clinical translation and commercialization.

Recent changes in federal funding priorities threaten the SCI research ecosystem, with the 2025 cessation of CDMRP SCIRP funding resulting in a loss of approximately $35 million annually. Proposed restructuring or elimination of the Administration for Community Living and a 40% reduction in NIH funding could lead to a total reduction of about 50% in federal SCI research support.

The paper highlights misalignment between federal priorities and the lived experiences of individuals with SCI, particularly regarding bowel/gastrointestinal health, autonomic dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, pressure sore injuries, mental wellness, which have received minimal funding despite their substantial impact on morbidity and mortality.

Strategies to address these issues include NIH's 2014 policy emphasizing sex as a biological variable, dedicated funding streams for underrepresented high-impact secondary complications, and the formation of working groups focusing on critical losses in SCI research funding.

Improved standardization and transparency in grant awards are suggested, with a proposal for a dynamic, publicly accessible platform to track and visualize federally funded SCI research. This platform would enhance transparency, standardize classification systems, and enable data-driven decision-making. However, the study has limitations such as not extending beyond 2025 or capturing funding from agencies outside of NIH, CDMRP, and VA. Future efforts should integrate private and state-level funding sources for a more comprehensive picture of the national SCI research ecosystem.

CONCLUSION

The study by Gillespie et al. concludes that while federal funding for Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) research aligns with agency missions, there are critical gaps in matching community priorities, supporting infrastructure, and advancing translational research. To address these issues, they suggest a centralized, living research dashboard to promote efficiency, collaboration, and progress towards improving quality of life for SCI individuals.

The authors also propose the formation of a working group to tackle the challenges arising from losses in SCI research funding. The study was funded by various organizations including the National Institutes of Health, the Craig H. Neilsen Foundation, and the Canadian Training Platform for Trials Leveraging Existing Networks, among others.

The authors declare potential conflicts of interest for some team members who review grants for certain funding bodies, but state that all other co-authors have no conflicts to disclose. The study was previously published as a preprint on MedRxiv.org.
