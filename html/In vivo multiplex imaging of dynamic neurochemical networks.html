Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The paper presents a new far-red dopamine sensor, HaloDA1.0, which combines a cpHaloTag-chemical dye approach with the G protein-coupled receptor activation-based (GRAB) strategy. This sensor offers high sensitivity for dopamine, sub-second response kinetics, and an extensive spectral range. When used in conjunction with existing green and red fluorescent neuromodulator sensors, calcium indicators, cAMP sensors, and optogenetic tools, HaloDA1.0 enables multiplex imaging in various settings, facilitating in-depth studies of dynamic neurochemical networks. The paper is a preprint and awaits peer review.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the crucial role neuromodulators, particularly dopamine (DA), play in shaping behavior and fine-tuning neural circuits via intracellular signaling mechanisms. DA is significant for reward, learning, and movement, with its interactions with other neuromodulators like acetylcholine (ACh), endocannabinoids (eCBs), and serotonin (5-HT) being integral to its multifaceted physiological role. The paper highlights that understanding DA's functions necessitates a comprehensive examination of its complex relationships within neurochemical networks, including other neuromodulators and intracellular signaling molecules with high spatial and temporal resolution.

To achieve this goal, the authors discuss the development of genetically encoded DA sensors based on the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) activation-based strategy, which can visualize DA dynamics in vivo with exceptional spatiotemporal resolution. However, these sensors are limited to the green and red spectrum, restricting their use for simultaneous tracking of multiple neurochemical signals.

As a solution, the paper proposes combining the dye-capture protein HaloTag with rhodamine derivatives as a promising alternative approach, offering a broad spectral range, high brightness, and photostability. The authors then describe developing a far-red DA sensor called GRABHaloDA1.0 (HaloDA1.0) using this chemigenetic strategy and performing three-color imaging with high spatiotemporal precision in various in vitro and in vivo applications, including cultured neurons, acute brain slices, and behaving animal models. The paper is available as a preprint, not yet certified by peer review, under the CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

RESULTS

The research introduces a new far-red sensor, HaloDA1.0, for detecting dopamine (DA) release in vivo. In mice, optogenetic stimulation of the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) increased SiR650-labeled HaloDA1.0 fluorescence, indicating DA release which was blocked by DAT and D1R antagonists.

Simultaneous recording of DA, acetylcholine (ACh), and cAMP dynamics was achieved in the Nucleus Accumbens (NAc) using three-color fiber photometry. Spontaneous fluctuations were observed, with a direct correlation between DA and cAMP, and inverse correlations between ACh and cAMP, and between DA and ACh. Cocaine disrupted these interactions. The study highlights HaloDA1.0's ability to measure the sub-second dynamics and interplay between these neurochemicals in vivo, providing a novel view of their dynamic interactions under different conditions.

DISCUSSION

The paper introduces HaloDA1.0, a far-red chemical DA sensor with improved properties for use in various models. Unlike genetically encoded DA sensors, HaloDA1.0 uses the chemical dye-cpHaloTag as its fluorescent module, relying on a shift in equilibrium between dye’s L and Z forms. The sensor exhibits excellent sensitivity, good membrane trafficking, high specificity for DA, rapid kinetics, and minimal downstream coupling.

In vivo applications of the cpHaloTag-based chemigenetic strategy were demonstrated using the dye SiR650, which provided the best performance in vivo. The study also presents a three-color recording system that simultaneously measures DA, ACh, and cAMP in D1-MSNs within the NAc during various behaviors, providing a more comprehensive view of how neuromodulators integrate to modulate synaptic plasticity.

The authors believe that this strategy will enable the development of a wide range of far-red neuromodulator sensors based on other GPCRs in the future, offering more options for researchers to simultaneously monitor multiple neurochemical signals and further increasing our understanding of dopaminergic systems under both physiological and pathological conditions.

METHODS

The scientific paper outlines two methods: one for the synthesis and characterization of SiR650-HTL and JF646-HTL chemical dyes, and another for neuroscience experiments using optogenetics, photometry, confocal microscopy, and immunohistochemistry.

  1. Synthesis and Characterization: The synthesized dyes are produced by reacting SiR-NHS Ester or JF646-NHS Ester with HaloTag(O2)amine in anhydrous DMF and DIPEA, then purified via reverse phase-HPLC. 1H NMR spectra and two-photon excitation spectra are measured to characterize the dyes.

  2. Neuroscience Experiments: This study involves injecting viruses expressing optogenetic tools and far-red dyes into specific brain regions of mice. Photometry recording with optogenetic stimulation measures changes in dopamine (DA), calcium (Ca2+), and ATP levels. Confocal microscopy is used for imaging purposes. The preliminary results suggest that optogenetics and advanced techniques can provide insights into brain function and potentially develop new treatments for neurological disorders.

The study employed dual-color optogenetic recordings in the Central Amygdaloid Nucleus (CeA) and Nucleus Accumbens (NAc) of D2R-Cre and D1R-Cre mice, respectively. Data analysis involves a custom MATLAB program, and statistical analysis is performed using ImageJ software and MATLAB programs.

For immunohistochemistry, mice are anesthetized, perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde, brains sectioned, stained with primary and secondary antibodies specific to the expressed proteins, and quantified using ImageJ software and MATLAB programs. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is calculated as the peak response divided by the standard deviation of the baseline fluorescence.

The paper presents a preprint that has not yet been peer-reviewed. The findings, while promising, should be considered preliminary until fully validated by the scientific community.
