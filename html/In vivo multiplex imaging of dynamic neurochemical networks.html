Final Summary

Title: Development of HaloDA1.0, a Novel Far-Red Dopamine Sensor for In-depth Studies of Dynamic Neurochemical Networks

This study introduces HaloDA1.0, a novel far-red dopamine sensor that addresses the challenge of detecting multiple neurochemicals simultaneously in vivo. The biosensor was developed by combining the cpHaloTag-chemical dye approach with the G protein-coupled receptor activation-based (GRAB) strategy, offering high sensitivity for dopamine, sub-second response kinetics, and an extensive spectral range.

Researchers used HaloDA1.0 in combination with existing green and red fluorescent neuromodulator sensors, Ca2+ indicators, cAMP sensors, and optogenetic tools to perform multi-color imaging in various neural systems, such as cultured neurons, acute brain slices, and behaving animal models.

Key findings include successful detection of dopamine release following Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) stimulation, compatibility for dual-color recordings during optogenetic stimulation in the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA), and simultaneous monitoring of dopamine, acetylcholine (ACh), and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) dynamics in the mouse Nucleus Accumbens (NAc).

The study provides a new perspective on the dynamic interplay between DA, ACh, and cAMP in D1-MSNs using HaloDA1.0 to measure sub-second dynamics and interactions between neurochemicals in vivo. The chemigenetic strategy offers potential for developing a wide range of far-red neuromodulator sensors based on other GPCRs, expanding options for researchers studying dynamic neurochemical networks.

Methods included synthesizing fluorescent dyes SiR650-HTL and JF646-HTL via chemical reactions, as well as experiments using transgenic animals, viral vectors, and various equipment such as confocal microscopes and photometry systems to investigate the dynamics of dopamine, calcium, and ATP in the brain. Data analysis was performed using MATLAB, with statistical significance determined using a two-tailed Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA.

PREAMBLE

The sensor combines a cpHaloTag-chemical dye approach with the G protein-coupled receptor activation-based (GRAB) strategy, offering high sensitivity for dopamine, sub-second response kinetics, and an extensive spectral range. The use of HaloDA1.0 in combination with existing green and red fluorescent neuromodulator sensors, Ca2+ indicators, cAMP sensors, and optogenetic tools provides versatility for multiplex imaging in various neural systems, facilitating in-depth studies of dynamic neurochemical networks. The study was published as a preprint on December 23, 2024.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the crucial role of neuromodulators, such as dopamine (DA), in shaping behavior by finely tuning neural circuits. DA plays significant roles in reward, learning, and movement, and interacts with other neuromodulators like acetylcholine (ACh), endocannabinoids (eCBs), and serotonin (5-HT). Achieving a comprehensive understanding of DA's functions necessitates examining its intricate interactions within neurochemical networks.

The authors highlight the need for tools that can monitor various neurochemical signals, particularly extending the spectral range of neuromodulator sensors to include far-red and near-infrared (NIR) wavelengths (>650 nm). This challenge arises due to the low brightness of existing far-red/NIR fluorescent proteins.

The paper then presents a novel approach using the dye-capture protein HaloTag combined with rhodamine derivatives, offering a promising alternative for developing far-red/NIR sensors with high brightness and photostability. The authors have developed a new DA sensor called GRABHaloDA1.0 (HaloDA1.0), which they used to perform three-color imaging in various in vitro and in vivo applications, including cultured neurons, acute brain slices, and behaving animal models.

RESULTS

The study introduces HaloDA1.0, a genetically encoded biosensor for detecting dopamine (DA) changes in living organisms, particularly useful for neurotransmission and neurological disorder studies. The biosensor, labeled with SiR650, showed the best results among three far-red dyes in terms of baseline fluorescence and signal-to-noise ratio.

In mice, optogenetic stimulation of the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) caused a moderate increase in HaloDA1.0 fluorescence, with effects enhanced by DAT blocker GBR but abolished by D1R antagonist SCH. The signals persisted for two days before degrading.

In sparsely innervated brain regions like the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), HaloDA1.0 detected DA release following VTA stimulation, while another sensor did not respond. The study also demonstrated compatibility for dual-color recordings during optogenetic stimulation in the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA).

Simultaneous monitoring of DA, acetylcholine (ACh), and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) dynamics in the mouse Nucleus Accumbens (NAc) revealed correlations between DA and cAMP, inverse correlations between ACh and cAMP, as well as between DA and ACh. Cocaine was found to disrupt these normal interactions. The research offers a new perspective on the dynamic interplay between DA, ACh, and cAMP in D1-MSNs using HaloDA1.0 to measure sub-second dynamics and interactions between neurochemicals in vivo.

DISCUSSION

The paper introduces HaloDA1.0, a new far-red chemigenetic DA sensor with distinct spectral properties, making it compatible for use with existing sensors to monitor other neuromodulators. HaloDA1.0 outperforms other sensors in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and rapid kinetics, and can be used for multi-color imaging in various models including cultured neurons, acute brain slices, zebrafish larvae, and mice. The sensor uses the chemical dye SiR650 for in vivo applications, demonstrating good performance. Simultaneous measurement of DA, ACh, and cAMP in D1-MSNs within the NAc provides a comprehensive view of how neuromodulators integrate to modulate synaptic plasticity. The chemigenetic strategy offers potential for developing a wide range of far-red neuromodulator sensors based on other GPCRs, expanding options for researchers to simultaneously monitor multiple neurochemical signals in both in vitro and in vivo applications.

METHODS

The scientific paper outlines two methods for synthesizing fluorescent dyes SiR650-HTL and JF646-HTL, likely used as labels in biological research. These are produced by reacting HaloTag(O2)amine with either SiR-NHS Ester or JF646-NHS Ester in anhydrous DMF, purified using reverse phase-HPLC, and characterized via 1H NMR spectroscopy and HRMS.

The paper also details experiments investigating the dynamics of dopamine (DA), calcium (Ca2+), and ATP in the brain. Transgenic animals and viral vectors express fluorescent proteins sensitive to these molecules in specific brain regions, using various equipment such as confocal microscopes, photometry systems, and stimulus isolators. The experiments involve electrical shock, PTZ administration, and optogenetic stimulation. Data analysis is performed using MATLAB, identifying cross-correlations between signals and peaks in response curves.

The study further uses dual-color optogenetic recording in the CeA and NAc of D2R-Cre and D1R-Cre mice, expressing different combinations of HaloDA1.0, jRGECO1a, rACh1h, GFlamp2, ChR2-mcherry or ChR2-EYFP. Mice are subjected to sucrose, foot shock, and cocaine experiments, with data analyzed using custom MATLAB programs. Statistical significance is determined using a two-tailed Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA, while immunohistochemistry confirms protein expression.

In summary, the paper presents methods for synthesizing fluorescent dyes and details experiments investigating the interactions between DA, Ca2+, and ATP in the brain under various conditions, utilizing a variety of techniques and equipment.
