Artificial hibernation reveals synaptic engram architecture associated with memory retention

Lin, Takahashi-Nakazato, Tsutsumi, Takahashi, Mercier, Ashitomi, Chiang, Haberl, Uytiepo, Maximov, Makino, Nemoto, Enoki, Hirano, Soga, Looprasertkul, Ohno, Kubota, Sakurai, Tanaka | 2026

Keywords: Artificial Hibernation, Neuronal Activity, Spines & Synapses, Neuronal Representation, Resilient Synaptic Architecture of Engram Cells

Final Summary

The research article published in Science on August 13, 2026, explores how memories can remain stable during artificial hibernation-induced extensive structural remodeling of neuronal networks. Using a mouse model, the study finds that memory-encoding neurons are organized into spatially clustered groups, with synaptic connections preferentially preserved during network reorganization. This distinctive architecture, characterized by clustered engram-engram synapses and multisynaptic boutons, is identified as a structural feature associated with memory retention, suggesting that memories may be stored through higher-order structural architecture within neuronal networks rather than solely on the strength of individual synapses.

Two studies show that controlled hypothermia (QIH) causes extensive remodeling in hippocampal dendrites but preserves memory and hippocampal representations, while quinolinic acid (QA)-induced QIH does not affect spatial memory or the structure of dendritic spines in mice. Additionally, clustered engram synapses were found to make synaptic contacts with multisynaptic boutons (MSBs) in QIH animals, indicating a specific presynaptic architecture for clustered engram synapses. Spine regrowth during the initial phase of QIH is minimal, and MSBs are unlikely to be newly formed as a consequence of network reorganization during QIH. The study also reveals that spine volumes of the clustered engram-engram synapses were smaller than other spines on the same engram dendrites from QIH animals.

PREAMBLE

The research article published in Science on August 13, 2026, investigates how memories can remain stable despite extensive structural remodeling of neuronal networks during artificial hibernation. Using a mouse model of artificial hibernation, the study reveals that synaptic connections among memory-encoding neurons are organized into spatially clustered groups and preferentially preserved during network remodeling. This distinctive architecture, characterized by clustered engram-engram synapses and multisynaptic boutons, is identified as a structural feature associated with memory retention. The findings suggest that memories may be stored through higher-order structural architecture embedded within neuronal networks rather than relying solely on the strength of individual synapses.

RESULTS

The combined results of the two studies show:

  1. Controlled Hypothermia (QIH) causes extensive structural remodeling in hippocampal dendrites, with significant elimination and regrowth of dendritic spines, but memory and hippocampal representations remain preserved.

  2. Quinolinic acid (QA) induced hypoxia-ischemia (QIH) does not affect spatial memory or the structure of dendritic spines in the hippocampal CA1 region in mice under these experimental conditions.

  3. In the study of engram synapses, 33.33% of eGRASP-positive clustered spines made synaptic contacts with multisynaptic boutons (MSBs) in QIH animals, suggesting a specific presynaptic architecture for clustered engram synapses and facilitation of coordination of population activity across neurons.

  4. Spine regrowth during the initial phase of QIH is minimal, and MSBs are unlikely to be newly formed as a consequence of network reorganization during QIH.

  5. The spine volumes of the clustered engram-engram synapses were smaller than other spines on the same engram dendrites from QIH animals, revealing a distinct neuronal architecture spared during massive spine elimination during QIH.

DISCUSSION

The combined discussion from the provided text includes two main sections: a neuroscience research study and a case study on climate change in the Amazon rainforest.

The neuroscience research investigates the impact of anesthesia on neural activity and synaptic density in mice. Anesthesia was found to decrease synaptic density, particularly for clustered engram synapses, and this effect was partially reversed by a quiet interval after hippocampus stimulation (QIH). The study also employed various methods such as optogenetics, calcium imaging, in vivo two-photon microscopy, and electron microscopy to analyze neural activity and synaptic changes.

The climate change case study focuses on the effects of climate change on biodiversity within the Amazon rainforest over a 19-year period (2006-2025). The analysis revealed a 1°C increase in average temperatures, an extension of the dry season, and a decrease in total annual rainfall by 5%. The findings show contrasting responses across different taxonomic groups, with mammals experiencing slight decreases in species richness and abundance, birds showing no significant changes, amphibians declining in species richness and abundance, and plants increasing in species richness.

The complex and varied responses of the Amazon rainforest's biodiversity to climate change highlight the need for conservation efforts targeting vulnerable species and habitats, as well as strategies that mitigate its impact on this critical ecosystem.
