Final Summary

Title: Automated Sleep-Disordered Breathing Event Detector for Improved Sleep Apnea Phenotyping

The study, led by Magnus Ruud Kjaer et al., introduces an expert-level probabilistic breathing event detector (ABED) to enhance sleep apnea phenotyping. ABED is a deep learning model trained on 5,456 polysomnographies and tested on 1,099 from six cohorts. It exhibits strong correlation with expert annotations for Apnea-Hypopnea Index (r² = 0.84) and achieves an F1 score of 0.78 across event types, outperforming previous detectors in identifying obstructive, central, hypopnea, and isolated respiratory events without arousals or desaturations (IRE). The model offers probabilities for each event type associated with each scored event, enabling apnea endotyping. ABED was developed by researchers from various institutions, using data from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, The Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study, Wisconsin Sleep Cohort, Cleveland Family Study, DREEM, and Alliance. The model shows potential benefits for increased diagnosis rates, alleviating economic burdens caused by undiagnosed sleep apnea, and offering a more consistent and objective method for scoring apnea events than human experts in large-scale global studies. However, its robustness to low signal quality is limited for EEG (Electroencephalography) and thoracic modalities. The data and code are available under restricted access and at https://github.com/RuudeResearch/ABED respectively.

Authors: Magnus Ruud Kjaer, Umaer Hanif, Andreas Brink-Kjaer, Mads Olsen, Oliver Sum-Ping, Oscar Carrillo, Scott A. Sands, Susan Redline, Katie L. Stone, Poul Jennum, and Emmanuel Mignot

ABSTRACT

The paper develops an automatic sleep-disordered breathing event detector using deep learning. The model is trained on 5,456 polysomnographies and tested on 1,099 from six cohorts. It shows a strong correlation with expert annotations for apnea-hypopnea index (r² = 0.84) and achieves an F1 score of 0.78 across event types. The model performs comparably or better than individual expert raters and provides a probabilistic output, called "apnotyping," which offers insights into sleep disordered breathing etiology and may enhance diagnostic accuracy, leading to improved patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The paper discusses sleep-related respiratory diseases, specifically central sleep apnea (CSA) and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which are the most common types under the umbrella term of sleep disordered breathing (SDB). The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) is the primary metric used to measure SDB severity, with apneas defined as complete cessations of breathing lasting at least 10 seconds. Scoring events is complex and subjective, leading to discrepancies in OSA prevalence across studies due to varying definitions of hypopneas and scoring inconsistencies. The paper suggests the need for revamping SDB event definitions, differentiating central versus obstructive events and distinguishing events with and without desaturations and/or arousals. The study presents an end-to-end deep learning model (ABED) to detect various types of SDB events in full night polysomnography (PSGs), aiming to improve reliability, reduce costs, and facilitate larger-scale investigation of sleep apnea.

RESULTS

The paper introduces ABED (Automated Bedside Evaluator of Sleep Apnea), a machine learning model that outperforms previous detectors in identifying obstructive, central, hypopnea, and a new event type: isolated respiratory events without arousals or desaturations (IRE). ABED provides probabilities for each event type associated with each event it scores, enabling apnea endotyping.

The model achieved an F1 score of 0.78 for event detection and significantly improved mean F1 scores compared to previous highest reported scores, though it ranked second in determining apnea severity and had a slightly inferior R2 for AHI correlation. ABED performed well on two unseen datasets, indicating good generalizability.

The model accurately predicted key physiological characteristics such as loop gain, arousal threshold, and pharyngeal muscle compensation. However, it has limitations like not being applicable to children and having its lowest diagnostic accuracy in the none-to-mild apnea range. Future work should focus on validating endotyping approaches across multiple nights, comparing them to gold-standard measurements, and investigating variability in arousal intensity.

ABED's potential benefits include increased diagnosis rates and alleviating economic burdens caused by undiagnosed sleep apnea, as well as offering a more consistent and objective method for scoring apnea events than human experts in large-scale global studies. However, its robustness to low signal quality is limited for EEG (Electroencephalography) and thoracic modalities.

METHODS

The study used data from six cohorts (Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, The Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study, Wisconsin Sleep Cohort, Cleveland Family Study, DREEM, and Alliance) to train and evaluate ABED, a deep learning model for detecting sleep-disordered breathing events (OA, CA, HYPO, and IRE). Seven modalities were used as input: Nasal Flow, Naso-oral Thermistor, Abdominal Plethysmography, Thoracic Plethysmography, Blood/Oxygen Saturation, Arousal Probability, and Wake Probability. ABED was trained on 5,456 PSGs across four cohorts, with 546 PSGs for validation and 1,099 for testing. The model employs classification and localization loss functions and uses Adam optimization. Performance was assessed through precision, recall, F1-score, and IOU metrics. An endotype-apnotyping analysis was also performed using ABED to examine the probability of each event type within predicted events and compare it with baseline demographics and manual annotations when used to predict sleep apnea endotypes such as loop gain, arousal threshold, and pharyngeal muscle compensation. The data is available under restricted access due to ethical and legal constraints, while the code is available at https://github.com/RuudeResearch/ABED.
