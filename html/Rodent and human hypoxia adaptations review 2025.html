Final Summary

The review article titled "Physiological Differences Underlying Divergent Hypoxia Responses and Altitude Adaptations in Humans, Rats, and Mice," published in Comprehensive Physiology in 2025, explores the unique physiological responses of humans, rats, and mice to hypoxia and their adaptations to high altitudes. The research highlights key factors such as erythropoietin, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), intermittent hypoxia conditioning, red blood cells, and species differences.

Findings indicate that humans maintain their metabolic rate during hypoxia by increasing ventilation, oxygen transport, and anaerobic metabolism, whereas rodents lower their metabolic rate in initial stages of hypoxic adaptation through hypothermia and reduced heart rate. Mice demonstrate exceptional high-altitude tolerance due to factors like higher lung volume, efficient pulmonary gas exchange, hypoxic hypometabolism, and greater mitochondrial electron-transport plasticity. In contrast, rats are more vulnerable to hypoxia, with high-altitude myocardial injury not observed in healthy humans or mice.

The study underscores the significance of comparative physiology and molecular biology studies to ensure the validity of animal models, particularly for hypoxia research. Recommendations include developing valid in vitro systems and cross-species panels of biomarkers to enable back-translation. To address the challenges posed by species differences, the study suggests explicit "translational fit" statements in rodent studies, considering dosing windows, and prioritizing human cohorts for clinical applications of hypoxia interventions.
