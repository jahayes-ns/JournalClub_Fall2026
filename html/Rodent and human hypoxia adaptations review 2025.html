Final Summary

Title: Physiological Differences Underlying Hypoxia Responses and Altitude Adaptations in Humans, Rats, and Mice

Authors: Johannes Burtscher et al. (2025)

This review paper examines the physiological differences driving hypoxia responses and altitude adaptations across humans, rats, and mice. The authors find that humans increase ventilation, oxygen transport, and anaerobic metabolism during hypoxia, while rodents lower their metabolic rate initially. Rodents demonstrate higher-altitude tolerance due to factors such as larger lung volume, efficient pulmonary gas exchange, and hypoxic hypometabolism. However, rats are more susceptible to hypoxia than mice or humans, experiencing myocardial injury, excessive erythropoiesis, and significant pulmonary vascular remodeling in response to hypoxia.

The authors propose combining rodent studies with large animal models, focusing on comparative physiology and molecular biology, developing valid in vitro systems for systemically relevant hypoxia responses, using biomarkers for back-translation, and considering dosing windows of intermittent/continuous hypoxia protocols. They emphasize that no single animal model is ideal for pre-clinical hypoxia research due to the species' distinct adaptations.

PREAMBLE

Title: Physiological Differences Underlying Divergent Hypoxia Responses and Altitude Adaptations in Humans, Rats, and Mice

Authors: Johannes Burtscher, Robert T. Mallet, Anupam Sah, Max Gassmann, Martin Burtscher, Rodrigo Iturriaga

Publisher: Comprehensive Physiology

Year: 2025

This review paper explores the physiological differences underpinning varying hypoxia responses and altitude adaptations in humans, rats, and mice. The authors discuss key factors such as erythropoietin production, Hypoxia Inducible Factor (HIF) regulation, red blood cell adjustments, and intermittent hypoxia conditioning. The research highlights species-specific adaptations to chronic continuous and intermittent hypoxia exposure, offering insights into the diverse mechanisms driving altitude acclimation across these three mammalian models.

ABSTRACT

The abstract discusses differences in hypoxia responses between humans, rats, and mice. Humans respond to hypoxia by increasing ventilation, oxygen transport, and anaerobic metabolism, while rodents lower their metabolic rate during the initial stages of hypoxia. Rodents exhibit higher-altitude tolerance due to factors like greater lung volume, efficient pulmonary gas exchange, and hypoxic hypometabolism. However, rats are more vulnerable to hypoxia than mice or humans, showing myocardial injury, excessive erythropoiesis, and pronounced pulmonary vascular remodeling in response to hypoxia. The authors recommend combining rodent studies with large animal models, adding translational fit statements, considering dosing windows of intermittent/continuous hypoxia protocols, focusing on comparative physiology and molecular biology, developing valid in vitro systems for systemically relevant hypoxia responses, and using biomarkers to enable back-translation. No single animal model is ideal for pre-clinical hypoxia research.
