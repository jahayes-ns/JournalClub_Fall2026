Final Summary

The paper titled "Physiological Differences Underlying Hypoxia Responses and Altitude Adaptations in Humans, Rats, and Mice" published in Comprehensive Physiology (2025) aims to explore physiological differences among humans, rats, and mice regarding their responses to hypoxia and altitude adaptations. The research question centers around understanding species-specific differences and similarities in response to chronic continuous and intermittent hypoxia.

The study reviews findings from various research studies on each species, highlighting unique physiological responses. Key findings include differences in ventilation, cardiovascular functions, chemosensing mechanisms, exercise tolerance, immune response, inflammation, redox programs, and high-altitude tolerance among these species. For instance, mice demonstrate a more pronounced initial ventilation response compared to humans and rats.

In terms of cardiovascular functions, mice exhibit little increase in blood pressure and reduced heart rate during hypoxia, while humans experience sympathetic nervous system activation leading to systemic hypertension. Rodents also lower their metabolic rate during exercise and hypoxia, contrasting with humans who increase oxygen transport and anaerobic metabolism. Hematological parameters and oxygen affinity of hemoglobin also vary between species.

Given the significant differences in physiological responses among these species, the authors caution against extrapolating the results of rodent studies on hypoxia interventions to humans. They propose a combination of studies in mice, rats, and large animal models for more comprehensive information on mechanisms, safety, and practicality of potential treatments. Additionally, they suggest adding "translational fit" statements to rodent studies, considering dosing windows for intermittent/continuous hypoxia protocols, developing valid in vitro systems, and prioritizing human cohorts for research on clinical applications of hypoxia interventions.

In conclusion, this comprehensive review emphasizes the need for caution when applying results from rodent studies on hypoxia interventions to humans due to divergent responses between species.

ABSTRACT

The abstract discusses differences in physiological responses to acute hypoxia among humans, rats, and mice. Mice show a more pronounced initial ventilation response compared to humans and rats. In terms of systemic responses, humans experience sympathetic nervous system activation leading to systemic hypertension during hypoxia, whereas mice exhibit little increase in blood pressure and reduced heart rate.

Cardiovascular functions also vary between species due to allometric scaling and quantitative differences. Rodents lower their metabolic rate during exercise and hypoxia, while humans increase oxygen transport and anaerobic metabolism. Hematological parameters and oxygen affinity of hemoglobin differ as well, with mice and rats having lower oxygen affinities compared to humans.

Significant differences exist in chemosensing mechanisms, exercise in hypoxia capacity, immune, inflammatory, and redox programs, and high-altitude tolerance among these species. Mice exhibit exceptional high-altitude tolerance, while rats are particularly vulnerable to hypoxia.

The study emphasizes that no ideal animal model exists for pre-clinical hypoxia research, suggesting a combination of studies in mice, rats, and large animal models for additional information on mechanisms, safety, and practicality of potential treatments. The authors recommend adding "translational fit" statements to rodent studies, considering dosing windows for intermittent/continuous hypoxia protocols, developing valid in vitro systems, and prioritizing human cohorts for research on clinical applications of hypoxia interventions.

The abstract concludes that extrapolation of the results of rodent studies on hypoxia interventions to humans requires caution due to divergent responses between species. No conflicts of interest were declared, and no funding was reported by the authors.
