Kumar, Rhone, Kovach, Mowla, Chan, Garcia, Kawasaki, Mueller, Kuhn, Frede, Ciliberto, Czech, Ganganna, Owens, Dabrowski, Sprigg, Howard, Hwang, Herrero, Pattinson, Richerson, Wemmie, Dlouhy | 2021

Final Summary

The study "A motor thalamic site in humans that suppresses involuntary breathing without awareness" investigates the role of the human thalamus in modulating breathing, focusing on the ventral anterior/ventral lateral anterior nuclei (VLa/VA). Through intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) recordings from 11 pediatric and adult patients with intractable epilepsy, the team found that stimulation of these specific thalamic regions could induce apnea without any sensory effects or motor impairments. The findings suggest a previously unrecognized thalamic function in controlling breathing and potentially contribute to understanding central apnea disorders like sleep apnea, SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy), and SIDS. This research expands our knowledge of brain circuitry responsible for respiratory regulation.

ABSTRACT

This study identifies a specific region in the human motor thalamus where electrical stimulation can suppress involuntary breathing and induce apnea without awareness. The effect was found to localize to the ventral anterior/ventral lateral anterior nuclei through intracranial recordings in both pediatric and adult patients. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized thalamic function in the forebrain control of breathing, suggesting that this circuit may coordinate breathing with volitional behaviors such as speech, and potentially play a role in central apnea disorders like sleep apnea, SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy), and SIDS (Sudden Infant Death Syndrome).

METHODS

The study involved eleven patients with medically refractory epilepsy, aged between 22 months and 27 years. Participants underwent intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) monitoring for seizure focus localization at the University of Iowa. Electrodes were implanted based on recommendations from the multidisciplinary epilepsy team, with some participants having at least one electrode targeting the thalamus.

During the 2-week monitoring period, bipolar electrical stimulation was delivered to adjacent electrode contact pairs via a constant current stimulator. The stimulation protocol involved increasing current from 1 mA in increments until a physiological or behavioral effect was observed or stimulation was terminated due to afterdischarges or other iEEG changes. Stimulation intensity varied across brain regions, with median values being similar across brain regions but lower in the thalamus compared to the amygdala and other sites excluding thalamus and amygdala.

Participants were resting quietly during stimulation and remained blinded to its delivery. Breathing was measured using piezoelectric belts, chest sensors, nasal pressure transducers, and oral thermistors. Apnea was defined as at least one missed breath with a flattened respiratory trace verified by absence of chest wall movement measurement or video.

Machine learning analysis using support vector machines (SVM) was employed to classify and predict thalamic sites that suppress breathing and induce apnea. The SVM was trained with MNI coordinates of 147 stimulation sites and their respective respiratory outcomes, and posterior probabilities for each of the two breathing outcomes were computed. Electrode localization and thalamus parcellation were also performed using high-resolution MRI scans and a probabilistic thalamic atlas.

RESULTS

In a study investigating the role of the thalamus in modulating breathing, electrical stimulation was applied to the thalami of 11 adult and pediatric participants. Stimulation of the thalamus induced apnea in all participants, with no effect observed when stimulating the posterior thalamus. The right or left thalamus could induce apnea, indicating that inhibition of breathing is not lateralized to one hemisphere. Participants did not experience sensory effects or motor impairments during the stimulation-induced apnea and remained unaware that their breathing had ceased. Thalamic stimulation was found to be the only forebrain structure consistently capable of inducing apnea, with the ventral anterior and ventral lateral (anterior) nuclei being the most sensitive locations for eliciting apnea. A machine learning algorithm also predicted a localized apneic site within the VLa/VA nuclei of the thalamus.
