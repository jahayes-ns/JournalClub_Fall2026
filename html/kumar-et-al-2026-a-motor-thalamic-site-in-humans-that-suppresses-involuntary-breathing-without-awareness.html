Final Summary

Title: A Focal Site in the Human Motor Thalamus Inhibiting Automatic Breathing

In this collaborative study led by Sukhbinder Kumar and multiple institutions, researchers aimed to investigate a previously unidentified neural circuit involved in controlling breathing by exploring the effects of thalamic stimulation on breathing during intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) for epilepsy monitoring in 11 patients.

Through bipolar electrical stimulation, the team identified that stimulating specific regions within the ventral lateral anterior (VLA) and ventral anterior (VA) nuclei of the human motor thalamus could induce central apnea without sensory or motor effects, awareness of breathing cessation, or impact on volitional breathing or speech. Stimulation of other forebrain regions did not affect breathing.

The study provides crucial insights into the neural mechanisms underlying respiratory control and offers potential implications for understanding and treating various respiratory disorders such as sleep apnea, SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy), and SIDS (Sudden Infant Death Syndrome). Despite some limitations, the consistency of stimulation-induced apnea across a heterogeneous cohort suggests that this thalamic site represents a generalizable forebrain node in human respiratory control.

PREAMBLE

Title: A Motor Thalamic Site in Humans that Inhibits Involuntary Breathing Unconsciously

Authors: Sukhbinder Kumar et al. (multiple institutions)

This research article investigates a motor thalamic site in humans capable of suppressing involuntary breathing without conscious awareness. The study involved a collaborative effort from various departments at the University of Iowa, University of Nebraska Medical Center, Veterans Affairs Medical Center, Zucker School of Medicine at Hofstra Northwell, and the University of Oxford.

The team identified a region in the human motor thalamus that, when stimulated during surgery, briefly inhibited involuntary breathing. This effect occurred without any conscious awareness or respiratory effort by the patient. The findings suggest the existence of a previously unidentified neural circuit involved in the control of breathing.

The researchers used intracranial electromyography (IEMG) recordings and video analysis to monitor diaphragm activity during stimulation. They found that stimulating the identified region caused a significant reduction in diaphragmatic activity, indicating inhibition of involuntary breathing.

This study provides important insights into the neural mechanisms underlying respiratory control, offering potential implications for understanding and treating various respiratory disorders.

ABSTRACT

The study investigates the role of the human thalamus in respiratory control by examining the effects of thalamic stimulation on breathing during intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) for epilepsy monitoring in 11 patients. Results show that thalamic stimulation induced central apnea in all participants, without sensory or motor effects and without awareness of breathing cessation. The effect was most consistent with stimulation of the ventral lateral anterior (VLa) and ventral anterior (VA) nuclei in the anterior motor thalamus. Volitional breathing and speech were preserved. In contrast, stimulation of other forebrain regions did not affect breathing. This research identifies a focal site in the human motor thalamus where electrical stimulation suppresses involuntary, automatic breathing and induces apnea without awareness. These findings expand our understanding of thalamic function and suggest a role for this circuit in central apnea disorders such as sleep apnea, SUDEP, and SIDS.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper highlights the role of brainstem respiratory networks in controlling breathing, but suggests that forebrain structures may exert modulatory influences. Particularly, stimulation of the amygdala can inhibit breathing and evoke apnea. However, the contribution of other forebrain regions, including the thalamus, to this hierarchy of control is largely unexplored. The paper aims to investigate whether thalamic activity alters breathing, if there are differences between right and left thalamus, in children versus adults, and if specific regions of the thalamus mediate these effects. Due to limited technology at the time, a previous study from 1967 did not delineate nucleus-specific effects on breathing. This current study prospectively records from and functionally maps forebrain sites in 11 pediatric and adult patients with intractable epilepsy undergoing intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), providing direct access to thalamic nuclei for investigation.

METHODS

In this study, 11 participants with medically intractable epilepsy were monitored for seizure focus localization at the University of Iowa using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG). Electrodes were implanted based on recommendations from a multidisciplinary epilepsy team. The study focused on participants who underwent direct electrical stimulation and had at least one electrode targeting the thalamus.

Intracranial recordings, respiratory signals, and electrical stimulation were acquired using a NeuraLynx Atlas Neurophysiology System. Bipolar, biphasic electrical stimulation trains (50 Hz, 200 ls pulse width) were delivered to adjacent electrode contact pairs via a constant current stimulator. The stimulation intensity varied across trials but had median values similar across brain regions. The stimulation protocol was designed to find the physiological or behavioral effect or the upper stimulation limit (5.5 mA).

Stimulation duration and interval between trials were adjusted based on the region and proximity to the seizure focus. Participants were resting quietly during procedures, with their parents/guardians blinded to the delivery of electrical stimulation. Trials that induced seizures or involuntary movements were excluded from respiratory analysis. Breathing was measured using piezoelectric belts or chest sensors, and central apnea was defined as at least one missed breath verified by absence of chest wall movement measurement.

The study also involved electrode localization and thalamus parcellation using high-resolution T1-weighted MRI scans, nonlinear warping of a probabilistic thalamic atlas, and support vector machine (SVM) analysis for classifying and predicting thalamic sites that suppress breathing and induce apnea.

RESULTS

In a study, electrical stimulation of the thalamus was found to induce apnea in all adult and pediatric participants. Stimulation of the anterior contacts in the ventrolateral anterior (VLA) nucleus of the thalamus caused apnea, while stimulation of the posterior thalamus had no effect on breathing. The right or left thalamus was found to induce apnea, indicating inhibition of breathing is not lateralized to one hemisphere. Participants were unaware that their breathing had ceased during stimulation, suggesting the thalamus may inhibit not only breathing but also interoceptive signals and threat perception associated with impaired ventilation. The thalamus was found to be the only forebrain structure that consistently induced apnea, with the VLA/VA nuclei being particularly sensitive to stimulation. A machine learning algorithm predicted a localized apneic site within the VLA/VA nuclei of the thalamus.

DISCUSSION

This text appears to be a scientific article about the discovery of a focal apneic region within the ventral lateral anterior (VLA) and ventral anterior (VA) nuclei of the human thalamus, which has been identified as a critical and active node in a forebrain-brainstem respiratory control hierarchy. The study was conducted on individuals with epilepsy undergoing invasive monitoring, and the results demonstrate that activating this circuit can silence involuntary automatic breathing while leaving volitional capacity and motor pathways intact.

The text mentions several limitations of the study, including the fact that not all thalamic nuclei were sampled, and the generalization to nonepilepsy populations requires caution. However, the consistency of stimulation-induced apnea across a heterogeneous cohort with diverse epilepsy syndromes and seizure foci suggests that this thalamic site represents a generalizable forebrain node in human respiratory control.
