Final Summary

This review article published in Clinical Science (2026) explores the role of circadian gene expression in the impact of Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), specifically intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation, on blood pressure regulation. The research question focuses on understanding the complex interaction between salt sensitivity, genetic variations in circadian rhythm genes, and systemic hypertension, particularly in relation to OSA.

The authors used a literature review approach to discuss this topic. They highlight that disruption of circadian rhythms by OSA can worsen hypertension through mechanisms involving hypoxia-induced gene regulation, mainly via HIF-1α. The key findings suggest that while conventional therapies like CPAP and upper airway tissue reconstructive surgery are effective for OSA treatment, novel therapies targeting circadian clock genes dysregulated by OSA could potentially improve cardiovascular health, especially in populations with disrupted sleep patterns or heightened salt sensitivity.

The authors propose that controlled moderate intermittent hypoxia might have beneficial effects on the heart and vasculatures through modulation of PER2, HIF-1α, and BMAL1 expression. However, more research is needed to fully understand these potential benefits and develop effective novel therapies. The conclusions emphasize the need for personalized treatment approaches for salt-sensitive hypertension and further studies on circadian clock genes as potential therapeutic targets in OSA patients.

PREAMBLE

This review article published in Clinical Science (2026) focuses on the role of circadian gene expression in the impact of intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation on blood pressure, particularly in relation to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The authors highlight that circadian clock genes like BMAL1, CLOCK, PER1/2/3, and CRY1/2 can regulate cardiovascular and renal functions, potentially through salt sensitivity and blood pressure regulation.

The review discusses the complex interaction between salt sensitivity, genetic variations in circadian rhythm genes, and systemic hypertension. It emphasizes that OSA, with its two key components—intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation—has a significant effect on the pathophysiologic processes of hypertension by disrupting circadian rhythms.

The authors suggest that this disruption may worsen hypertension through mechanisms involving hypoxia-induced gene regulation, mainly via hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α). They also propose that controlled moderate intermittent hypoxia might have beneficial effects on the heart and vasculatures, possibly through modulation of PER2, HIF-1α, and BMAL1 expression.

In addition to conventional therapies for OSA treatment like continuous positive airway pressure and upper airway tissue reconstructive surgery, the authors propose that novel therapies targeting circadian clock genes dysregulated by OSA could be developed to improve cardiovascular health, especially in populations with disrupted sleep patterns or heightened salt sensitivity.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the scientific paper discusses the relationship between Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), sleep fragmentation, circadian rhythm genes, and blood pressure regulation. OSA is a disorder characterized by repeated episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to fragmented sleep, hypoxia, and reoxygenation. These events can impact oxygen metabolism through Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1 (HIF-1), a heterodimeric complex that includes circadian clock genes like BMAL1 and CLOCK.

Disruption of circadian rhythm is common in OSA patients, affecting blood pressure regulation by impacting circadian amplitude, phase timing, salt sensitivity, renal sodium handling, aldosterone signaling, and autonomic balance. This disruption can manifest as clinically relevant phenotypes such as non-dipping or nocturnal hypertension.

The paper also discusses the use of Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) for OSA treatment, which has been shown to reduce blood pressure, inflammatory markers, and nocturnal BP patterns in patients with resistant hypertension. Intermittent Hypoxia Conditioning (IHC), involving controlled exposures to mild hypoxic episodes followed by periods of normoxia or hyperoxia, can potentially positively impact cardiovascular health by modulating HIF-1α. A 2022 study showed that 15 days of IHC reduced systolic blood pressure at rest during wakefulness over 24 hours.

Sleep fragmentation (SF), independent of intermittent hypoxia, contributes significantly to the pathophysiology of OSA by disrupting circadian rhythm and contributing to ongoing symptoms even with adequate treatment. The paper aims to highlight the complex interaction between salt sensitivity, genetic alterations in circadian rhythm genes, and systemic hypertension, and potential for personalized treatment approaches for salt-sensitive hypertension and novel therapies targeting circadian clock genes dysregulated by OSA to improve cardiovascular health.
