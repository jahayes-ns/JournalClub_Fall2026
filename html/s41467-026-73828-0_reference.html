Final Summary

The research titled "Human Forebrain Neural Synchronization and Entrainment to Breathing During Wakefulness, Sleep, and External Mechanical Ventilation" investigates the relationship between human brain activity and respiration in various states. The study monitored brain activity using intracranial electrodes placed within the frontal lobes of 19 participants who were either awake, sleeping, or under external mechanical ventilation.

Key findings reveal a strong correlation between brain activity and respiration across all three states, suggesting that the forebrain is capable of entraining to breathing rhythms. Specific areas within the insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus synchronize with breathing rhythms during wakefulness. During sleep, this synchronization persists in the amygdala and hippocampus, potentially supporting vigilance and memory consolidation.

Contrary to rodents, nasal airflow was not essential for this synchronization, suggesting multiple afferent pathways in respiratory interoception and possible unique evolutionary changes in humans. When breathing was controlled by an external mechanical ventilator, the imposed rhythm directly entrained forebrain activity, indicating a causal link. Notably, slow, deep breathing entrained more forebrain sites, potentially explaining how breath-based practices may influence emotion and cognition.

The findings could provide new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying sleep apnea, epilepsy, and other neurological disorders associated with abnormal breathing patterns or seizures. The study was published in Nature Communications.

PREAMBLE

Title: Human Forebrain Neural Synchronization and Entrainment to Breathing During Wakefulness, Sleep, and External Mechanical Ventilation

The study "Human forebrain neural synchronization and entrainment to breathing during wakefulness, sleep, and external mechanical ventilation" investigates the relationship between human brain activity and respiration in various states. The research was conducted by a multi-disciplinary team of doctors and scientists from various institutions including the University of Iowa, Massachusetts Eye and Ear, Harvard Medical School, and the University of Florida.

The study monitored brain activity using intracranial electrodes placed within the frontal lobes (forebrain) of 19 participants who were either awake, sleeping, or under external mechanical ventilation. The results reveal a strong correlation between brain activity and respiration across all three states, suggesting that the forebrain is capable of entraining to breathing rhythms.

The findings could provide new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying sleep apnea, epilepsy, and other neurological disorders associated with abnormal breathing patterns or seizures. The study was published in Nature Communications.

ABSTRACT

This scientific paper investigates breathing interoception, the brain's ability to track and integrate respiratory signals, which is crucial for survival. Using human intracranial recordings, the study found that during wakefulness, specific areas within the insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus synchronize with breathing rhythms. During sleep, this synchronization persists in the amygdala and hippocampus, potentially supporting vigilance and memory consolidation.

Contrary to rodents, nasal airflow was not essential for this synchronization, suggesting multiple afferent pathways in respiratory interoception and possible unique evolutionary changes in humans. When breathing was controlled by an external mechanical ventilator, the imposed rhythm directly entrained forebrain activity, indicating a causal link. Notably, slow, deep breathing entrained more forebrain sites, potentially explaining how breath-based practices may influence emotion and cognition. The findings redefine breathing interoception as a pervasive influence within the forebrain, with implications for understanding disorders of respiratory awareness, emotional regulation, and cognitive health.

INTRODUCTION

The paper focuses on the interoceptive process of breathing, essential for life and recently linked to emotional regulation and cognitive performance. While much is known about its function during wakefulness, little is understood about its operation during sleep. The study aims to investigate how the forebrain senses, monitors, and responds to breathing during sleep using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) on nine participants with intractable epilepsy.

The research addresses four key questions: whether all forebrain sites synchronize with breathing, if this synchrony varies with sleep, if nasal breathing is necessary for the synchronization, and if the synchronization reflects direct entrainment of the breathing rhythm. The study uses various conditions, including different states of wakefulness and airflow delivery methods, to determine these factors by analyzing coherence between the breathing rhythm and forebrain oscillatory activity. This research aims to increase understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying breathing interoception in humans, particularly during sleep, with implications for sleep-related breathing disorders and sudden death syndromes.

RESULTS

The study explores the synchronization of brain activity with different breathing conditions: automatic, external mechanical ventilation, and slow, deep breathing.

  1. In automatic breathing, brain regions like sensorimotor cortex and insula synchronize with breathing rhythm without nasal airflow.
  2. During external mechanical ventilation, the imposed breathing rhythm directly entrains forebrain oscillations.
  3. Slow, deep breathing increases the number of brain sites entrained to breathing, suggesting adaptability to different frequencies and tidal volumes.
  4. The amyggdala and hippocampus exhibit high coherence with the imposed breathing rhythm during external mechanical ventilation, particularly during slow, deep ventilation at 0.08 Hz compared to standard ventilation (amygdala: 0.69 vs 0.20; hippocampus: 0.68 vs 0.20).

These findings suggest that certain brain regions become recruited and synchronized during slow, deep breathing, and forebrain synchronization adapts flexibly to the frequency of breathing. The study does not provide conclusions or implications but presents the observed findings.

DISCUSSION

The study examines nine participants with intracranial recordings for seizure focus localization, focusing on breathing interoception. Key findings include:

  1. Identification of distinct sites within known interoceptive brain regions that synchronize with the breathing rhythm during wakefulness, such as the posterior dorsal insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and areas outside classic interoceptive regions involved in cognition, emotion, memory, and motor function.
  2. Breathing-related synchronization varied significantly within regions, suggesting localized neuronal integration rather than artifactual effects like electrode movement or physiological pulsations.
  3. Sleep reduced synchronization in the cortex, while the hippocampus and amygdala exhibited persistent synchronization with breathing rhythms during sleep, potentially indicating a redistribution of interoceptive processing toward subcortical structures.
  4. The study found that airflow through the nasal passages is not required for synchronization in humans, suggesting multiple respiratory afferent pathways are critical for this process.
  5. Enhanced synchronization during slow, deep ventilator-driven breathing suggests adaptability of forebrain circuits to varying respiratory inputs.
  6. The findings hold implications for disorders linked to disrupted interoceptive signaling, including anxiety, panic disorders, chronic pain, and cognitive impairments.
  7. Limitations include a small sample size, restricted electrode placement, potential artifacts from electrode movement or physiological factors, and the use of propofol anesthesia during certain procedures.

METHODS

The study investigates neural-respiratory interactions in the human brain during anesthetized conditions and natural breathing using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG). Nine participants with epilepsy were involved, and a demodulated band transform (DBT) was used for denoising and analysis. Bipolar re-referencing enhanced spatial resolution and reduced common-mode noise. The researchers found significant interactions in various brain regions, with certain areas more tightly coupled with respiration than others. The phase relationship between iEEG signals and respiratory patterns showed specific phase locking in selected brain sites.

The research employs power spectral density (PSD) estimates, coherence analysis, phase relationships, statistical analysis, and linear mixed-effects modeling to examine neural-respiratory interactions. A hierarchical linear regression model was used for coherence analysis between brain regions, conditions, and breathing modes, accounting for individual differences and variability across specific brain sites.

The study reveals valuable insights into neural-respiratory interactions during anesthetized conditions and natural breathing, which could aid in understanding consciousness, sleep, and respiratory control. However, it was conducted on a limited number of participants with epilepsy, necessitating further research on larger and more diverse populations.

Data availability: Processed data is accessible on Zenodo, while patient privacy restrictions protect raw data. The analysis code can be found on Zenodo and GitHub. All analyses were performed using MATLAB R2023b with the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox, along with the Demodulated Band Transform (DBT) toolbox for signal denoising.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

This scientific study investigates the synchronization of brain neural oscillations with breathing under various conditions: awake, asleep, anesthetized, and ventilator-driven. The findings suggest that during wakefulness, many forebrain regions sync with breathing, but this decreases during sleep, with some areas like the amyggdala and hippocampus still showing significant synchronization. Anesthesia does not affect this synchronization, as the forebrain also synchronizes with artificial breathing. Under ventilator-driven conditions, significant coherence between neural oscillations and ventilator-driven breathing was found across various regions of the forebrain.

The study focuses on Breathing-LFP (Local Field Potentials) coherence in several brain regions like the temporal, sensorimotor, frontal, parietal, insula, hippocampus, amygdala, and precuneus. During automatic breathing, a higher degree of coherence between LFPs and breathing signals was observed compared to ventilator-driven standard or slow, deep ventilation, suggesting enhanced brain synchronization with the respiratory rhythm.

The study sheds light on how different breathing conditions impact brain activity synchronization with breathing, potentially informing our understanding of neural mechanisms underlying respiration and paving the way for new therapies for respiratory disorders.
