Human forebrain neural synchronization and entrainment to breathing during wakefulness, sleep, and external mechanical ventilation

Mowla, Rhone, Kumar, Kovach, Liu, Chan, Kawasaki, Mueller, Kuhn, Frede, Ciliberto, Czech, Thati Ganganna, Owens, Dabrowski, Sprigg, Granner, Simonyan, Nourski, Krause, Banks, Howard III, Davenport, Pattinson, Richerson, Wemmie, Dlouhy | 2026

Final Summary

This study investigates the synchronization of human forebrain neural oscillations with breathing patterns during wakefulness, sleep, and external mechanical ventilation. Using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), researchers identified widespread synchronization across key interoceptive regions like the insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus during wakefulness.

During sleep, overall synchronization decreased but persisted in the amygdala and hippocampus. Interestingly, humans were found to sync without nasal airflow, implying multiple respiratory afferent pathways. Slow, deep breathing entrained more forebrain sites compared to standard ventilation, particularly in the amygdala (0.13-0.26) and hippocampus (0.14-0.21).

The study suggests that certain brain regions are recruited during slow, deep breathing, with the forebrain synchronization flexibly adapting to the frequency of breathing. These findings redefine breathing interoception as a pervasive influence within the forebrain, potentially impacting sleep-related breathing disorders, emotional regulation, and cognitive health.

Limitations include a small sample size, potential limitations due to electrode placement, and the use of anesthesia. Despite these limitations, the findings could have significant implications for understanding breathing-related mechanisms and therapeutic strategies aimed at optimizing respiratory monitoring during sleep in at-risk individuals.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The preamble section of the scientific paper presents a study on human forebrain neural synchronization and entrainment to breathing during wakefulness, sleep, and external mechanical ventilation. The research is a collaborative effort involving multiple researchers from various departments at the University of Iowa, University of Nebraska Medical Center, University of Wisconsin, University of Florida, Massachusetts Eye and Ear, Harvard Medical School, University of Oxford, and more. The study aims to understand how brain activity synchronizes with breathing patterns in different states, providing insights into neurological and respiratory functions during wakefulness, sleep, and artificial ventilation.

ABSTRACT

In this study, the neural mechanisms of breathing interoception, crucial for detecting respiratory threats, were explored using human intracranial recordings. Widespread synchronization between forebrain neural oscillations and breathing rhythms was identified across wakefulness, sleep, and external mechanical ventilation. In wakefulness, key interoceptive regions like insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygala, and hippocampus showed synchronization with breathing. During sleep, while overall synchronization decreased, it persisted in the amygdala and hippocampus. Unlike rodents, nasal airflow was not necessary for this synchronization, suggesting multiple afferent pathways in humans. Intriguingly, breath-driven slow, deep breathing entrained more forebrain sites, potentially explaining how breath-based practices could impact emotion and cognition. The findings redefine breathing interoception as a pervasive influence within the forebrain, with implications for understanding disorders of respiratory awareness, emotional regulation, and cognitive health.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction summarizes the importance of interoceptive awareness of breathing (breathing interoception) for survival and its potential influence on emotional regulation and cognitive performance. Despite its significance, the underlying mechanisms are unclear, particularly during sleep when respiration is critical. This study aims to investigate these mechanisms in humans using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) monitoring. The research focuses on determining if forebrain neural oscillations synchronize with breathing, whether nasal airflow is required for this synchronization, and if the breathing rhythm directly entrains forebrain neural oscillations. These investigations could enhance understanding of breathing interoception in humans, potentially providing insights into sleep-related breathing disorders and sudden death syndromes.

RESULTS

The scientific study investigates the synchronization of brain activity with breathing patterns in humans. Three experimental paradigms were employed: automatic breathing, external mechanical ventilation, and slow, deep breathing using an externally controlled ventilator. The results reveal that various brain regions, such as the sensorimotor cortex, insula, and parts of the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes, synchronize with the breathing rhythm.

Slow, deep breathing engages and entrains more brain sites compared to standard ventilation, demonstrating that the brain adapts dynamically to different breathing frequencies and tidal volumes. The study observed significant increases in coherence between brain activity and slow, deep ventilation at specific sites in the amygala (0.13-0.26) and hippocampus (0.14-0.21). Notably, the increase in coherence during slow, deep ventilation at 0.08 Hz reached 0.69 (p<0.0001) in the amygala and 0.68 (p<0.0001) in the hippocampus. These findings suggest that certain brain regions are recruited and synchronized during slow, deep breathing, with forebrain synchronization flexibly adapting to the frequency of breathing.

DISCUSSION

The study investigated nine participants' intracranial recordings for seizure focus localization, focusing on breathing interoception. Findings showed that the forebrain synchronizes with breathing, similar to rodent studies, with distinct sites in known interoceptive regions such as the posterior dorsal insula, somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala identified during wakefulness. Sites outside classic interoceptive regions were also found to synchronize with breathing, potentially linking respiratory signals to higher-order cognitive, emotional, and sensorimotor networks.

Sleep reduced synchronization in the cortex, with a possible redistribution of interoceptive processing toward subcortical structures like the hippocampus and amygdala during sleep. The persistent amygdala synchronization with breathing may represent an evolutionarily adaptive vigilance mechanism.

Contrary to previous studies suggesting that airflow through nasal passages is required for forebrain synchronization, this study found that humans can sync without it, implying multiple respiratory afferent pathways are critical for synchronization. The findings suggest breathing could drive or shape forebrain neural oscillations and may have implications for stress management and disorders linked to disrupted interoceptive signaling.

Limitations of the study include a small sample size, potential limitations due to electrode placement, and the use of anesthesia. Despite these limitations, the findings could have significant implications for understanding breathing-related mechanisms, sleep-related breathing disorders, sudden death syndromes, and therapeutic strategies aimed at optimizing respiratory monitoring during sleep in at-risk individuals.

METHODS

The study investigates neural-respiratory interactions in humans using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG). 10 participants with epilepsy underwent iEEG recording at a sampling rate of 2kHz, denoised with demodulated band transform (DBT). The coherence between breathing and LFPs was calculated across different brain regions and conditions using Welch's method and general linear model (GLM), with multiple comparisons controlled by Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction.

Significant neural-respiratory interactions were found in various brain regions, particularly in the frontal cortex during automatic breathing and in the posterior cingulate cortex during ventilator-driven slow, deep breathing. The research utilizes a hierarchical linear regression model to analyze brain coherence during different breathing conditions, accounting for individual differences and condition-dependent changes. Data and code for the analyses are available on Zenodo and GitHub, but raw iEEG data cannot be shared due to patient privacy and IRB guidelines.
