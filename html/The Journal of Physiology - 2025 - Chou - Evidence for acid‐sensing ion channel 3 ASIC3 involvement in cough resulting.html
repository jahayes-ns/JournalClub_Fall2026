Final Summary

Title: Acid-Sensing Ion Channels (ASICs) in Cough Evoked by Aspiration of Gastric Fluid

This study, published in J Physiol 604.3 (2026), investigates the role of ASICs in coughing triggered by aspiration of gastric fluid using a multidisciplinary animal model approach. The research aimed to understand the mechanisms underlying cough reflexes induced by gastric fluid aspiration, with a focus on ASICs as key regulators.

Key findings include: 1. Coughing initiated by aspirated gastric fluid involves activation of peripheral terminals of C-fibers and cough receptors through ASICs. 2. Gastric fluid and citric acid cause cough reflexes in guinea pigs, with an acidic pH being essential for this effect. 3. Vagal afferent nerves responsible for regulating cough express mRNA for ASIC1, ASIC2, and ASIC3. 4. The coughing evoked by gastric fluid and acid was prevented by the ASIC inhibitors diminazene and diclofenac but not by transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) blockade.

The authors conclude that airway mucosal afferent neuronal ASIC channels are essential for airway defense against aspiration of gastric fluid, suggesting they may increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia in susceptible patients. The study also implies potential applications in understanding and treating conditions like gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).

The research employed techniques including gastric fluid aspiration, single cell RT-PCR analysis of vagal ganglia, and electrophysiology recording action potentials of tracheal afferent neurons. The study was funded by grants from the National Institutes of Health, with B. J. Canning taking responsibility for maintaining the content's integrity and data accuracy. Keywords associated with the study include capsaicin, diminazene, reflux, TRPV1, and vagal.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study presented in J Physiol 604.3 (2026) provides evidence for the involvement of acid-sensing ion channel 3 (ASIC3) in cough resulting from aspiration of gastric fluid. The research, conducted by a multidisciplinary team, utilized an animal model to investigate the mechanisms behind coughing initiated by aspirated gastric fluid.

Key findings include: 1. The coughing evoked by aspirated gastric fluid involves activation of ASICs localized to the peripheral terminals of C-fibres and cough receptors. 2. Gastric fluid and citric acid were found to evoke cough reflexes in guinea-pigs, with an acidic pH being essential for this effect. 3. Vagal afferent nerves regulating cough expressed mRNA for ASIC1, ASIC2, and ASIC3. 4. The coughing evoked by gastric fluid and by acid was prevented by the ASIC inhibitors diminazene and diclofenac but not by transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 blockade.

The authors conclude that airway mucosal afferent neuronal ASIC channels are essential to airway defense against aspiration of gastric fluid, and speculate that dysfunction in these pathways may increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia in susceptible patients. The study also suggests that this signaling pathway could explain the emergence of cough in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the life-threatening acute lung injury (ALI) that can result from aspiration of gastric contents, which remains a significant issue with over 25% of patients with ALI/ARDS dying in hospital. Chronic aspiration is also linked to various respiratory diseases and organ rejection in lung transplant recipients. The cough reflex plays a crucial role in airway defense against aspiration, and an ineffective or dysfunctional cough reflex increases susceptibility to these conditions. The paper aims to investigate the mechanisms underlying cough evoked by aspiration of gastric fluid, focusing on acid-sensitive ion channels, particularly ASICs, as essential regulators of the cough reflex.

METHODS

The study investigates the role of acid-sensing ion channels (ASICs) in cough evoked by gastric fluid aspiration, using guinea pigs as subjects. Methods include:

  1. Aspiration of gastric fluid and preparation of tracheal strips for contractile responses to capsaicin.
  2. Single cell RT-PCR analysis of vagal ganglia to identify neurochemistry of retrogradely labeled neurons.
  3. Electrophysiology recording action potentials of tracheal afferent neurons in response to citric acid challenge, mechanical stimulation, or capsaicin.

Animals were euthanized following AVMA guidelines. The research aims to understand cough reflex mechanisms and potentially develop new treatments for conditions like aspiration pneumonia. Statistical analysis was performed using Excel, Prism, and VassarStats, with significance set at P < 0.05. Various reagents were used, including drugs purchased from Sigma, Alomone Labs, AstraZeneca, and Invitrogen, with indomethacin in all buffers to control inflammation and DMSO concentrations kept low for drug treatments and vehicle controls.

RESULTS

The study extracted gastric fluid from guinea-pig stomachs and found varying levels of acidity with a mean pH of 3.7 and a range of 1.5–5.6. When topically applied to the tracheal or laryngeal mucosa of anesthetized guinea-pigs, gastric fluid caused coughing similar to that induced by acid in this model. The cough responses were dependent on the acidity of the solution, with solutions at pH ≤3.6 causing coughing in eight out of twelve experiments, while solutions at pH ≥3.8 did not evoke coughing in any experiments.

The study also investigated a drug called diminazene and its effect on acid-induced coughing. Diminazene dose-dependently inhibited acid-evoked action potential discharge in jugular and nodose ganglia neurones projecting to the guinea-pig trachea, with more pronounced effects on nodose ganglia Aδ fibers. The study concluded that diminazene may be useful for treating acid-induced coughing.

DISCUSSION

The discussed research article focuses on acid-sensitive ion channels (ASICs) and their role in the cough response to gastric fluid aspiration. Key findings include:

  1. ASIC3-dependent coughing is induced by gastric fluid aspiration, which can be inhibited by the ASIC3 blocker diclofenac.
  2. Protons are likely central to the acute tussive effects of gastric fluid, although other acid-sensitive signaling mechanisms may also contribute.
  3. The research implies potential implications for identifying patients at risk for aspiration pneumonia and developing therapeutic strategies for cough hypersensitivity.
  4. ASICs have been linked to pain-related processes but may vary in their role depending on the context or organ system being studied.
  5. There is uncertainty about whether ASIC blockade would be beneficial in cough therapy, as it could potentially have inhibitory effects under certain conditions.
  6. The study suggests a link between ASIC blockade or vagal reflex pathway defects (caused by ASIC activation) and an increased risk of aspiration-induced pneumonia and acute lung injury due to suppression of productive cough, exposing airways to harmful gastric fluid components.
  7. Previous studies support this theory, showing that suppressing cough may increase the risk of pneumonia or acute lung injury, while enhanced responsiveness to tussive stimuli may reduce the risk of aspiration pneumonia.
  8. The findings suggest potential unintended consequences of ASIC inhibition in drug treatment strategies.
