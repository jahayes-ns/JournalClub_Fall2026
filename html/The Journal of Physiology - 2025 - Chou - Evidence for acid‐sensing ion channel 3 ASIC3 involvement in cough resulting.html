Evidence for acid-sensing ion channel 3 (ASIC3) involvement in cough resulting from aspiration of gastric fluid

Chou, Mori, Pavelkova, Liu, Liptak, Meeker, Halicka, Undem, Kollarik, Canning | 2026

Final Summary

In this study published in The Journal of Physiology, researchers provide evidence for the role of acid-sensing ion channel 3 (ASIC3) in cough resulting from aspiration of gastric fluid in guinea pigs. The research question focuses on understanding the essential role of protons in gastric fluid-evoked coughing and the molecular, electrophysiological, and functional evidence that acid-sensitive ion channels (ASICs) are primarily responsible for initiating cough evoked by rapid airway acidification by acid or gastric contents.

The methods used included animal preparation, capsaicin-induced contractions, electrophysiology, ASIC3-dependent coughing, single cell RT-PCR analysis, and action potential study. The key findings showed that acidic gastric fluids induced coughing comparable to that initiated by topically applied acid and that afferent neurons with cell bodies in the jugular and nodose ganglia of the vagus nerves are involved in initiating cough due to airway surface liquid acidification. Diminazene, a compound known for its effects on acid-induced activation of guinea-pig tracheal vagal afferent neurons, dose-dependently inhibited citric acid and gastric fluid evoked coughing in anesthetized guinea-pigs, indicating the significant role of ASIC3 in the development of cough due to acid aspiration.

The researchers concluded that airway mucosal afferent neuronal ASIC channels are essential to airway defense against aspiration of gastric fluid and speculate that dysfunction of these reflex pathways may increase the risk for aspiration pneumonia in susceptible patients. The study also suggests that this signaling pathway could explain the emergence of cough in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). However, the data presented in this study suggests potential unintended consequences of ASIC inhibition as a drug treatment strategy, such as inhibition of productive cough and increased risk of aspiration-induced pneumonia and acute lung injury.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a study published in The Journal of Physiology, researchers provide evidence for the involvement of acid-sensing ion channel 3 (ASIC3) in cough resulting from aspiration of gastric fluid. The study was conducted on guinea pigs and utilized an animal model to investigate the mechanisms that trigger cough when gastric fluid is aspirated.

The team observed that the acidity of gastric fluid plays a crucial role in initiating cough, with an acidic pH being essential for its tussive actions. They found that vagal afferent nerves responsible for initiating this reflex express mRNA for the acid-sensitive ion channels ASIC1, ASIC2, and ASIC3. The coughing evoked by gastric fluid and by protons was prevented by ASIC inhibitors diminazene and diclofenac but not by transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 blockade.

Based on these findings, the researchers concluded that airway mucosal afferent neuronal ASIC channels are essential to airway defense against aspiration of gastric fluid. They speculate that dysfunction of these reflex pathways may increase the risk for aspiration pneumonia in susceptible patients. The study also suggests that this signaling pathway could explain the emergence of cough in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the life-threatening acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) that can occur due to aspiration of gastric contents. Despite improvements in diagnostics, treatment strategies, and increased awareness of the underlying pathophysiology, more than 25% of patients with ALI/ARDS still die in hospital. Chronic aspiration or microaspiration may also contribute to pulmonary fibrosis, asthma exacerbations, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and organ rejection in lung transplant recipients.

The cough reflex is crucial for airway defense against aspiration, and an ineffective or dysfunctional cough reflex increases susceptibility to acute lung injury and pneumonia. The role of cough in airway protection against aspiration is further implicated by the chronic cough that emerges with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GORD).

The paper focuses on the signaling pathways underlying the cough reflex in GORD, which may result from central sensitization or direct activation of bronchopulmonary vagal afferents. The pH of refluxate is considered critical to initiating cough, but other components such as pepsin, trypsin, bilirubin, and bile acids may also be important.

The paper also explores the role of capsaicin-sensitive C-fibers and acid-sensitive, mechanically-sensitive Aδ 'cough receptors' in regulating the cough reflex. Protons activate these vagal afferent nerve subtypes, with electrophysiological evidence implicating gating of the capsaicin-sensitive cation channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) expressed by C-fibers and other acid-sensitive ion channels expressed by both afferent nerve subtypes. These ion channels have been the focus of drug discovery efforts for conditions including pain and cough, with several potent and selective TRPV1 blockers described and evaluated clinically.

In this study, the authors aim to describe the essential role of protons in gastric fluid-evoked coughing in guinea pigs and present molecular, electrophysiological, and functional evidence that acid-sensitive ion channels (ASICs) are primarily responsible for the initiation of cough evoked by rapid airway acidification by acid or gastric contents.

METHODS

The scientific paper presents a series of methods used to study cough mechanisms, specifically in response to gastric aspiration and the role of various ion channels and neurochemicals.

  1. Animal Preparation: Guinea pigs were euthanized following AVMA guidelines, and tracheal strips were isolated.
  2. Capsaicin-Induced Contractions: The contractile responses to capsaicin were measured in these tracheal strips.
  3. Electrophysiology: Electrical activity of tracheal afferent neurons was recorded in response to citric acid challenges and mechanical stimuli, with conduction velocities also measured.
  4. ASIC3-Dependent Coughing: The role of ASIC3 in cough was studied by blocking this channel and observing the effects on cough responses.
  5. Single Cell RT-PCR Analysis: Gene expression analysis was performed to identify neurochemistry, specifically for protein gene product 9.5 (neuronal marker), TRPV1 (C-fibre identifier), and ASICs (ASIC1, ASIC2, and ASIC3).
  6. Action Potential Study: The time elapsed between electrical stimulation and recorded action potential in the trachea and jugular or nodose ganglia was measured using statistical analysis with Excel, Prism, VassarStats, t tests, analyses of variance, Mann–Whitney tests, Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, chi-squared analyses, and various drugs such as capsaicin, diclofenac, and indomethacin. All buffers contained 3 μm indomethacin to maintain consistency. The DMSO concentration in the perfusing buffers did not exceed 0.1 μL mL−1.

RESULTS

In this study, gastric fluid samples extracted from guinea-pig stomachs displayed varying levels of acidity with a mean pH of 3.7. When applied topically to the tracheal and laryngeal mucosa of anesthetized guinea-pigs, these acidic gastric fluids induced coughing comparable to that initiated by topically applied acid. The cough responses were dependent upon the acidity of the solution, with samples having a pH ≤3.6 evoking coughing in 8 out of 12 experiments, while those with a pH ≥3.8 failed to induce coughing.

The study further revealed the involvement of afferent neurones with cell bodies in the jugular and nodose ganglia of the vagus nerves in initiating cough due to airway surface liquid acidification. RT-PCR performed on single nodose and jugular ganglia neurones showed that ASIC subunit mRNA was reliably detected.

Diminazene, a compound known for its effects on acid-induced activation of guinea-pig tracheal vagal afferent neurons, dose-dependently inhibited citric acid and gastric fluid evoked coughing in anesthetized guinea-pigs. The study suggests that ASIC3 plays a significant role in the development of cough due to acid aspiration.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific paper explores the role of Acid-Sensing Ion Channels (ASICs) in coughing, specifically in response to aspiration of gastric fluid. The research suggests that ASIC3 significantly contributes to the acute tussive effects, while ASIC1 might not be as influential. This study's findings could potentially help identify patients at risk for aspiration pneumonia and develop treatment strategies for Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD).

The discussion also highlights the link between ASIC blockade and increased risk of aspiration-induced pneumonia and acute lung injury, due to possible inhibition of productive cough. This could expose airways to harmful components of gastric fluid. The paper references several studies supporting this hypothesis (Bynum & Pierce, 1976; Castro et al., 2019; du Moulin et al., 1982; Gatti et al., 2006; Gu & Lee, 2010; Koufman, 1991; Meixiong et al., 2019; Meixiong et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). However, the data presented in this study suggests potential unintended consequences of ASIC inhibition as a drug treatment strategy. The paper seems to be missing information about dry cough and its relation to ASICs.
