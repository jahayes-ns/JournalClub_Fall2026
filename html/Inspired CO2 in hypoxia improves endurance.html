Final Summary

Title: Low-Dose CO2 Inhalation for Enhanced Oxygenation and Exercise Capacity at High Altitudes

Authors: Shanfeng Liang, et al. Journal: Respirology (2026)

This double-blind pilot study examines the potential benefits of low-dose CO2 inhalation for improving oxygenation and exercise capacity in individuals at high altitudes. The research, led by an international team, assessed 28 participants from China, Australia, the USA, and the UK who underwent Incremental Shuttle Walk Test (ISWT) and Endurance Shuttle Walk Test (ESWT) both at sea level and a high altitude of 4505 meters. Participants were randomly divided into an intervention group receiving low-dose CO2 inhalation or a control group breathing ambient air.

Key findings revealed no significant difference in exercise capacity during ESWT between groups at high altitude, but the CO2 group demonstrated improvements in oxygen saturation (SpO2), reduced dyspnoea and fatigue scores, and minimal changes in respiratory rate (RR) and heart rate (HR). Blood gas measurements from 23 participants showed that CO2 inhalation increased PaCO2 and decreased pH, with no significant impact on PaO2 compared to ambient air.

While the study suggests that intermittent CO2 inhalation may alleviate symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) and preserve functional performance at high altitude, further research is needed to confirm these findings and explore long-term effects on exercise performance and health outcomes. The study was ethically conducted with participants' informed consent and funded by multiple grants.

Further research is needed to confirm these findings and establish optimal dosing regimens for low-dose CO2 inhalation as a potential aid for individuals living or working at high altitudes.

ABSTRACT

This research paper published in Respirology explores the effects of inhaling a low concentration (2.5%) of carbon dioxide (CO2) on exercise capacity, blood gas levels, and symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) at high altitude compared to breathing ambient air.

The study involved 28 participants who performed Incremental Shuttle Walk Test (ISWT) and Endurance Shuttle Walk Test (ESWT) at sea level and a high altitude of 4505 meters, with some inhaling CO2 and others breathing ambient air. The results showed that exercise capacity was lower at high altitude for both groups but no significant differences were found between distances covered during ESWT at high altitude with and without CO2 inhalation.

Blood gas measurements from 23 participants revealed that CO2 inhalation increased PaCO2 and decreased pH, with no significant effect on PaO2 compared to ambient air. At the end of an exercise test (ESWT) at high altitude, the CO2 group showed a significant increase in SpO2, non-significant changes in RR and HR, and a decrease in dyspnoea and fatigue scores compared to the ambient air group.

The study suggests that intermittent CO2 inhalation may provide symptomatic relief for AMS and preserve functional performance at high altitude, but further research is needed to confirm these findings and investigate long-term effects on exercise performance and health outcomes. The research was funded by multiple grants and was conducted ethically with participants' informed consent.
