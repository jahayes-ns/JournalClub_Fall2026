Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Chen et al., published in Cell (2018), investigates why complete paralysis occurs despite anatomically incomplete spinal cord injuries in humans. The research was conducted on mice with staggered bilateral hemisections, leaving dormant relay circuits intact. A small-molecule screen led to the discovery of a KCC2 agonist that restored stepping ability in these paralyzed mice, an effect mimicked by selective expression of KCC2 or hyperpolarizing DREADDs (designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs) in inhibitory interneurons between and around the spinal lesions. By transforming the injury-induced dysfunctional spinal circuit into a functional state, these treatments facilitated the relay of brain-derived commands toward the lumbar spinal cord. The findings suggest that spinal inhibitory interneurons limit the integration of descending inputs into relay circuits after injury and identify KCC2 agonists as promising treatments for promoting functional recovery after spinal cord injury.

INTRODUCTION

The paper introduces research focused on understanding why spared connections in the human spinal cord, although present after a spinal cord injury (SCI), often fail to function and how they might be reactivated to aid in recovery. The primary focus is on the central pattern generator (CPG) in the lumbar spinal cord, responsible for basic locomotion.

After SCI, the lumbar spinal cord fails to initiate locomotor function due to a disruption of connections between descending inputs from the brain and the spinal cord. To restore function, compensatory axon regrowth and synapse reorganization at different spinal levels can help re-establish these connections. In severe SCI cases, intraspinal networks consisting of local interneurons and projecting propriospinal neurons may serve as indirect relay pathways for brain-derived motor commands to the lumbar spinal cord.

Factors limiting the ability of spared connections to compensate after SCI include compromised neuronal firing and conduction properties or non-functional local spinal circuits due to injury. The study aims to identify small molecule compounds that can modulate neuronal excitability in an unbiased manner, with the goal of reactivating dormant spinal circuitry and facilitating functional recovery in SCI models. These compounds are chosen because they can cross the blood-brain barrier, enabling systemic administration to analyze their effects in SCI animal models.

RESULTS

The study explores the impact of KCC2, a potassium-chloride co-transporter, on functional recovery in mice with staggered spinal cord injuries. Both CLP290, an activator of KCC2, and AAV-PHP.B-syn-HA-KCC2 (a vector delivering KCC2 to the central nervous system) led to significant improvements in stepping abilities, body weight support, and stride length.

The research indicates that selective expression of KCC2 in inhibitory interneurons contributes to functional recovery, with injury-induced KCC2 downregulation restoring an immature state where GABA and glycine receptors can depolarize neurons.

When KCC2 was introduced into spinal cord segments between and around staggered lesions in thoracic levels, it led to persistent functional recovery comparable to AAV-PHP.B-KCC2 treatment. Conversely, treatments with AAV-KCC2 or AAV-FLEX-KCC2 in lumbar segments did not yield significant recovery.

The study also found that reducing the excitability of inhibitory interneurons through hM4Di-mCherry expression mimicked the effects of KCC2/CLP290, while direct activation of excitatory interneurons did not lead to functional recovery, suggesting that reducing inhibitory interneuron excitability is an effective strategy for promoting lasting functional recovery after severe spinal cord injury.
