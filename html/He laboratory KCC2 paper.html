Reactivation of Dormant Relay Pathways in Injured Spinal Cord by KCC2 Manipulations

Chen, Li, Yu, Zicong Zhang, Brommer, Williams, Liu, Hegarty, Zhou, Zhu, Gu, He | 2018

Final Summary

Chen et al.'s study (2018) explores why spinal cord injuries (SCIs) result in complete paralysis despite intact connections, focusing on a KCC2 agonist, CLP290, that restores stepping ability in mice with bilateral hemisections. The researchers found that overexpressing KCC2 or hyperpolarizing DREADDs in inhibitory interneurons between and around the lesions facilitated the relay of brain-derived commands to the lumbar spinal cord, thus transforming the injury-induced dysfunctional circuit into a functional state. The study suggests that inhibiting spinal inhibitory interneurons may limit the integration of descending inputs into relay circuits after SCI, making them potential targets for therapeutic intervention. CLP290's mechanism involves enhancing neural activity by modulating KCC2 expression, with the potential to restore normal ion balance and improve neuronal function post-SCI. The findings underscore the promise of CLP290 as a treatment for promoting functional recovery after SCI.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Chen et al. (2018) investigates the reasons behind complete paralysis despite anatomically incomplete spinal cord injuries in mice. The researchers performed a small-molecule screen on mice with bilateral hemisections, where direct brain-derived innervation is lost, but dormant relay circuits remain. They discovered that a KCC2 agonist restored stepping ability in these paralyzed mice. This effect could also be mimicked by selective expression of KCC2 or hyperpolarizing DREADDs (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs) in inhibitory interneurons between and around the staggered spinal lesions. The treatment facilitated the relay of brain-derived commands toward the lumbar spinal cord, transforming the injury-induced dysfunctional spinal circuit into a functional state. The findings suggest that spinal inhibitory interneurons may limit the integration of descending inputs into relay circuits after injury, and KCC2 agonists could be promising treatments for promoting functional recovery after spinal cord injury.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the paradoxical situation in human spinal cord injuries (SCIs), where although many connections within the spinal cord remain intact after injury, patients often experience a total loss of muscle control and sensation below the injury level. This suggests that these spared connections are functionally dormant. The paper proposes that epidural stimulation combined with rehabilitative training can help some chronically paralyzed SCI patients regain voluntary movement by reactivating dormant spinal circuitry.

The primary focus of the study is on the central pattern generator (CPG) in the lumbar spinal cord, responsible for basic locomotion, which fails to initiate function after SCI due to disrupted connections between descending inputs and the lumbar spinal cord. The authors aim to understand why these spared connections have limited ability to compensate after SCI, and how they can best be reactivated to restore function.

The paper further explores various hypotheses for the dysfunction of spared connections after SCI, such as compromised neuron firing and conduction properties or non-functional local spinal cord circuits. The study also investigates whether inhibiting or enhancing the excitability of these spared spinal neurons would be beneficial for functional recovery after SCI.

The research employs a non-biased compound screening approach to identify small molecules that can reactivate dormant spinal circuitry and contribute to functional recovery in SCI models. These compounds are developed to target key regulators such as ion channels and receptors, many of which can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) for systemic administration in SCI animal models.

RESULTS

The research paper investigates the compound CLP290's role in enhancing hindlimb function recovery in mice with staggered spinal cord injuries (SCIs). It finds that KCC2, a protein involved in regulating chloride ions, plays a critical role in this process.

The study reveals that overexpressing KCC2 in the central nervous system neurons produces effects similar to those of CLP290. Inhibitory interneurons are identified as potential targets for enhancing functional recovery.

Key findings include: 1. AAV-mediated gene delivery techniques were used to express KCC2 selectively in different types of neurons. 2. Overexpression of KCC2 in spinal cord segments between and around the staggered lesions resulted in significant and persistent functional recovery, comparable to AAV-PHP.B-KCC2 treatment. 3. CLP290/KCC2 primarily acts on inhibitory neurons in the relay zone, between and adjacent to the lesion sites in thoracic spinal cord levels, to facilitate hindlimb functional recovery. 4. Chronic KCC2/CLP290 treatment restored a more physiological neuronal activity pattern, making the local spinal cord circuitry more receptive to descending inputs. 5. Reducing the excitability of inhibitory interneurons could mimic the effects of KCC2/CLP290, while activating excitatory interneurons did not lead to functional recovery.

These findings suggest that KCC2 could be a potential target for therapeutic interventions aimed at promoting recovery in patients with spinal cord injuries.

DISCUSSION

This is a research article titled "Reversing spinal cord injury by boosting neural activity with small molecules" published in the journal Cell on July 26, 2018. The study explores the potential of a small molecule called CLP290 to reverse the effects of spinal cord injuries (SCIs) by enhancing neural activity.

The authors used various experimental models and techniques, including surgical procedures, post-surgical treatments, behavioral experiments, EMG recording, cortical stimulation, immunohistochemistry, and western blotting. They found that CLP290 treatment led to the recovery of hindlimb function in mice with complete spinal cord injuries (SCIs).

The study also investigated the mechanism behind this effect, finding that CLP290 increased neural activity by modulating the expression of a protein called KCC2. The authors suggest that this increase in neural activity may help to restore the normal balance of ions within neurons and improve their function after SCI.

The study concludes that CLP290 is a promising therapeutic agent for the treatment of spinal cord injuries, and future research should investigate its potential in combination with other treatments such as rehabilitative training.
