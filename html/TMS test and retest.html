Test-retest reliability of threshold-tracking TMS and peripheral nerve testing in healthy adults and individuals with chronic cervical spinal cord injury

Hrycyk, Gramm Kristensen, Tankisi, Spooren, Tedesco Triccas

Keywords: Transcranial magnetic stimulation (tms), Nerve excitability testing (net), Reliability, Spinal cord injury (SCI), Threshold tracking

Final Summary

The study by Hrycyk et al. (2026) aims to establish the test-retest reliability of neuroexcitability measures using Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) of the primary motor cortex (M1) and nerve excitability tests (NET) of the median nerve in healthy adults and individuals with chronic cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). The research focuses on Threshold-Tracking (T-T) Resting Motor Threshold (RMT) and Rate of Muscle Response (R-R RMT), assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), standard error of measurement (SEM), smallest detectable change (SDC), and coefficient of variation (CV).

The study involved 27 healthy controls and 10 individuals with chronic cervical SCI, who underwent a test-retest assessment with approximately seven days in between. Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs) were measured at different muscle locations depending on the participant group.

Results showed excellent reliability for resting motor threshold and intermediate-to-good reliability for stimulus-response function using TMS measures. The majority of NET outcome variables showed at least moderate reliability estimates in both groups. While T-T RMT was found to be reliable with a fast acquisition rate, the reliability of NET outcome variables varied, particularly in the SCI group.

The study concludes that threshold-tracking-based measures of the central and peripheral nervous systems provide reliable measures of neuroexcitability, both in healthy adults and individuals with SCI. However, it suggests that future research with larger sample sizes and further parameter analysis is necessary to confirm these findings and determine optimal parameters for clinical decision-making.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Hrycyk et al. (2026) investigates the test-retest reliability of neuroexcitability measures in healthy adults and individuals with chronic cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). The research focuses on threshold-tracking Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) of the primary motor cortex (M1) and nerve excitability tests (NET) of the median nerve.

The study involved 27 healthy controls and 10 individuals with chronic cervical SCI, who underwent a test-retest assessment with approximately seven days in between. Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs) were measured at the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) of the dominant hand in healthy controls and either APB or extensor carpi radialis longus in individuals with SCI.

Results showed excellent reliability for resting motor threshold and intermediate-to-good reliability for stimulus-response function using TMS measures. The majority of NET outcome variables showed at least moderate reliability estimates in both the healthy controls and SCI groups. The study concludes that threshold-tracking-based measures of the central and peripheral nervous systems provide reliable measures of neuroexcitability, both in healthy adults and individuals with SCI. This supports the consideration of these measures in research and clinical settings.

METHOD

The research compares the test-retest reliability of T-T RMT (Threshold Tracking) and R-R RMT (Rate of Muscle Response) methods, used for measuring corticomotor and nerve excitability via Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) and Nerve Excitability Testing (NET), in 27 healthy adults and 10 individuals with chronic cervical Spinal Cord Injury (SCI).

The study assessed intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), standard error of measurement (SEM), smallest detectable change (SDC), and coefficient of variation (CV) for both TMS and NET assessments. The ICC values, generally above 0.7, indicate good test-retest reliability for most subtests.

T-T RMT was found to be reliable with a fast acquisition rate, making it suitable for clinical research. However, the reliability of NET outcome variables varied, with SCI participants showing lower ICC values and greater variability. Limitations included a small sample size in the SCI group, variations in TMS coil placement and hand position, and active lifestyles of the participants.

The study suggests that threshold-tracking techniques are reliable for measuring central and peripheral nervous system excitability in neurological disorder populations such as stroke or SCI patients. Future research with larger sample sizes and further parameter analysis is necessary to confirm these findings and determine optimal parameters for clinical decision-making.
