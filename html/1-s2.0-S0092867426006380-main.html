Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a new study published in Cell, Belliveau et al. identified TMEM154/Galvanin as a cellular antenna that senses electrical gradients and guides migration towards the cathode. Cells maintain an electrical potential across their plasma membrane due to unequal ion distributions created by ion pumps and channels. Exogenous electrical fields affect various cellular processes outside neurons, including electrotaxis or migration in electric fields, first reported in protozoa in 1890.

In the study, Belliveau et al. conducted a genome-wide loss-of-function electrotaxis screen using human granulocytes. They found that Galvanin, a transmembrane receptor, is crucial for electrotaxis as Galvanin-deficient cells lost their capacity to perform electrotaxis but retained intact chemotaxis. The authors confirmed this finding in various cell types and demonstrated that ectopically expressed Galvanin can make a non-electrotactic cell type electrotactic.

Further experiments showed that Galvanin rapidly redistributes to the anodal pole of the cell in an electrical field, suggesting an electrophoretic process. This behavior was consistent with previous work, which suggested that single-cell electrotaxis is the consequence of charged surface molecules being dragged toward one pole of the cell. The study provides compelling evidence for Galvanin as a specific receptor conferring electrotactic sensing capacity to cells.
