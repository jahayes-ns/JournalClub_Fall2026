The cellular inflammatory response in human spinal cords after injury

Fleming, Norenberg, Ramsay, Dekaban, Marcillo, Saenz, Pasquale-Styles, Dietrich, Weaver | 2006

Keywords: inflammation, macrophage, neutrophil, oxidative activity, SCI

Final Summary

Title: Inflammatory Response and Enzyme Expression in Acute Spinal Cord Injuries: A Histological Analysis

Research Question: This study investigates the inflammatory response, enzyme expression, and histopathological changes in acute spinal cord injuries (SCI) in humans, focusing on neutrophils, macrophages, activated microglia, CD8+ cells, and Matrix Metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9).

Methods: The research involves a histological analysis using immunohistochemical staining techniques and quantification methods to measure the area of necrosis at lesion epicenters. Semi-quantitative assessment of overall injury in spinal cord cross-sections is conducted, along with a method for quantifying neutrophil, microglia/macrophage, and MPO immunolabelling. Digital images are captured using a Retiga 1300 videocamera.

Key Findings: Neutrophils are the earliest responders, expressing MPO and gp91phox as early as 0-4 hours post-injury. At later time points, some macrophages and activated microglia also express gp91phox but not defensin. Macrophages play a significant role in tissue clearing, expressing MPO, defensin, and CD68 to varying degrees. The inflammatory response persists for weeks to months post-injury, with neutrophils dominating the scene within three days of SCI at the lesion epicenter, while lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ cells, appear weeks to months post-injury.

Conclusions: The study suggests a complex immune response to SCI that could have implications for therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting recovery after spinal cord injuries. Limiting neutrophil entry within the first 3 days post-injury is crucial, and treatments like anti-CD11d monoclonal antibody, which improves neurological outcome in rats by blocking neutrophil entry, may have potential benefits as a clinical neuroprotective therapy.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study published in Brain (2006) explores the inflammatory response in human spinal cords following injury. Researchers evaluated the time course and distribution of pathological change, inflammatory cells, and microglial activation in post-mortem spinal cords from patients who died at the scene or survived up to a year after SCI.

The researchers identified three zones of injury: Zone 1 (necrotic areas and cavities) expanded over time, while the overall injury size remained constant. Zones 2 (areas with axonal swellings, inflammation, and Wallerian degeneration) and 3 (histologically intact areas) also showed up in the spinal cord after SCI.

Neutrophils were most numerous within 1–3 days after SCI and were detected for up to 10 days. Activated microglia, monocytes/macrophages, and phagocytic macrophages were present for weeks to months post-injury. A few CD8+ lymphocytes were found throughout the sampling intervals.

The study further revealed that neutrophils and activated microglia exhibit oxidative and proteolytic enzymes, which could cause damage. Anti-inflammatory 'neuroprotective' strategies should focus on preventing early neutrophil influx and modifying microglial activation, as these cells appear within the first few days of SCI.

INTRODUCTION

The introduced study is a histological analysis focusing on the inflammatory response in acute spinal cord injuries. The research investigates the influx and distribution of various immune cells (neutrophils, macrophages, T-cells, B-cells) and activation of enzymes (MPO, gp91phox) in patient tissue samples. Immunohistochemical staining techniques are used for identification, and a quantification method is employed to measure the area of necrosis at lesion epicenters. The study also includes control sections and negative controls for specificity.

A semi-quantitative assessment of overall injury in spinal cord cross-sections is conducted, with eight distinct regions scored based on injury severity (ranging from 0 to 5). The paper also details a method for quantifying neutrophil, microglia/macrophage, and MPO immunolabelling. Digital images are captured using a Retiga 1300 videocamera, and the study aims to provide insights into immune response in acute spinal cord injuries, potentially contributing to a better understanding of their pathophysiology.

Additionally, the paper describes histopathological changes observed, such as cystic cavities, edema, and axonal swelling, with calculated overall injury scores for both grey and white matter regions without bias in severity or location.

BACKGROUND

The paper quantifies the inflammatory response in the spinal cord following injury, focusing on neutrophils, microglia/macrophages, and CD8+ cells. The method used to analyze the inflammatory response involves determining the cumulative area occupied by immunoreactive pixels (associated with defensin, MPO, or CD68) as a percentage of the spinal cord's cross-sectional area, excluding the pia. This approach allows for the assessment of the inflammatory response attributable to specific cell types and provides an overall estimate of the response extent based on cell numbers and size.

To quantify neutrophils, microglia/macrophages, and distinguish them from one another when not fully apparent in a section or clustered, the method employed area measurement instead of cell counting. To analyze defensin and MPO immunolabeling specificity for neutrophils, their areas were compared with corresponding CD68-ir cells (monocytes/macrophages) areas.

CD8+cells could not be assessed using the area measurement method due to their scarcity in spinal cords; instead, they were counted in four randomly selected high power fields of view and averaged per case.

Morphological characterization of microglia and macrophages was performed based on immunoreactivity for CD68, with ramified microglia, activated microglia, and macrophages being distinctly defined. Despite cell clustering, sufficient numbers were separate enough to permit morphology examination for the expression of MPO, gp91phox, and MMP-9 at each time interval after injury.

Statistical analysis was carried out using parametric statistical tests such as one-way ANOVA, Fisher's LSD protected t-test, and Spearman’s correlation analysis, with the required probability value set to P < 0.05 for statistical significance.

RESULTS

The study by Fleming et al. (2006) focuses on the immune response and enzyme expression in spinal cord injuries (SCI) in rats. After an SCI, various immune cells including neutrophils, macrophages, activated microglia, CD8+, CD4+ lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes are involved at different stages.

Neutrophils are the earliest responders, expressing MPO and gp91phox, an oxidative enzyme associated with their reactivity, as early as 0-4 hours post-injury. At later time points, some macrophages and activated microglia also start expressing gp91phox but none of the foamy macrophages or microglia express defensin.

MMP expression in the lesion center shows strong neutrophil immunoreactivity for MMP-9 at 0–4 h after injury, with almost all neutrophils expressing this protein at 1–3 days post-injury, but the proportion declines at 5–10 days. In contrast, neither ramified nor activated microglia were MMP-9 immunoreactive at any time point assessed after injury.

Macrophages play a significant role in tissue clearing and express MPO, defensin, and CD68 to varying degrees. The inflammatory response persists for weeks to months post-injury with the intensity varying among cases. Some cases show intense CD68 immunoreactivity weeks to months post-injury.

The expression pattern of these enzymes at the margins of the lesion is similar but less abundant due to the smaller number of inflammatory cells outside the lesion epicenter, as the inflammation wanes with distance from the injury epicenter. The pathology in the spinal cords also diminishes accordingly. The study suggests a complex immune response to SCI that could have implications for therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting recovery after spinal cord injuries.

DISCUSSION

The scientific discussion summarizes the inflammatory response and histopathological appearance in human spinal cord injury (SCI). Neutrophils dominate the scene within three days of SCI at the lesion epicenter, while macrophages are present in necrotic, preserved, and partially damaged tissue. The role of these cells in recovery or damage expansion is uncertain due to technical challenges. Lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ cells, appear weeks to months post-injury, with unclear impacts on secondary degenerative processes, wound healing, and repair after SCI.

The study focuses on CD4+ lymphocytes, microglia, and Matrix Metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) in human SCI. Neutrophils are the primary source of MMP-9, contributing to early tissue damage, while MMP-9 may also play a role in chronic phase extracellular matrix remodeling. Limitations include prolonged storage and fixation of archived tissues potentially underestimating CD4+ lymphocytes and microglia due to artefactual loss.

The human SCI general pathology is similar to that of experimental animals, with differences in fibroblast-like cells at the mouse lesion site. The inflammatory response, neutrophil infiltration, microglial and macrophage reactions, and lymphocyte infiltration are generally similar among humans, rats, and mice.

The study supports the notion that limiting neutrophil entry within the first 3 days post-injury is crucial. The similarities between human and rat SCI suggest potential benefits from treatments like anti-CD11d monoclonal antibody, which improves neurological outcome in rats by blocking neutrophil entry, as a clinical neuroprotective therapy.
