Final Summary

Title: The cellular inflammatory response in human spinal cords after injury

In this study, Fleming et al. (2006) explored the time course and distribution of pathological changes, inflammatory cells, and microglial activation in injured spinal cords from patients who survived up to 1 year after spinal cord injury (SCI). The research aimed to understand the immune response and oxidative enzymes in SCI, focusing on neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, lymphocytes, MPO, gp91phox, and MMP-9.

The authors used digital imaging and software to quantify the area of necrosis at the lesion epicenter, dividing the spinal cord cross-section into distinct areas and scoring the overall injury in each area based on severity. They found that SCI causes zones of pathological change, including areas of inflammation, necrosis, and cystic cavities in the acute cases. Zone 1 increased in size with time after injury while the overall injury size remained relatively constant.

Immunolabelling was used to quantify neutrophils, microglia/macrophages (CD68), and MPO, with neutrophils being most numerous 1-3 days after SCI and detectable for up to 10 days. Activated microglia, a few monocytes/macrophages, and phagocytic macrophages were present for weeks to months after SCI. The data suggests that anti-inflammatory strategies should be directed at preventing early neutrophil influx and modifying microglial activation to mitigate potential damage caused by these cells in the first few days following SCI.

The study highlights the expression of oxidative and proteolytic enzymes in cells following SCI, the inflammatory response, and pathology in the spinal cord after injury. Neutrophils express gp91phox and MMP-9 at different time points after SCI, while activated microglia express gp91phox. CD8+ and CD4+ cells infiltrate the injured spinal cord weeks after injury. The study emphasizes the need for limiting neutrophil entry, particularly within the first 3 days post-injury, as a potential neuroprotective therapy. By 5 days after SCI, activated microglia and macrophages dominate, with concern for their capacity to produce damaging enzymes when interventions are made. The study also underscores similarities between human and rodent inflammatory responses after SCI, facilitating the translation of animal treatment developments to clinical use.

INTRODUCTION

The study presented in this scientific paper focuses on understanding the inflammatory response in the spinal cord after injury, potentially leading to new treatments for spinal cord injuries. The research investigates cellular and enzymatic changes following contusion injuries, labeling specific cells (neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, lymphocytes) and enzymes (MPO, gp91phox, MMP-9).

The authors developed a method to quantify the area of necrosis at the lesion epicenter using digital imaging and software. They divided the spinal cord cross-section into distinct areas and scored the overall injury in each area based on severity. The study also assessed cystic cavities, edema, and axonal swelling without finding a preference for these injuries in terms of severity or location within eight grey or white matter regions.

The study quantified immunolabelling of neutrophils, microglia/macrophages (CD68), and MPO using digital images processed with software, calculating the area represented by one pixel in mm2 and applying a color-based threshold detection function for analysis. The overall injury score is defined as Zones 1 and 2 in the Results section.

BACKGROUND

The paper quantifies the inflammatory response in the spinal cord following injury by analyzing areas of immunoreactivity for defensin, a neutrophil marker, and CD68, a macrophage/microglia marker. The method involves selecting areas of immunoprecipitate of intensity greater than background and marking them with a red mask, excluding the pia. The extent of immunoreactivity reflects the number and size of cells involved in the inflammatory response. CD8+cells were counted instead of measured due to their scarcity.

The paper also examines the expression of MPO, gp91phox (markers of oxidative reactivity), and MMP-9 (a pro-inflammatory protease) at different time intervals after injury. Cellular morphology and serial sections stained for defensin and CD68 were used to identify the cell populations with immunoreactivity for these markers.

Statistical analysis involved parametric statistical analysis, Fisher's LSD protected t-test, one-tailed Student’s t-test, and Spearman’s correlation analysis to determine significant differences (P<0.05).

RESULTS

The study by Fleming et al. (2006) investigates the immune response and oxidative enzymes in spinal cord injuries (SCI). After SCI, neutrophils express gp91phox and MMP-9 within 1-3 days, which decreases at 5-10 days. Activated microglia express gp91phox at 5-10 days and weeks to months after injury. Phagocytic macrophages do not express gp91phox or MMP-9.

Neutrophils are strongly MMP-9-ir at 1–3 days, while microglia and macrophages do not express MMP-9. At 5–10 days after injury, only a few scattered neutrophils express MMP-9. CD8+ and CD4+ cells infiltrate the injured spinal cord weeks after SCI, being distributed among macrophages and adjacent to blood vessels, with the average number of CD8+ lymphocytes per field increasing over time, peaking at 5 days after injury.

The study highlights the expression of oxidative and proteolytic enzymes in cells following SCI, the inflammatory response, and pathology in the spinal cord after injury. Neutrophils express gp91phox and MMP-9 at different time points after SCI, while activated microglia express gp91phox. CD8+ and CD4+ cells infiltrate the injured spinal cord weeks after injury.

DISCUSSION

The scientific discussion summarized focuses on the inflammatory response and histopathological appearance in the spinal cord after injury in humans. Neutrophils and macrophages are significant players in this process, with neutrophils dominating immediately after injury but decreasing over time, while macrophages increase. Activated microglia and macrophages have a ramified morphology and can either promote neurological recovery or contribute to tissue damage. Lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ T-cells, are present among macrophages, though their role in secondary degenerative processes and wound healing remains unclear.

The study also examines the prevalence of CD4+ lymphocytes and MMP-9 expression in human spinal cord injury (SCI) tissues compared to control appendix sections, finding that prolonged storage may have underestimated CD4+ lymphocyte presence due to artefactual loss. MMP-9, which degrades components of the blood–CNS barrier and contributes to demyelination, is produced by neutrophils after SCI in humans, contributing to early secondary tissue damage.

The inflammatory response's complexity is highlighted due to factors associated with human tissue use, such as unknown injury severity, cardiovascular and thermoregulatory status, drugs taken prior to trauma, etc. The study emphasizes the need for limiting neutrophil entry, particularly within the first 3 days post-injury, as a potential neuroprotective therapy. By 5 days after SCI, activated microglia and macrophages dominate, with concern for their capacity to produce damaging enzymes when interventions are made. The study also underscores similarities between human and rodent inflammatory responses after SCI, facilitating the translation of animal treatment developments to clinical use.
