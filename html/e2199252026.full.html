Final Summary

Title: Opposing Control of Respiratory Brainstem on Multiple Timescales via Noradrenaline and Glutamate Release from the Locus Ceruleus

Research Question: How do Locus Ceruleus (LC) neurons exert flexible, opposing effects on the key respiratory brainstem region, Kölliker–Fuse nucleus (KF), via glutamatergic and noradrenergic signaling?

Methods: Combining in vitro and in vivo experiments utilizing mice for in vivo studies and brain slices for in vitro research. Immunohistochemistry, whole-cell recordings, RNAscope, optogenetics with ChR2 protein were employed to investigate glutamate co-release from LC neurons and its impact on KF neurons. Optical stimulation induced firing in eYFP-expressing LC neurons, and drugs were used according to manufacturers' directions for specific experiments.

Key Findings: The study confirms the co-release of glutamate and norepinephrine from LC to KF, suggesting a multiplexing of opposing signals on multiple timescales. This co-release results in fast glutamatergic excitation, slow noradrenergic excitation, and inhibition of KF neurons.

Conclusions: The research uncovers a novel pathway between LC neurons and the key respiratory nucleus KF, which could significantly influence future models of neural control by the LC. Understanding this dual transmitter system may provide insights into various cognitive and homeostatic functions, particularly in neurodegenerative diseases and neuropsychiatric disorders where LC integrity is compromised.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a scientific paper titled "Opposing Control of the Respiratory Brainstem on Multiple Timescales Achieved by Noradrenaline and Glutamate Release from the Locus Ceruleus," researchers from various institutions reveal that the locus ceruleus (LC) in adult mice sends projections to the Kölliker–Fuse nucleus (KF), a crucial respiratory brainstem region. The study demonstrates that long-duration activation of LC neuron terminals induces persistent inhibitory and excitatory noradrenergic currents or increases firing rate in postsynaptic KF neurons. Short stimulation leads to the rapid release of glutamate, which may co-occur with slower noradrenergic transmission in a monosynaptic circuit. This research suggests that LC neurons can exert flexible, opposing effects on different timescales via glutamatergic and noradrenergic signaling onto the KF, providing a mechanism for dynamic adjustment of breathing during various behavioral states or metabolic demand. Keywords associated with this study include arousal circuitry, breathing, co-transmission, glutamate, Kölliker–Fuse nucleus, noradrenaline/norepinephrine, pons, and vesicular glutamate transporter 2. The significance of the findings lies in the implications for respiratory disorders where LC function is compromised and offers a conserved circuit model for studies on the state-dependent control of breathing.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper presents a research protocol investigating glutamate co-release from Locus Ceruleus (LC) neurons, known for their noradrenergic function. The study combines in vitro and in vivo experiments, using mice for in vivo and brain slices for in vitro studies.

In vivo, the research employs immunohistochemistry to examine the overlap between TH+ and labeled LC neurons projecting to the key area of interest (KF). In vitro, the study uses whole-cell recordings on transfected LC cells to investigate the effect of glutamate receptor antagonists on these neurons. Additionally, RNAscope is used for studying potential co-expression of VGluT2 and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH).

Using optogenetics, the researchers stimulated LC neurons with blue light via ChR2, a light-sensitive protein, confirming efficient expression through voltage-clamp and current-clamp recordings. Optical stimulation induced firing in eYFP-expressing LC neurons but not in controls.

The study found that 5 ms light pulses induced glutamate release, as evidenced by optically evoked postsynaptic currents (oEPSCs) in KF neurons. The amplitude of persistent currents was similar regardless of pulse train or continuous light, allowing pooled data analysis.

The paper also details the firing patterns induced by optical stimulation, measuring latency to first action potential, classifying firing patterns as tonic or bursting, and calculating firing rates. Various drugs were used for specific experiments according to manufacturers' directions, with statistical analyses conducted in GraphPad Prism version 10, and error bars representing standard error of mean (SEM) unless otherwise stated.

Overall, the research aims to understand the role of glutamate co-release from LC neurons in various brain functions.

RESULTS

The study explores the co-release of glutamate and norepinephrine (NA) from Locus Ceruleus (LC) neurons, impacting Kolliker-Fuse (KF) neurons, a key breathing center in the brainstem. Light stimulation induced both glutamatergic oEPSCs and inhibitory α2 receptor currents in the KF. The α2 autoreceptors were found to inhibit glutamate release from LC terminals, while α2-antagonists restored the amplitude of oEPSCs to baseline. This research confirms the co-release of glutamate and NA from LC to KF, suggesting a multiplexing of opposing signals on multiple timescales from LC–NA neurons onto their target area. These findings could significantly influence future models of neural control by the LC.

DISCUSSION

The LC (locus coeruleus) regulates various cognitive and homeostatic functions, potentially serving as a gateway between respiratory and higher-order brain regions. While much is known about the LC's contributions to respiratory control, its connections with respiratory nuclei are understudied.

A novel pathway between LC neurons and the key respiratory nucleus KF has been identified. This synapse releases both glutamate and norepinephrine (NA), providing fast glutamatergic excitation, slow NAergic excitation, and inhibition of KF neurons. This circuit is a prime candidate for integrating emotional, cognitive, and state-dependent information to flexibly modulate brainstem breathing control.

The study found that while some glutamatergic responses could originate from non-NAergic LC/peri-LC neurons, the majority of evidence points to co-release from NAergic LC neurons as the predominant mechanism. This dual transmitter system enables context-dependent breathing adjustments by switching between rapid (glutamate-dominated) and sustained (NA-dominated) modes depending on firing patterns.

The findings have implications for understanding LC modulation of diverse functions beyond breathing, suggesting dysregulation of this multitransmitter circuit may contribute to symptoms in neurodegenerative diseases and neuropsychiatric disorders where LC integrity is compromised.
