Respiratory-related evoked potential mea-sures of respiratory sensory gating

Chan, Davenport | 2008

Keywords: respiratory sensation, inspiratory occlusion, cerebral cortex, mechanosensation

Final Summary

This scientific study investigates the principle of sensory gating in respiratory sensation using paired occlusion-elicited RREPs (Respiratory-related Event-Related Potentials). The research involved 20 subjects who participated in three types of trials: paired RREP, paired air puff MEP (Motor Evoked Potentials), and paired SEP (Somatosensory Evoked Potentials) trials.

The study aimed to determine if the S2 peak amplitude of the RREP P1 (Positive 1) and N1 (Negative 1) peaks in a paired occlusion paradigm would be less than the S1 amplitude, as well as whether the S2/S1 ratio of the RREP would be similar to the SEP ratio, with both being 0.5 or less.

In Protocol 1, both S1 and S2 occlusions within the same inspiratory phase produced similar changes in mouth pressure, but the S2 amplitudes were significantly reduced compared to S1 for all peaks (Nf, P1, N1) in the respective channels. In Protocol 2, early and late inspiratory obstruction elicited RREP showed similar maximal Pm change, latencies, and peak amplitudes for S1, S2, and single late inspiratory occlusions, but the amplitudes for S1 and late single inspiratory occlusions were significantly greater than S2 for all peaks (Nf, P1, N1) in the respective channels.

The study confirms the presence of temporal sensory gating for respiratory mechanoreceptor stimuli (RREP), somatosensory stimuli via skin air puffs (SEP), and auditory stimuli, indicating that the brain suppresses or reduces responses to redundant stimuli. Potential neural structures involved include the thalamus and limbic system (amyggdala and hippocampus).

Reduced sensory gating, or increased S2/S1 ratio, could potentially serve as a marker for respiratory-related anxiety disorders in asthma patients, requiring further investigation. The study suggests altered central neural processing of respiratory information might explain the absence of RREP P1 peak in severe asthma cases.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper presents a study investigating respiratory sensory gating using paired occlusion-elicited RREPs (Respiratory-related Event-Related Potentials) to determine if the principles of sensory gating observed in somatosensory stimulation also apply to respiratory sensation. The experiment involved 20 subjects who were prescreened for lung health and instructed to sit comfortably while wearing an EEG cap, breathing through a nonrebreathing valve connected to an occlusion valve.

The study included three types of trials: paired RREP trial, paired air puff MEP (Motor Evoked Potentials) trial, and paired SEP (Somatosensory Evoked Potentials) trial. The subjects participated in the RREP trial first, followed by a randomized sequence of MEP and SEP trials.

In each trial, an occlusion valve was used to momentarily obstruct the subject's airflow for 150 ms with an interstimulus interval (ISI) of 500 ms. The RREP trial consisted of paired occlusions, while the MEP and SEP trials involved alternate presentation of air puffs or tactile stimuli on the skin.

The study aimed to investigate whether the S2 peak amplitude of the RREP P1 (Positive 1) and N1 (Negative 1) peaks in a paired occlusion paradigm would be less than the S1 amplitude, as well as whether the S2/S1 ratio of the RREP would be similar to the SEP ratio, with both being 0.5 or less.

Data analysis involved measuring peak latencies and amplitudes for each component identified based on previous reports for peak localization, with differences between S1 (first stimulus) and S2 (second stimulus) compared for each peak independently for the RREP, MEP, and SEP using repeated measures one-way ANOVA (RMANOVA).

Additionally, Protocol 2 aimed to compare paired occlusions with single occlusions presented during late inspiration to test if the RREP elicited by single occlusions at the onset of inspiration would be significantly different from those in late inspiration. The setup and data analysis methods were similar to those used for the initial RREP recordings.

The study aimed to investigate respiratory sensory gating using paired occlusion-elicited RREPs, MEP (Motor Evoked Potentials), and SEP (Somatosensory Evoked Potentials) across multiple trials to compare responses to paired stimuli and single stimuli.

RESULTS

The study employed two protocols to examine sensory gating using RREP (Resting Motor Evoked Potential), SEP (Somatosensory Evoked Potential), and MEP (Motor Evoked Potential).

In Protocol 1, occlusion-elicited RREP, air puff MEP, and SEP were measured. Both S1 and S2 occlusions within the same inspiratory phase produced similar changes in mouth pressure with no significant difference. The S1 occlusion elicited peaks Nf, P1, and N1 in the frontal, central, and vertex regions, while the S2 occlusion produced the same peaks but with significantly reduced amplitudes.

In Protocol 1, an air puff presented to the buccal surface or hand also produced similar peaks (P50 and N100) bilaterally and at the vertex, with reduced amplitudes for S2 compared to S1.

The S2/S1 ratios for Nf, P1, and N1 peaks were significantly less for Nf but not significantly different from P1 for RREP, MEP, and SEP. No significant difference was found between the S2/S1 ratio for the N1 and N100 peaks of the three methods.

In Protocol 2, early and late inspiratory obstruction elicited RREP. Maximal Pm change, latencies, and peak amplitudes were similar for S1, S2, and single late inspiratory occlusions, but the amplitudes for S1 and late single inspiratory occlusions were significantly greater than S2 for all peaks (Nf, P1, N1) in the respective channels.

DISCUSSION

The study investigates central neural gating of various sensory stimuli, including respiratory mechanoreceptor stimuli (RREP), somatosensory stimuli via skin air puffs (SEP), and auditory stimuli. Utilizing paired stimulus paradigms, the research confirms the presence of temporal sensory gating for all modalities, indicating that the brain suppresses or reduces responses to redundant stimuli. Potential neural structures involved include the thalamus and limbic system (amyggdala and hippocampus).

The study focuses on the temporal phase of central neural respiratory gating, finding alterations in stimulus frequency may impact peak amplitudes of paired evoked potentials in respiratory sensation. Excessive respiratory gating could explain the absence of RREP P1 peak in severe asthma cases, suggesting altered central neural processing of respiratory information.

Reduced sensory gating, or increased S2/S1 ratio, has been linked to sensory flooding and anxiety disorders, and an increased ratio for RREP P1 and N1 peaks could potentially serve as a marker for respiratory-related anxiety disorders in asthma patients, requiring further investigation.
