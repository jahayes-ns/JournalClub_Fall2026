Respiratory-related evoked potential measures of respiratory sensory gating

Chan, Davenport | 2008

Keywords: respiratory sensation, inspiratory occlusion, cerebral cortex, mechanosensation

Final Summary

This scientific research article investigates respiratory sensory gating through Respiratory Electromyography (RREPs), aiming to determine if the S2 peak amplitude of the RREP P1, and N1 peak in a paired occlusion paradigm will be less than the S1 amplitude, and whether the S2/S1 ratio of the RREP will be similar to the Somatosensory Evoked Potential (SEP).

The study involved 20 subjects who underwent three experimental protocols: Respiratory-Related Event-Related Potentials (RREP), Motor Evoked Potentials (MEP), and Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SEP). The results show that S2 peak amplitudes were significantly less than S1 in both RREPs and auditory stimuli, suggesting an inhibitory control at the subcortical level. However, no significant differences were found between S1 and S2 occlusions in terms of mouth pressure change or maximal Pm change.

The paper concludes that mechanosensory information is relayed to various parts of the brain, including the cortex, and that emotional status and habituation can modulate sensory gating. Altered S2/S1 ratios in respiratory sensation deficits could explain why some children with severe asthma lack the RREP P1 peak, indicating altered central neural processing of respiratory sensory information in this subgroup. The paper also suggests that excessive respiratory gating might cause a lack of "gating in" of respiratory-related sensory information in children with life-threatening asthma, and an increased S2/S1 ratio for the RREP P1 and N1 peaks could serve as a marker for respiratory-related anxiety disorders, requiring further investigation.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study presents a scientific research article focusing on investigating respiratory sensory gating through Respiratory Electromyography (RREPs). The primary aim is to determine if the S2 peak amplitude of the RREP P1, and N1 peak in a paired occlusion paradigm will be less than the S1 amplitude. Additionally, it hypothesizes that the S2/S1 ratio of the RREP will be similar to the Somatosensory Evoked Potential (SEP).

The study involved 20 subjects who underwent three experimental protocols: Respiratory-Related Event-Related Potentials (RREP), Motor Evoked Potentials (MEP), and Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SEP). In the RREP trial, paired occlusions were introduced during inspiration for 100 pairs over approximately 40-60 minutes. The MEP trial involved positive-pressure air puffs delivered to the buccal surface of the cheeks for 256 pairs, while the SEP trial used air puffs on the subject's hand for 512 pairs.

Protocol 2 compared RREPs elicited by paired occlusions and single occlusions applied at the same inspiratory times, testing whether the RREP elicited by single occlusions presented at the onset of inspiration would be significantly different from those presented in the late inspiratory phase.

Data analysis for all trials involved measuring peak latencies and amplitudes based on specific electrodes and timeframes, with statistical comparisons between S1 (first stimulus) and S2 (second stimulus) for each peak. The significance level was set at P < 0.05. This research aims to contribute to understanding sensory processing in the central nervous system by investigating if respiratory sensation follows similar pathways to somatosensory sensation in terms of sensory gating. Further analysis and interpretation of the data are required to draw conclusions from this study.

RESULTS

The study presents results from two protocols investigating sensorimotor gating. In Protocol 1, occlusion-elicited RREP, air puff MEP, and SEP were compared. No significant difference was found in mouth pressure change between S1 and S2 occlusions. Both S1 and S2 occlusions elicited Nf, P1, and N1 peaks, with S2 peak amplitudes being significantly less than S1, particularly for the N1 peak. The S2/S1 ratio for N1 was significantly less than Nf but not significantly different from P1. For air puff stimuli, both S1 and S2 elicited P50 and N100 peaks, with reduced amplitudes in S2 compared to S1. Lastly, the S1 air puff presented to the hand resulted in similar P50 and N100 peaks, with reduced amplitudes in S2.

In Protocol 2, early and late inspiratory obstruction elicited RREP. No significant differences were found in the maximal Pm change or peak latencies between S1, S2, and single late inspiratory occlusions. However, S1 and late single inspiratory occlusions had significantly greater peak amplitudes than S2 for Nf, P1, and N1 peaks.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific paper focuses on sensory gating in various modalities, particularly investigating temporal sensory gating in respiratory, somatosensory, and auditory stimuli. The reduction in response amplitude to the second stimulus in a paired stimulation paradigm suggests an inhibitory control at the subcortical level, rather than cortical inhibition.

The study also explores potential neural structures involved in sensory gating, such as the thalamus and limbic system, although a specific area is not definitively identified. The paper concludes that mechanosensory information is relayed to various parts of the brain, including the cortex, and that emotional status and habituation can modulate sensory gating.

The discussion section of the paper suggests that both frequency and intensity may influence sensory gating, with the current study focusing on frequency. The study proposes that altered S2/S1 ratios in respiratory sensation deficits could explain why some children with severe asthma lack the RREP P1 peak, indicating altered central neural processing of respiratory sensory information in this subgroup.

The paper also suggests that excessive respiratory gating might cause a lack of "gating in" of respiratory-related sensory information in children with life-threatening asthma.
