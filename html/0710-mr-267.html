MINIREVIEW Intermittent Hypoxia: Cause of or Therapy for Systemic Hypertension?

Serebrovskaya, Manukhina, Smith, Downey, Mallet | 2008

Keywords: angiogenesis, hypertension, intermittent hypoxia, nitric oxide, obstructive sleep apnea

Final Summary

The mini-review explores the contrasting effects of Intermittent Hypoxia (IH) in Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and as a therapeutic approach for systemic hypertension. OSA, prevalent in a significant portion of the American population, is associated with hypertension due to recurring hypoxia and hypercapnia episodes stimulating persistent sympathetic activity. On the contrary, controlled IH conditioning programs show promise as safe and effective treatments for hypertension in both animals and humans.

The review discusses the potential mechanisms behind both the pro- and antihypertensive effects of IH, including angiogenesis, nitric oxide production, and reactive oxygen species. It highlights research primarily from former Soviet Union countries where IH conditioning programs are used therapeutically for hypertension treatment. The review suggests that IH therapy reduces sympathetic nervous system activation, inflammation, and vasoconstriction compared to OSA, promoting endothelium-dependent relaxation and preventing endothelial dysfunction in hypertensive rats. The article was published in Exp Biol Med 233:627–650, 2008.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This mini-review focuses on the contrasting effects of Intermittent Hypoxia (IH) in conditions like Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and as a therapeutic approach for systemic hypertension. OSA, prevalent in 15-25% of American men and 5-10% of women, is associated with hypertension due to repeated hypoxia and hypercapnia episodes stimulating persistent sympathetic activity.

On the other hand, controlled IH conditioning programs have shown promise as safe and effective treatments for hypertension in both animals and humans. This review aims to explain the disparities between the hypertensive effects of OSA and the antihypertensive effects of IH therapy.

The article highlights research, particularly from former Soviet Union countries, where IH conditioning programs are used therapeutically for hypertension treatment. It also discusses potential mechanisms behind both the pro- and anti-hypertensive effects of IH, including angiogenesis, nitric oxide production, and reactive oxygen species. The review is published in Exp Biol Med 233:627–650, 2008.

INTRODUCTION

The text discusses the connection between Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and hypertension, as well as the potential use of intermittent hypoxia as a therapy for hypertension.

OSA is a condition in which airways become blocked during sleep, leading to interrupted breathing and decreased oxygen levels. This repeated arousal from sleep can cause chronic increases in blood pressure independent of hypoxemia. Studies have shown a dose-response relationship between OSA and the incidence of hypertension, with more severe cases of OSA being associated with higher blood pressure levels.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is effective in reducing blood pressure in OSA patients. Intermittent hypoxia has been suggested as a potential therapy for hypertension, although much of the research on this topic has been published in Russian and Ukrainian journals and is difficult to access by Western readers. Some findings from this research suggest that intermittent hypoxia conditioning protocols can increase exercise tolerance, hypoxic ventilatory response, hematocrit, and blood hemoglobin levels.

The text also highlights the differences between OSA and Intermittent Hypoxia Treatment (IHT) in relation to blood pressure regulation:

  1. Duration and frequency of hypoxic episodes: OSA involves brief, recurrent cycles of hypoxia-reoxygenation that last less than 60 seconds, while IHT uses longer periods of several minutes to hours.
  2. Oxidative stress: OSA causes intense oxidative stress, contributing to hypertension by activating the sympathetic nervous system, inducing endothelial dysfunction, and increasing circulating activity of the vasoconstrictor endothelin-1. In contrast, IHT generates moderate amounts of ROS that function as signaling molecules, contributing to cardio- and cerebroprotective adaptations.
  3. Sympathetic nervous system activation: OSA activates the sympathetic nervous system due to stress from asphyxia, CO2 accumulation, H+ ions, and lack of restful sleep, leading to increased blood pressure. IHT enhances parasympathetic tone and reduces sympathetic activity.
  4. Inflammation: OSA is pro-inflammatory, causing hypertension by damaging vascular endothelium. IHT does not seem to have the same inflammatory effects.
  5. Effects on blood vessels: OSA causes vasoconstriction and long-term facilitation of the carotid chemoreflex, which increases tonic sympathetic activity. IHT promotes endothelium-dependent relaxation and prevents endothelial dysfunction in hypertensive rats.
  6. Formation of NO stores: IHT stimulates the formation of NO stores, contributing to adaptive responses of the circulation and protecting against harmful effects of both excessive NO synthesized during repeated exposure to hypoxia and decreased production of NO by endothelial cells.

The text also includes references to various research studies related to intermittent hypoxia, its effects on various physiological systems, particularly in relation to hypertension, nitric oxide, and erythropoietin.
