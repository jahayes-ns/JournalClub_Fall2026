Final Summary

The mini-review explores the conflicting effects of Intermittent Hypoxia (IH) on systemic hypertension, with a particular focus on its connection to Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA). The research question revolves around whether IH can be used as a potential treatment for hypertension due to its ability to lower blood pressure in individuals with the condition.

The study, primarily conducted in the former Soviet Union, employed various experimental models of hypertension in rats and found that adaptation to moderate IH regimens could prevent endothelial dysfunction and high blood pressure. Mechanisms behind these effects include enhanced sarcoplasmic reticular (SR) Ca2+ sequestration, increased resistance of Na+ - K+ ATPase to oxidative stress, potentiation of antioxidant defenses, reduction in microvascular rarefaction, and adjustments in water and salt metabolism.

Intermittent Hypoxia Treatment (IHT) differs from OSA in that it uses longer periods of several minutes to hours, causing systemic hypoxia which activates ventilation, resulting in hypocapnea and alkalemia. Unlike OSA, IHT enhances parasympathetic tone and decreases blood pressure.

Key findings suggest the potential benefits of IH over traditional drug therapies due to its minimal side effects and possible stimulation of an organism's general resistance. However, further studies are needed to determine safe and effective protocols for its application. The paper also provides a list of references related to nitric oxide, intermittent hypoxia, and cardiovascular physiology.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This mini-review focuses on the conflicting effects of intermittent hypoxia on systemic hypertension. Acute hypoxia increases heart rate, cardiac output, and blood pressure, but different intermittent hypoxia paradigms can result in either hypertension (as in Obstructive Sleep Apnea) or a decrease in blood pressure (therapeutic hypoxia). The article emphasizes research conducted in the former Soviet Union, where intermittent hypoxia is used to treat hypertension. The review discusses mechanisms behind both pro- and anti-hypertensive effects of intermittent hypoxia, including angiogenesis, nitric oxide production, and reactive oxygen species.

INTRODUCTION

The article discusses the effects of Intermittent Hypoxia (IH) on hypertension, with a focus on its connection to Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA). IH involves periods of low oxygen levels followed by normal oxygen levels, which can potentially lower blood pressure in individuals with hypertension.

The research suggests that adaptation to moderate IH regimens can prevent the development of endothelial dysfunction and high blood pressure in rats with various forms of experimental hypertension. The mechanisms behind these effects include enhanced sarcoplasmic reticular (SR) Ca2+ sequestration, increased resistance of Na+ - K+ ATPase to oxidative stress, potentiation of antioxidant defenses, reduction in microvascular rarefaction, and adjustments in water and salt metabolism.

Intermittent Hypoxia Treatment (IHT) differs from OSA in that it uses longer periods of several minutes to hours, causing systemic hypoxia which activates ventilation and results in hypocapnea and alkalemia. IHT also enhances parasympathetic tone and decreases blood pressure, unlike OSA which causes intense oxidative stress, sympathetic nervous system activation, pro-inflammation, and increased blood pressure.

The research highlights the potential benefits of IH over traditional drug therapies as it may not have negative side effects commonly associated with those drugs and may stimulate an organism's general resistance. However, further studies are needed to determine safe and effective protocols for its application.
