Final Summary

This research paper, titled "Clinical Application of Near-Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury," explores the feasibility and potential benefits of using near-infrared light-Oxygen saturation (NIR-SO2) monitoring for cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) patients undergoing surgery. The study involved 37 patients, ages ranging from 18 to 90 years, who had sustained CSCI due to various reasons like falls and road traffic accidents.

The researchers used NIR-SO2 technology to detect regional SO2 under lightproof conditions during the operation, finding an improvement in regional SO2 after laminectomy and durotomy, indicating better spinal cord blood supply. The study concluded that NIR-SO2 technology is safe, feasible, and can directly reflect improvements in spinal cord blood supply.

The research provides preliminary evidence for the potential use of NIR-SO2 as a clinical tool to monitor and predict outcomes in CSCI patients undergoing surgical interventions like laminectomy and durotomy. The authors suggest that this technology could be valuable in checking blood flow in CSCI patients, ensuring the effectiveness of surgical procedures, and predicting prognosis.

Title: Clinical Application of Near-Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

Journal: Research Article, Volume 6 • Issue 4, 100

Authors: Jamal Alshorman, Ruba Altahla, Gao Yana, Liu Xiangyan

The study explores the clinical application of near-infrared blood oxygen monitoring (NIBOM) in patients with cervical spinal cord injury. The research was conducted on a group of patients who sustained cervical spinal cord injuries and were treated with surgical interventions followed by intensive care.

The team used NIBOM to monitor the regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) in the injured spinal cord tissue. The data collected showed significant correlations between rSO2 levels and clinical outcomes, suggesting that NIBOM could potentially predict recovery and prognosis in cervical spinal cord injury patients.

The research methodology involved non-invasive NIBOM measurements at regular intervals during the hospital stay of the patients. The findings provide preliminary evidence for the potential use of NIBOM as a clinical tool to monitor and predict outcomes in cervical spinal cord injury patients.

ABSTRACT

Title: Clinical Application of Near Infrared Blood Oxygen Monitoring in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

This scientific study investigates the feasibility of using NIR-SO2 (Near infrared light-Oxygen saturation) monitoring for cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI). The research was conducted on 37 patients with ages ranging from 18 to 90 years, who underwent posterior approach surgery for CSCI.

The NIR-SO2 technology was used to detect regional SO2 under lightproof conditions during the operation. The results showed an improvement in regional SO2 after laminectomy and durotomy, indicating better spinal cord blood supply. The study concluded that NIR-SO2 technology is safe, feasible, and can directly reflect improvements in spinal cord blood supply.

The study suggests that this technology can monitor the regional SO2 of CSCI patients and assess spinal cord perfusion and compression in real-time during surgery. The affiliations of the authors are from the Department of Orthopedics, The Second Affiliated Hospital, Hubei University of Science and Technology, Xiangning 437100, China, and the Department of Rehabilitation, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan 430030, China.

The article was published in the Journal of Spine Research and Surgery in December 2024. The corresponding author is Jamal Alshorman from the Orthopedics department of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Clinical Medical College, Hubei University of Science and Technology, Xianning 437100, Hubei, China. Keywords associated with this study are Near-infrared light, Blood oxygen saturation, Cervical spinal cord injury, Laminectomy, Durotomy.

INTRODUCTION

The paper discusses the challenges in diagnosing and treating Cervical Spinal Cord Injury (CSCI), emphasizing the need for early and adequate decompression. However, there's no standard on whether further spinal canal decompression or durotomy is necessary after fracture reduction and internal fixation.

The authors highlight the importance of assessing spinal cord blood perfusion, ischemia, and hypoxia to determine the degree of CSCI, guide treatment choices, and evaluate outcomes. The paper suggests that current methods for decompression decision-making, such as preoperative signs, CT/MRI, intraoperative dural pulsation, and the use of invasive pressure measurements, have limitations.

The authors propose Near Infrared (NIR) blood oxygen monitoring as a potential solution. NIR is non-destructive, continuous, painless, cost-effective, and can be used on both adults and children. It has been used to monitor cerebral perfusion status but its application in guiding treatment for CSCI remains unexplored.

The paper concludes by stating that NIR could be a valuable tool for checking blood flow in CSCI patients, ensuring the effectiveness of surgical procedures, and predicting prognosis. The authors suggest that finding a safe and simple technique to monitor real-time blood flow during surgery is crucial for accurate prognosis and avoiding complications related to durotomies. The paper poses three key clinical questions: how to improve blood flow, whether laminectomy or durotomy should be performed, and how to monitor blood flow at the injury site.

METHOD

In the study by Alshorman et al., the Non-Invasive Real-Time Near-Infrared (NIR) SpO2 measurement technique was utilized to evaluate spinal cord blood perfusion and determine whether decompression after laminectomy was complete or if further durotomy with duroplasty was necessary. Patients with traumatic CSCI, fracture and dislocation, edema, hematoma, contusion, and ASIA grades A, B, and C were included, while those without these conditions were excluded.

After laminectomy, the dura pulse and darkening (hematoma beneath the dura) were observed under direct vision to confirm NIR advantages. An incision of about 3 mm was made when necessary to confirm hematoma presence in thick dures. The dura was opened if no dural pulsation was seen, or if spinal cord surface hematoma was present.

The data analysis involved IBM SPSS version 22 and t-test for comparison of patients' age, gender, follow-up time, initial and final ASIA, sensory and motor functions, SO2 values, and operation time. NIR-SO2 monitoring detects blood and brain oxygen levels in the range of 700~900 nm, with acceptable tissue penetrability for detection of local tissue SO2, regional hemoglobin, oxyhemoglobin concentration, and total hemoglobin concentration.

Based on NIR-SO2 values, no dural decompression is required if the spinal cord blood SO2 level is between 58%~68%, while durotomy with duroplasty is required if SO2 <58%. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Medical College of Hubei University of Science and Technology.

RESULTS

The study, published in the Journal of Spine Research and Surgery in 2024, involved 37 patients with cervical spinal cord injuries. Of these, 23 (62.2%) were male, and 14 (37.8%) were female, with an average age of 51.4±9.7 years. The most common mechanisms of injury were falls (46.0%), followed by road traffic accidents (RTA) (40.5%), and falling objects (13.5%).

The patients underwent laminectomy, with some requiring durotomy due to low blood oxygen saturation (SO2) levels. The SO2 before laminectomy was 53.7±1.6%, which increased to 65.1±1.6% after the procedure in 22 patients (59.5%). However, 15 patients (40.5%) still had SO2 levels below 50% and required durotomy, with the SO2 level improving to 62.7±3.1% post-durotomy.

Preoperatively, 16 patients had ASIA grade A, 14 had B, and 7 had C. After an average follow-up of 21 months, the ASIA grades changed for various patients: one patient with grade A improved to grade B, five moved from grade A to C, ten remained the same, fourteen with grade B improved to grade C in four and D in seven, while two remained in grade B.

The study demonstrates the potential utility of near-infrared blood oxygen monitoring in cervical spinal cord injury cases for guiding surgical interventions and improving patient outcomes.

DISCUSSION

The study discusses the application of Near Infrared Spectroscopy (NIR-SO2) for detecting blood flow during cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) surgeries, specifically during laminectomy procedures. The aim is to determine if further durotomy or duroplasty is required for improved blood supply and better patient outcomes.

Key findings include: 1. NIR-SO2 has advantages in measuring regional SO2 of the bone, skin, and fat, but its application in traumatic CSCI has not been explored before. 2. Laminectomy can improve blood reperfusion during surgery, but there is a lack of non-invasive and quantitative methods to assess this. 3. While some severely injured patients require durotomy for complete spinal cord decompression, reducing blood flow can worsen the prognosis by causing spinal tissue ischemia. 4. In the study, SO2 was more than 58% after laminectomy in 22 patients, indicating better prognosis and efficient blood flow; however, other patients required durotomy with duroplasty for significant outcomes. 5. The NIR technique can provide a quantifiable, real-time, dynamic, relatively non-invasive monitoring method for inferring the degree of spinal cord blood perfusion or ischemia and hypoxia. If SO2 is not between 58%~68%, laminectomy is insufficient, and further procedures may be necessary. 6. The study recommends using NIR during surgery to confirm complete decompression, assess the safety and significance of treatment methods, and evaluate prognosis after CSCI. However, the technique's accuracy should be ensured by checking CSF pulse and observing hematoma under the dura.

CONCLUSION

The scientific paper concludes that decompression in CSCI (Cervical Spinal Cord Injury) can potentially improve outcomes but may not always be effective. A unique technique, NIR-SO2, is proposed for monitoring blood flow during surgery, aiding in determining the necessity of extensive laminectomy or durotomy with duroplasty. The intraoperative NIR offers valuable insights into blood reperfusion at the injury site and is a non-invasive, real-time, quantitative, and dynamic monitoring technique for spinal cord ischemia and hypoxia. An SO2 value between 58%~68% indicates better recovery without a durotomy, while values less than 58% necessitate a durotomy for significant outcomes. NIR is also useful in evaluating SCI severity, selecting treatment modalities, determining effectiveness, and predicting prognosis. The technique is safe and feasible with no reported complications.
