Final Summary

The research study published in The Journal of Physiology investigates the role of lactate, a metabolite produced by astrocytes, in enhancing neuronal NMDA receptor (NMDAR) function and synaptic plasticity via a redox mechanism. Lactate enters neurons through monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), is oxidized by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) to pyruvate, generating NADH and increasing the intracellular NADH/NAD+ ratio. This redox shift promotes calcium release from ryanodine-sensitive stores (RyR) and recruits Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) to GluN2B-containing NMDARs, leading to enhanced NMDAR currents and prolonged decay kinetics. This strengthened NMDAR–CaMKII signaling increases NMDAR presence at postsynaptic sites, facilitating the molecular processes underlying long-term synaptic plasticity and memory formation. The research was conducted by a team from various institutions including King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST), UConn Health, Lausanne University Hospital, Università degli Studi di Milano, VIB-KU Leuven, and The Physiological Society. The study reveals a molecular pathway by which lactate, produced by astrocytes, can significantly influence neuronal activity and synaptic function, linking brain metabolism with learning and memory processes.

The modulation of INMDAR by lactate involves calcium signaling through Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which interacts directly with the GluN2B subunit of the receptor. Lactate strengthens the interaction between CaMKII and GluN2B through redox-sensitive cysteine residues in GluN2B, facilitating synaptic localization of this complex and enhancing synaptic responses.

RESULTS

The combined results of the studies discussed show that lactate potentiates NMDA receptor currents in cortical neurons, primarily through GluN2B-containing receptors. This process involves cellular uptake mediated by monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), followed by conversion to pyruvate via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH).

In a separate study, it was found that lactate potentiates NMDAR responses through an intracellular redox mechanism, modifying cysteine residues in the C-terminal domain of the GluN2B subunit. However, this effect seems independent of CaMKII's redox state.

Investigations into potential redox-sensitive regions within the disordered C-terminal domain (CTD) of GluN2B subunits revealed two cysteine-rich sequences that are crucial for lactate-induced potentiation of NMDAR. Redox modifications of these CTD regions promote the association between CaMKII and GluN2B, leading to increased clustering with PSD-95, which is essential for synaptic plasticity changes by lactate in neurons.

Overall, these findings provide insights into the role of lactate in regulating NMDARs and its impact on synaptic plasticity and learning processes in the brain.

DISCUSSION

This text appears to be a scientific article discussing the role of lactate, a metabolite produced mainly by astrocytes (a type of brain cell), in modulating neuronal function, specifically the potentiation of NMDA receptors on neurons and the facilitation of synaptic plasticity.

The study found that lactate is transported into neurons via monocarboxylate transporters 2 (MCT-2) where it is converted to pyruvate by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). This metabolic process increases the NADH/NAD+ ratio, leading to a more reduced intracellular milieu. The change in redox state affects cysteine-rich redox-sensitive sites on the C-terminal domain of the NMDA receptor GluN2B subunits, promoting CaMKII binding and modulating the activity of ryanodine receptors (RyRs), causing an increase in intracellular Ca2+ levels. Elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels activate CaMKII, which allows its binding to NMDA receptors, stimulating the accumulation of NMDA receptors at synapses and enhancing synaptic NMDA receptor function and synaptic plasticity.

The findings suggest that lactate plays a key role in modulating synaptic transmission and facilitating learning and memory through its effects on neuronal calcium signaling and NMDA receptor function.
