Final Summary

Title: Resistance Training-Induced Adaptations in the Neuromuscular System: A Topical Review

In this review published in The Journal of Physiology, Edoardo Lecce's team from the University of Rome ‘Foro Italico’ examines the neuromuscular adaptations resulting from resistance training (RT). The research explores changes at the brain, spinal circuitries, and skeletal muscle levels.

Central adaptations include increased excitability, improved synaptic input transmission, and decreased synaptic noise in the brain and spinal cord, leading to a more stable neural drive. At the spinal level, intrinsic motoneuron excitability, neuromodulatory input, and spinal circuitry are modified, resulting in enhanced motor unit (MU) excitability due to attenuated inhibitory pathways.

Skeletal muscle adaptations involve activation of contractile protein synthesis, structural repair by satellite cells, and endurance capacity improvement through mitochondrial biogenesis and capillarization. These changes lead to increased strength, improved physical performance, and alterations in muscle fiber type composition, energy metabolism, glucose uptake, and hormone levels.

The study aims to expand understanding of neuromuscular function and its practical applications for healthy individuals and those affected by chronic disorders. By comprehensively addressing neuromuscular adaptations induced by resistance training, the research provides valuable insights that can aid in developing effective clinical and athletic prescriptions for improved health, function, and independence throughout the lifespan.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction discusses the various adaptations at central and spinal levels in response to resistance training (RT). Central adaptations include enhanced excitability of reticulospinal pathways, increased motoneuron gain, and reduced firing variability in motor units. Spinal adaptations involve changes in intrinsic motoneuron excitability, neuromodulatory input, and spinal circuitry. Inhibitory pathways are attenuated during RT, leading to increased MU excitability. The text also highlights the role of synaptic plasticity in these adaptations, including upregulated persistent Na+ and Ca2+ currents and changes in glutamate and GABA inhibition. Methodological challenges persist in understanding these adaptations in humans.

The provided text also discusses mechanical, metabolic, and endocrine adaptations in response to RT, leading to increased strength, improved physical performance, and alterations in muscle fiber type composition, energy metabolism, mitochondrial biogenesis, glucose uptake, and hormone levels.

In summary, the text provides an overview of neuromuscular adaptations that occur due to resistance training, focusing on changes in motor units, intrinsic motoneuron excitability, and neuromodulatory input, as well as mechanical, metabolic, and endocrine adaptations. Understanding these adaptations can aid in developing effective clinical and athletic prescriptions for improved health, function, and independence throughout the lifespan.
