Moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning to enhance cerebrovascular function in the elderly: a randomized controlled trial

Hugo Randy, Titouan Paul Perrin, Abdallah Ghaith, Dario Kohlbrenner, Patrice Flore, Benoit Champigneulle, Michel Francois Guinot, Stephane Doutreleau, Samuel Verges, Julien V. Brugniaux, Mathieu Marillier | 2021

Keywords: cerebrovascular function, cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide, intermittent hypoxia conditioning, vascular aging

Final Summary

Title: Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning and Cerebrovascular Function in Elderly Individuals

Research Question: Does an 8-week Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) program improve cerebrovascular responsiveness in healthy elderly individuals?

Methods: A single-blind, parallel group, randomized controlled trial involving 18 healthy Caucasian elderly individuals without chronic cardiovascular, metabolic, or respiratory diseases underwent either an IHC protocol or a sham normoxic control. Participants completed 3 sessions per week over an 8-week period and cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide was evaluated using pulsed transcranial Doppler.

Key Findings: After the 8-week intervention, no significant differences in cerebrovascular responsiveness were found between the IHC group and the control group before and after the intervention.

Conclusions: This study did not find evidence that an 8-week IHC program improves cerebrovascular responsiveness in healthy older adults. The authors recommend future research using alternative methodologies to better understand the potential effects of IHC on NO bioavailability and cerebrovascular function.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

Title: Moderate Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning to Boost Cerebrovascular Function in the Elderly

Authors: Hugo Randy, Titouan Paul Perrin, Abdallah Ghaith, Dario Kohlbrenner, Patrice Flore, Benoit Champigneulle, Michel Francois Guinot, Stéphane Doutreleau, Samuel Verges, Julien V. Brugniaux, Mathieu Marillier (HP2, Inserm, CHU Grenoble Alpes, University Grenoble Alpes, Grenoble, France)

Summary: This short report presents a randomized controlled trial investigating the effects of moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning on cerebrovascular function in the elderly. The study involved 24 participants aged 65 or older who were randomly assigned to either an intervention group (IG) undergoing intermittent hypoxia training for six weeks, or a control group (CG). Cerebral hemodynamic response was assessed using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) before and after the intervention. The results showed significant improvements in cerebrovascular reactivity in the IG compared to the CG, indicating that moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning may enhance cerebrovascular function in elderly individuals.

ABSTRACT

This study investigates the impact of Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) on cerebrovascular function in elderly individuals over short- and mid-term. Contrary to expectations, 8 weeks of moderate IHC did not significantly improve cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide at any time point, suggesting that IHC may not confer beneficial effects on cerebrovascular function in older adults. Future studies are recommended to confirm these preliminary findings and explore a more comprehensive evaluation of cerebral hemodynamic control.

INTRODUCTION

The paper introduces the significance of endothelial cells in maintaining vascular health, particularly in the elderly population due to age-related endothelial dysfunction and its association with cardio- and cerebrovascular diseases. The study explores intermittent hypoxic conditioning (IHC), which has shown benefits for cardiovascular system, metabolism, and cognitive function across various populations. IHC is thought to enhance endothelial function by stimulating nitric oxide synthase (NOS) isoforms synthesis. Previous research suggests that both acute and repeated increases in shear stress improve peripheral vascular function, and hypoxic exposure can increase blood flow and improve peripheral vascular function. In the cerebrovascular domain, acute hypoxia induces vasodilation and increases blood velocity. However, the study aims to investigate whether IHC may serve as a potential strategy to enhance brain health in elderly individuals, with the hypothesis that IHC would enhance cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) both short-term and medium-term post-intervention.

METHODS

The study is a single-blind, parallel group, randomized controlled trial involving 18 healthy Caucasian elderly individuals (aged 60–80 years) without chronic cardiovascular, metabolic, or respiratory diseases (except for common aging conditions like hypertension and treated obstructive sleep apnea). Recruitment took place from October 2021 to May 2024 through elderly associations in Grenoble, France, and by word of mouth.

Participants underwent a clinical examination, including a 12-lead resting electrocardiogram and spirometry, before being randomly assigned (1:1 allocation ratio) to either the intermittent hypoxic conditioning (IHC) group or the sham normoxic control group.

Each participant completed 3 sessions per week over an 8-week period, with sessions involving intermittent hypoxia and continuous monitoring of heart rate and oxygen saturation. Intermittent hypoxic conditioning consisted of seven 5-minute cycles of hypoxia followed by 3-minute normoxic breathing periods, for a total of 35 minutes of hypoxic breathing per session.

Cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVR) was evaluated using pulsed transcranial Doppler during each experimental visit, with participants breathed through a mouthpiece connected to a gas analyzer. The CVR assessment protocol involved a 20-minute period of supine rest, a 3-minute hyperventilation task, and a 10-minute washout period before initiating the hypercapnia assessment.

Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using digital converter and LabChart software, with sample size calculated based on previous results showing a 25% improvement in CVR following exercise training. The study aimed to achieve a statistical power of 80% with an expected difference of 5 ± 4% in CVR between the IHC and control conditions. Statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

The study initially included 31 participants, but only 18 were retained for analysis due to difficulties in MCA insonation. Participants with inaccessible MCA during the initial visit were invited for the conditioning protocol. Two participants were excluded due to incomplete adherence. The Interhemispheric (IHC) protocol was well-tolerated, and no adverse events related to the intervention were reported. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of anthropometric, cardiovascular, or pulmonary function parameters. One participant in the IHC group had obstructive sleep apnea treated by continuous positive airway pressure, and two others had treated glaucoma.

Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) to carbon dioxide data are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 2. During hypercapnia, MCAv and PET were comparable between groups across all time points, with no significant group or time effects. Similarly, during hypocapnia, baseline MCAv and PET were consistent between groups and all time points, with no differences over time points. As a result, CVR values remained unchanged in both groups throughout the study for both absolute and relative changes. The calibrated hyperventilation task produced similar physiological responses in both groups with no differences over time points, and CVR values also remained stable along the conditioning program.

DISCUSSION

This text appears to be a research article about the effects of intermittent hypoxic conditioning (IHC) on cerebrovascular function in elderly individuals. The study aimed to investigate whether an 8-week IHC program would have a positive impact on cerebrovascular responsiveness.

The research involved 12 healthy older adults who were randomly assigned to either an intermittent hypoxic conditioning (IHC) group or a control group. Participants in the IHC group underwent a specific breathing protocol that consisted of inhaling a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen at reduced levels for short periods, followed by normal air for longer periods.

The study found no significant differences in cerebrovascular responsiveness to hyper- or hypocapnia between the IHC group and the control group before and after the 8-week intervention. The authors suggest that traditional methods of measuring cerebrovascular responsiveness may lack sensitivity to detect subtle changes in NO bioavailability, which is a mechanism thought to be enhanced by intermittent hypoxic conditioning.

The researchers acknowledge several limitations to their study, including the small sample size and difficulties associated with non-spatially resolved transcranial doppler ultrasonography in elderly individuals. They suggest that future studies should consider alternative methodologies to better evaluate the effects of IHC on NO bioavailability and cerebrovascular function.

In summary, this study did not find evidence that an 8-week intermittent hypoxic conditioning program improves cerebrovascular responsiveness in healthy older adults. The authors recommend further research using alternative methodologies to better understand the potential effects of IHC on NO bioavailability and cerebrovascular function.
