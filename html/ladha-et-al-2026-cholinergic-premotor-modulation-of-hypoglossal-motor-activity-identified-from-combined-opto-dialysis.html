Final Summary

Title: Cholinergic Modulation of Hypoglossal Motor Activity in Vivo

The research paper, led by R. Amin Ladha and R. L. Horner, investigates the role of cholinergic neurons in controlling tongue movements during sleep through a combined optogenetics (optical manipulation of neurons) and dialysis (substance removal from living organisms) approach. The study was conducted at the University of Toronto and St. Michael's Hospital in Canada, focusing on mice expressing Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)-positive neurons.

The researchers identified that intermediate reticular nucleus (IRt) stimulation increases tongue motor responses power-dependently, with lesser responses from the IRt compared to hypoglossal motor nucleus (HMN). The study revealed opposing inhibitory and excitatory mechanisms of muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (MAchR) and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (N AchR) modulation in respiratory hypoglossal motor output from the HMN. Specifically, MAchR antagonism increased endogenous respiratory tongue motor activity at higher stimulation intensities during tonic IRt stimulation, while addition of N AchR antagonism decreased respiratory tongue activity at lower stimulation intensities. Conversely, MAchR antagonism alone or combined with N AchR antagonism decreased evoked tonic motor activity.

These findings shed light on the complex mechanisms underlying obstructive sleep apnea treatment by identifying cholinergic modulation of IRt-evoked HMN activity that shapes both tonic and respiratory hypoglossal motor outputs in vivo. The study offers potential targets for future therapeutic interventions related to sleep disorders.

PREAMBLE

Title: Cholinergic premotor modulation of hypoglossal motor activity identified using combined "opto-dialysis" in vivo

In this research article, the authors, led by R. Amin Ladha and R. L. Horner, investigate the role of cholinergic neurons in controlling the activity of the hypoglossal nerve, which is responsible for regulating tongue movements during sleep. The study was conducted at the University of Toronto and St. Michael's Hospital in Canada.

The researchers combined two techniques – optogenetics (optical manipulation of neurons) and dialysis (removal of substances from a living organism) – to record the activity of cholinergic neurons in awake, behaving animals while measuring their effect on the hypoglossal nerve. The results suggest that these neurons play a critical role in modulating the activity of this nerve during sleep and wakefulness.

This study provides new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying sleep disorders and offers potential targets for future therapeutic interventions.

ABSTRACT

This study explores the cholinergic modulation of the hypoglossal motor nucleus (HMN), a key component in treating obstructive sleep apnea, by focusing on the intermediate reticular nucleus (IRt) as a significant source of cholinergic input. The researchers used optical stimulation and pharmacological manipulation in anesthetized transgenic mice to identify that the IRt stimulation increases tongue motor responses power-dependently, with lesser responses from the IRt compared to HMN.

They found opposing inhibitory and excitatory mechanisms of muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (MAchR) and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (N AchR) modulation in respiratory hypoglossal motor output from the HMN. Specifically, MAchR antagonism increased endogenous respiratory tongue motor activity at higher stimulation intensities during tonic IRt stimulation, while addition of N AchR antagonism decreased respiratory tongue activity at lower stimulation intensities. Conversely, MAchR antagonism alone or combined with N AchR antagonism decreased evoked tonic motor activity.

In summary, this study identifies cholinergic modulation of IRt-evoked HMN activity that shapes both tonic and respiratory hypoglossal motor outputs in vivo, shedding light on the complex mechanisms underlying obstructive sleep apnea treatment.

INTRODUCTION

The research paper 'Cholinergic Modulation of Hypoglossal Motor Activity in Vivo' investigates the modulation of hypoglossal motor activity in mice through optical stimulation, using genetically engineered ChR2-EYFP-ChAT mice. The study employs four distinct protocols to manipulate cholinergic receptors and observe changes in tongue motor activity: 1. Pulsed optical stimulation of hypoglossal motor activity. 2. Continuous tonic optical stimulation of hypoglossal motor activity at varying powers. 3. Combined continuous tonic optical stimulation of the intermediomedial thalamic nucleus (IRt) with cholinergic receptor antagonism in the hypoglossal motor nucleus (HMN). 4. Continuous tonic optical stimulation of IRt along with combined muscarinic and nicotinic cholinergic receptor antagonism at HMN.

The study also includes analysis methods for each protocol and histology procedures to confirm probe locations, aiming to understand the role of cholinergic modulation in hypoglossal motor activity related to respiration. The researchers analyzed the spatial distribution of co-expressed ChAT and EYFP transcripts in various regions of the mouse medulla, including HMN, DMX, NA, para-HMN region, and non-cholinergic spinal trigeminal nucleus (Sp5), using automated cell detection algorithms and statistical analyses (P < 0.05).

RESULTS

In this scientific study, researchers investigate the modulation of hypoglossal motor activity (tongue movements) using optogenetics, a technique that controls genetically modified neurons with light. The study focuses on mice expressing Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)-positive neurons, primarily found in the hypoglossal motor nucleus and inferior colliculus.

Two optical stimulation protocols were conducted: pulsed and graded tonic photostimulation. The results showed that:

  1. Pulsed optical stimulation at the Hypoglossal Motor Nucleus (HMN) elicited selective increases in endogenously generated respiratory-related tongue motor output without affecting diaphragm activity.
  2. Tonic optical stimulation at higher powers led to greater effects on the magnitude of the endogenous peak respiratory HMN output, with stronger effects from tonic excitation at the HMN compared to the Intermediate Reticular Nucleus (IRt).
  3. Microperfusion of carbachol, a mixed muscarinic and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist, into the HMN suppressed tongue motor activity, an effect attenuated by the muscarinic antagonist scopolamine.
  4. Continuous tonic optical stimulation of the IRt combined with cholinergic receptor antagonism at the HMN modulated optically induced respiratory and tonic hypoglossal motor responses, with interactions between stimulation power at the IRt and cholinergic receptor manipulation at the HMN.

The findings demonstrate the feasibility of using optogenetics to control and investigate tongue movements in mice, potentially providing insights into brain function and motor neuron disorders.

DISCUSSION

The study investigates the modulation of hypoglossal motor activity (HMN) in vivo, focusing on the influence of cholinergic receptors. Optically induced IRt excitation increases tonic HMN motor activity, which is reduced with mecamylamine (an antagonist for nicotinic acetylcholine receptors - NachR) and muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (MAchR) antagonism at the HMN. The findings suggest an inhibitory cholinergic mechanism at the HMN during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, potentially originating from the IRt, pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, or neurons with large cholinergic terminals at hypoglossal motoneurons. The study also demonstrates distinct directional effects of MAchR antagonism (increased respiratory motor activity) vs. NachR antagonism (decreased tonic hypoglassal motor activity). These findings could relate to the clinical responses to antimuscarinic-noradrenergic drug combination in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) pharmacotherapy. Future studies are proposed to further explore these mechanisms and their implications for OSA management.
