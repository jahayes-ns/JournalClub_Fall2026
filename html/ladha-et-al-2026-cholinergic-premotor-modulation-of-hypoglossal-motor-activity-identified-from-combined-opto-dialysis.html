Sleep Disorders: Cholinergic premotor modulation of hypoglossal motor activity identified from combined ‘opto-dialysis’ in vivo

Amin Ladha, Raina, da Silveira Scarpellini, Carolina, Liu, Wen-Ying, Hattie Liu, Liu, Kevin Patrick Grace, Kevin, Montandon, Gaspard, Horner, Richard L. | 2026

Keywords: genioglossus, hypoglossal motor nucleus, obstructive sleep apnea, upper airway

Final Summary

The research titled "Sleep Disorders: Cholinergic premotor modulation of hypoglossal motor activity identified from combined ‘opto-dialysis’ in vivo" investigates the cholinergic control of muscle movements during sleep. The study was conducted by a team at the University of Toronto and St. Michael's Hospital, Canada, employing an innovative method called "opto-dialysis."

The researchers found that cholinergic neurons in the brainstem modulate hypoglossal motor activity during sleep, contributing to sleep-related muscle movements such as twitches and episodes of bruxism. Specifically, they discovered that the intermediate reticular nucleus (IRt) is a significant source of cholinergic input to the Hypoglossal Motor Nerve (HMN), controlling tongue movement. Optical stimulation of cholinergic neurons in both the HMN and IRt led to increased tongue motor responses, with lesser responses observed from the IRt.

Furthermore, the study revealed that muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (MAchR) antagonism at the HMN increases endogenous respiratory tongue motor activity at higher stimulation intensities during tonic IRt stimulation, while the addition of nicotinic AchR antagonism decreased respiratory tongue activity at lower intensities. These findings suggest opposing inhibitory and excitatory MAchR- and NAchR-dependent modulation of respiratory hypoglossal motor output.

The study's conclusions highlight the potential implications for understanding and treating obstructive sleep apnea, as anti-MAchR agents are components of its pharmacotherapy. The research could have significant implications for developing new therapies for disorders involving muscle control during sleep.

PREAMBLE

This research article, titled "Sleep Disorders: Cholinergic premotor modulation of hypoglossal motor activity identified from combined ‘opto-dialysis’ in vivo," presents findings on the cholinergic control of muscle movements during sleep. The study was conducted by a team of researchers at the University of Toronto and St. Michael's Hospital, Canada.

The team employed an innovative method called "opto-dialysis" to directly measure neural activity in sleeping animals. They identified that cholinergic neurons in the brainstem modulate hypoglossal motor activity during sleep, contributing to sleep-related muscle movements such as twitches and episodes of bruxism (teeth grinding).

The research offers valuable insights into the physiological mechanisms underlying sleep disorders, paving the way for potential future therapeutic strategies to manage these conditions.

ABSTRACT

This study investigates the cholinergic modulation of the hypoglossal motor nucleus (HMN), a key area controlling tongue movement, in anesthetized transgenic mice. The intermediate reticular nucleus (IRt) was found to be a significant source of cholinergic input to the HMN. Optical stimulation of cholinergic neurons in both the HMN and IRt led to power-dependent increases in tongue motor responses, with lesser responses observed from the IRt.

During tonic IRt stimulation, MAchR antagonism at the HMN increased endogenous respiratory tongue motor activity at higher stimulation intensities, while addition of nicotinic AchR antagonism decreased respiratory tongue activity at lower intensities. These findings suggest opposing inhibitory and excitatory MAchR- and NAchR-dependent modulation of respiratory hypoglossal motor output.

Furthermore, MAchR antagonism alone or combined with NAchR antagonism at the HMN decreased evoked tonic motor activity, indicating a reduction of a tonic excitatory effect. The study reveals cholinergic modulation of IRt-evoked HMN activity that shapes both tonic and respiratory hypoglossal motor outputs in vivo. This research could have implications for understanding and treating obstructive sleep apnea, as anti-MAchR agents are components of its pharmacotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

The study presented in the text is a scientific research article focusing on cholinergic modulation of hypoglossal motor activity in vivo. Genetically modified mice expressing light-sensitive proteins (ChR2) in cholinergic neurons were used for optical stimulation and pharmacological manipulation using microdialysis probes.

The study employed four main protocols to analyze the effects of pulsed and continuous tonic optical stimulation on tongue motor activity, as well as the role of cholinergic receptor antagonism at the hypoglossal nerve nucleus. Post-experiment, histology and immunohistochemistry were conducted to confirm probe placements and assess the spatial distribution and co-expression of ChAT and EYFP transcripts across multiple regions of interest in the caudal medulla.

The objective was to understand how continuous tonic optical stimulation affects tongue motor activity in vivo, and the study was powered to detect a 20% difference in the primary outcome variable with 80% power and a 5% significance level. For immunohistochemistry, brain sections were double-stained for ChAT and EYFP using primary antibodies followed by secondary antibodies, while in situ hybridization used an RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Assay to visualize ChAT and EYFP mRNA.

RESULTS

In this scientific study, researchers used optogenetics to modulate hypoglossal motor activity in mice. They found that photostimulation of the Hypoglossal Motor Nerve (HMN) resulted in larger tongue motor responses compared to stimulation at the Inferior Salient Nucleus (ISN). The study also discovered that cholinergic receptors play a crucial role in modulating respiratory motor activities, particularly affecting tongue motor activity more than diaphragm activation.

The experiments involved two protocols: pulsed optical stimulation and graded tonic photostimulation. Results showed that increasing levels of optical stimulation elicited increased tonic activity with greater responses and lower activation thresholds at the HMN compared to the IRt. Tonic excitation at the HMN also led to selective increases in the magnitude of endogenously generated respiratory-related tongue motor output but not diaphragm activation.

Pharmacological manipulation of the HMN showed that microperfusion of the mixed MAchR and NAchR agonist carbachol into the HMN suppressed tongue motor activity, but this effect was attenuated by the MAchR antagonist scopolamine. The study also found that MAchR antagonism at the HMN increased the magnitude of endogenous peak respiratory HMN output at higher stimulation levels at the IRt.

Overall, these findings suggest that cholinergic receptors modulate respiratory motor activities and that the HMN is a key site for these effects. The study also highlights the potential use of optogenetics in developing new therapies for disorders involving motor control.

DISCUSSION

This study investigates the modulation of hypoglossal motor activity (HMN) by cholinergic receptors, focusing on their potential implications for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) pharmacotherapy. The researchers used optogenetics in mice to investigate the effects of muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (MAchR) and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (NAchR) antagonism on hypoglossal motor responses elicited by continuous tonic photostimulation of the cholinergic intermediate reticular nucleus (IRt).

Key findings include: 1. MAchR antagonism at the HMN leads to increased tongue motor activity during sleep, particularly in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, indicating an inhibitory cholinergic mechanism at the HMN during REM sleep. 2. The IRt acts as a major source of inputs to the HMN and plays a role in augmenting the HMN output. 3. During REM sleep, the withdrawal of facilitating monoaminergic inputs results in a major suppression of HMN activity, mediated by the withdrawal of noradrenergic and other monoaminergic inputs rather than active inhibition by glycine or c-aminobutyric acid. 4. Differential inhibitory and excitatory MAchR and NAchR modulation of IRt-evoked HMN activity shape tonic and respiratory hypoglossal motor outputs. 5. These findings could have implications for the clinical responses to antimuscarinic-noradrenergic drug combinations in OSA pharmacotherapy, with future studies targeting IRt inputs across sleep-wake states being suggested to generate new knowledge relevant to HMN activity and OSA pharmacotherapy management.
