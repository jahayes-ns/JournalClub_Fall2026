Final Summary

Title: Exercise-Induced Neural Plasticity Along the Mesencephalic Locomotor Region (MLR)-Spinal Cord (SC) Pathway in Mice

The study by Yue Dai et al., published on January 2nd, 2026, investigates the effects of exercise on neural plasticity from the MLR to the spinal cord in mice. The research employed a controlled experimental design with C57BL/6J mice subjected to a 3-week treadmill exercise regimen.

Key findings include: 1. Exercise increased neuronal excitability, with ventral SC neurons more affected than dorsal ones. 2. Persistent inward currents (PICs) were enhanced, particularly in SC neurons, affecting firing duration/frequency (calcium-mediated PICs) and threshold/capacity (sodium-mediated PICs). 3. Exercise led to increased dendritic complexity, more pronounced in SC versus MLR neurons, with ventral spinal neurons showing greater dendritic complexity than dorsal ones. 4. A correlation was found between exercise-driven dendritic plasticity and enhanced PICs and excitability, collectively promoting locomotor adaptation.

Overall, the study reveals an exercise-induced coordinated plasticity throughout the locomotor system, with spinal circuits, particularly ventral components, exhibiting greater functional and structural adaptability than MLR. The research provides electrophysiological, ionic, and morphological insights into activity-dependent neural adaptation.

PREAMBLE

Title: Exercise Induces Neural Plasticity in the Mesencephalic Locomotor Region to Spinal Cord in Mice

Author(s): Yue Dai, Liming Yang, Yi Cheng, Xinyi Wei

Published: January 2nd, 2026

DOI: https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8363166/v1

Keywords: Exercise, Plasticity, Motor Control, Locomotion, Neuromodulation

Summary: This study investigates the effect of exercise on neural plasticity from the Mesencephalic Locomotor Region (MLR) to the spinal cord in mice. The research employs a controlled experimental design to analyze changes in motor control and locomotion following various exercise regimens. Data obtained from this study sheds light on the role of exercise in modulating neural connections between the MLR and the spinal cord, thereby contributing to our understanding of motor control mechanisms.

ABSTRACT

The abstract of the scientific paper summarizes a study on exercise-induced neuroplasticity along the mesencephalic locomotor region (MLR)–spinal cord (SC) pathway in P42-P45 mice after three weeks of treadmill exercise. Key findings include:

  1. Exercise increased neuronal excitability, with ventral SC neurons more affected than dorsal ones.
  2. Persistent inward currents (PICs) were enhanced, particularly in SC neurons, with calcium-mediated PICs affecting firing duration/frequency and sodium-mediated PICs influencing threshold/capacity.
  3. Exercise led to increased dendritic complexity, more pronounced in SC versus MLR neurons, with ventral spinal neurons showing greater dendritic complexity than dorsal ones.
  4. A correlation was found between exercise-driven dendritic plasticity and enhanced PICs and excitability, collectively promoting locomotor adaptation.

Overall, the study reveals an exercise-induced coordinated plasticity throughout the locomotor system, with spinal circuits, particularly ventral components, exhibiting greater functional and structural adaptability than MLR. The research provides electrophysiological, ionic, and morphological insights into activity-dependent neural adaptation.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper outlines the fundamental role of locomotion, initiated within the mesencephalic locomotor region (MLR) and controlled by central pattern generators (CPGs) in the spinal cord. The locomotor system not only generates rhythmic locomotor output but also adapts to dynamic environments through adaptive plasticity in response to exercise, affecting neuronal excitability, dendritic plasticity, channel modulation, gene expression, and protein synthesis.

Recent studies on mouse midbrain and spinal cord neurons have demonstrated that chronic exercise enhances persistent inward currents (PICs), promotes dendritic plasticity, and upregulates the excitability of specific neurons in both regions. This research indicates that exercise modulates the locomotor system at both the midbrain and spinal cord levels.

The study aims to address four critical questions: differences in regulatory effects of exercise on neuronal excitability between MLR neurons and spinal interneurons, simultaneous enhancement of PICs in both regions, synchronous promotion of neuronal dendritic plasticity, and the dominant role of either the MLR or the spinal cord in the adaptability of the locomotor system. To answer these questions, this study will subject C57BL/6J mice to a 3-week treadmill exercise regimen and examine the concurrent effects on the electrophysiological, morphological, and ionic properties of MLR neurons and spinal interneurons using whole-cell patch-clamp recording.

RESULTS

The study investigates the effects of exercise on midbrain and spinal cord neurons, focusing on persistent inward currents (PICs) composed of sodium (Na-PIC) and calcium (Ca-PIC) currents. Exercise hyperpolarizes PIC onset and increases PIC amplitude in both types of neurons, with a stronger effect on spinal cord neurons. The study also finds that exercise enhances the current thresholds for sustained firing and increases the firing duration in both types of neurons, with Ca-PIC playing a more significant role in spinal cord neurons. Dendritic plasticity changes were observed in both types of neurons, with spinal cord neurons showing a trend towards larger dendritic values. The study concludes that both Na-PIC and Ca-PIC play essential roles in regulating the excitability of midbrain and spinal cord neurons during exercise, with spinal cord neurons exhibiting greater plasticity.

In another study, the effects of exercise on dorsal (proprioceptive) and ventral (locomotor) spinal cord neurons were compared. Exercise enhanced the excitability of both types of neurons, with a stronger effect on ventral neurons regarding reductions in rheobase, AP half-width, and AHP half-width. Regarding dendritic plasticity, exercise promoted dendritic growth in both types of neurons, with ventral neurons showing larger values for total dendritic length, number of primary dendrites, dendritic branch points, dendritic terminals, and somatic area. The study suggests that exercise has a stronger modulatory effect on the excitability and dendritic plasticity of ventral neurons compared to dorsal neurons.

Lastly, a study investigates the functional significance of exercise-induced plasticity in spinal cord (SC) and motor root letve (MLR) neurons. Exercise promoted dendritic plasticity, enhanced PICs, and increased neuronal excitability in both SC and MLR neurons. The study found that the hyperpolarization of Vth induced by exercise is primarily due to the hyperpolarization of PIC onset, particularly driven by Na-PIC, while the increase in PIC amplitude may result from the growth in dendritic length. The study suggests that SC neurons show greater plasticity than MLR neurons and play a dominant role in the adaptive remodeling of the locomotor system in response to exercise intervention.

DISCUSSION

The study by Ge & Dai (2020) investigates the impact of moderate-intensity treadmill exercise on neuronal plasticity in the spinal cord (SC), midbrain locomotor regions (MLR), and dorsal and ventral spinal interneurons. Exercise enhances excitability, potentiates persistent inward currents (PICs), and promotes dendritic growth in all three populations. The SC motor system exhibits greater plasticity than the MLR system. Ventral neurons show higher intrinsic excitability than dorsal neurons, with exercise-induced changes being more pronounced in ventral neurons.

The study employed image analysis using ImageJ software to examine dendritic complexity in SC and MLR neurons. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and Prism, with a P-value threshold of <0.05 considered significant. The research does not differentiate between neurons from specific subregions of the cuneiform nucleus and pedunculopontine nucleus in the MLR. Further investigation is needed to understand the differences between SC and MLR neurons, as well as DRN 5-HT neurons in the context of exercise-induced adaptation.
