Acute Intermittent Hypoxia Exerts Beneficial Effects and Promotes Repair in Male Mice in the Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis Model of Multiple Sclerosis

Tokarska, Roberts, Rathnayaka Koralage, Naniong, Johnston, Wei, Verge | 2026

Keywords: axon protection, macrophage, microglia, multiple sclerosis, myelination, oligodendrocyte, oligodendrocyte precursor cell

Final Summary

This study, published in Glia (2026), investigates the impact of Acute Intermittent Hypoxia on a mouse model of Multiple Sclerosis (EAE). Conducted by researchers from the University of Saskatchewan and McGill University, the research aimed to determine if hypoxia could promote repair, specifically focusing on axon protection, macrophages, microglia, myelination, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells. The results suggest that acute intermittent hypoxia promoted an axon-protective phenotype in male EAE mice, as evidenced by increased levels of phosphorylated neurofilaments (pNF) in inflamed areas compared to normoxia-treated animals. Additionally, hypoxia treatment increased the recruitment of Oligodendrocyte Progenitor Cells (OPCs) and mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes in demyelinated regions. The study was funded by multiple grants from organizations including the Multiple Sclerosis Society of Canada, the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, and the University of Saskatchewan. The data supporting these findings are available upon request, with no conflicts of interest declared by the authors.

ABSTRACT

The research study titled "Alternating Current Stimulation promotes axon protection and remyelination in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, an animal model of multiple sclerosis," published in Glia in 2026, investigates the effects of Alternating Current Stimulation (ACS) on a mouse model of Multiple Sclerosis (MS). The study found that ACS treatment initiated at the peak of EAE disease score (2.5) promoted an axon protective phenotype in male EAE mice, as evidenced by increased levels of phosphorylated neurofilaments (pNF) in inflamed areas compared to Normoxia-treated animals. ACS treatment also increased the recruitment of Oligodendrocyte Progenitor Cells (OPCs) and mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes in demyelinated regions in male EAE mice, supporting remyelination. The work was funded by multiple grants from organizations such as the Multiple Sclerosis Society of Canada, the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, and the University of Saskatchewan. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The authors declare no conflicts of interest.
