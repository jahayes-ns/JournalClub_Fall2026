Final Summary

In the study titled "Funding Distributions, Trends, Gaps, and Policy Implications for Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Research: A Systematic Analysis of U.S. Federal Funds," researchers evaluated federal funding for SCI research from 2008-2025 across three primary agencies – National Institutes of Health (NIH), Department of Defense's Congressionally Directed Medical Research Programs (CDMRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). The study aimed to identify trends, gaps, and policy implications in SCI research funding.

The key findings reveal that NIH mainly focuses on basic and early-stage translational research in SCI pathology, regeneration, and motor functional recovery. In contrast, CDMRP funds applied and clinical research primarily in pain, bladder function, and neuromodulatory device development, while the VA invests in rehabilitation studies aimed at musculoskeletal and functional health outcomes. Critical funding gaps were identified in areas like bowel/gastrointestinal health, cardiovascular function, and mental health. The study warns of a 50% decline in federal SCI research funding due to proposed NIH budget reductions, highlighting the need for coordinated, data-driven strategies and the development of a "living dashboard" for enhanced transparency and strategic investment in SCI research.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study titled "Funding Distributions, Trends, Gaps, and Policy Implications for Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Research: A Systematic Analysis of U.S. Federal Funds" is a comprehensive analysis of federally funded SCI research in the United States. The researchers followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews and used data from NIH RePORT, NIH RePORTER, CDMRP/DTIC portal, and the VA Office of Research and Development. They classified awards into categories using a schema developed with federal and private program staff and SCI experts, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. The study identified trends, gaps, and policy implications in SCI research funding, providing valuable insights for future research priorities. The study involved researchers from various institutions including Texas A&M University, the University of British Columbia, San Francisco State University, Unite2Fight Paralysis, and others.

ABSTRACT

The study evaluates the distribution of federal funding for spinal cord injury (SCI) research from NIH, DoD's CDMRP SCIRP, and VA. Analysis of 1,589 awards from 2008-2025 reveals that NIH focuses on basic and early-stage translational research in SCI pathology, regeneration, and motor functional recovery. In contrast, CDMRP funds applied and clinical research primarily in pain, bladder function, and neuromodulatory device development. The VA invests mainly in rehabilitation studies aimed at musculoskeletal and functional health outcomes. Critical funding gaps were identified in areas like bowel/gastrointestinal health, cardiovascular function, and mental health. The study warns of a 50% decline in federal SCI research funding due to proposed NIH budget reductions, highlighting the need for coordinated, data-driven strategies and the development of a "living dashboard" for enhanced transparency and strategic investment in SCI research.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction outlines the impact and urgency of spinal cord injury (SCI), affecting over 300,000 individuals in the US with approximately 18,000 new cases annually. The socioeconomic burden is substantial, with an estimated $7.74 billion in healthcare costs in 1997 and likely higher today. Federal agencies like NIH, DoD's CDMRP SCIRP, and VA are the primary funders of SCI research in the US, focusing on various aspects from basic studies to clinical trials. However, a comprehensive analysis of federal SCI research funding has not been conducted due to a lack of transparency, which this study aims to address by curating and analyzing 1,589 federally funded SCI research awards from 2008–2025 across the three agencies. The findings will provide insights into funding trends, agency priorities, and gaps in research, potentially informing the development of a centralized SCI research funding dashboard.

METHODS

The study aimed to analyze and classify funding data for Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) research from three primary US federal agencies: National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Department of Defense's Congressionally Directed Medical Research Programs (CDMRP), and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA).

The investigating team consisted of members from the advocacy organization Unite2Fight Paralysis, SCI researchers, individuals with lived SCI experience, and trainees. The data classification team included experts in the SCI field, people with SCI experiences, and trainees. Data retrieval was done using specific search strategies and quality control measures were applied to ensure accuracy.

The NIH data was retrieved from their Research Portfolio Online Reporting Tools (RePORT) for fiscal years 2008-2023. Duplicate entries, incomplete or conflicting data were resolved, resulting in a curated dataset of 4,010 records. CDMRP SCIRP data was obtained from internal sources and included 353 unique awards. VA data for FY2017-FY2025 was retrieved from their website, with only SCI-related grants being included in the analysis.

A three-axis classification system was used to categorize individual awards based on system/problem, approach/intervention, and readiness. Each award was reviewed by at least three members of the classification team, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion. Awards not relevant to SCI were excluded from the dataset.

Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 10, and R Studio. Pairwise correlation analyses were conducted to assess relationships between funding distributions from different federal agencies. Chi-square tests with Bonferroni corrections were used to determine significant differences in funding allocations among categories across funding agencies. Detailed information about network analyses of combinatorial awards is provided in the supplementary data.

RESULTS

The analysis reviews federal funding for spinal cord injury (SCI) research from 2017 to 2023, focusing on the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Congressionally Directed Medical Research Programs (CDMRP), and Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). Key findings include:

  • Total funding was approximately $860 million, with NIH contributing the largest share, followed by VA and CDMRP.
  • Funding primarily went to Discovery/Tools, Therapeutic Interventions, and Community/Resources categories, with a focus on in vivo approaches, invasive devices, drug interventions, and biological approaches.
  • Agency priorities were evident, for example, NIH invested more in In vivo approaches compared to CDMRP and VA, while the VA focused on cell/tissue therapies and secondary health conditions.
  • The CDMRP showed dynamic funding trends, potentially focusing on emerging therapeutic strategies.
  • Approximately 14% of NIH awards involved multiple interventions.
  • Combinatorial SCI research awards displayed a structured pattern of intervention pairing, with cell/tissue therapies combined with other biologicals being the most frequent approach.
  • From 2017 to 2023, $617M was allocated to in vitro and animal studies, while $292M supported clinical studies. The CDMRP focused on early-phase clinical trials, while NIH and VA invested minimally in these areas.
  • Intersectional analyses showed that drug-based interventions were the largest proportion of preclinical funding, while neuromodulatory device interventions received more than half of clinical study funding.
  • Funding for specific therapeutic interventions was distributed across system/problem categories, with distinct priorities among federal agencies.

The findings suggest a growing trend towards integrative approaches in SCI research and highlight complementary priorities of federal agencies addressing diverse aspects of SCI pathology through targeted therapeutic strategies.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzes 1,589 research awards from NIH, CDMRP, and VA to investigate the allocation of funds in Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) research in the U.S. The findings show that each agency has a distinct role: NIH supports basic and early-stage translational research, CDMRP focuses on translational and clinical readiness with an emphasis on neuromodulatory devices, and VA concentrates on rehabilitation, muscle and bone health, and implementation studies.

The study highlights the high disability burden associated with SCI and suggests that current funding levels may be insufficient to support late-stage clinical translation and commercialization due to the cost of clinical trials and developing new therapeutic compounds.

Recent and proposed changes in federal funding priorities pose threats to the stability of the SCI research ecosystem, including the cessation of appropriations to CDMRP SCIRP, potential restructuring or elimination of the Administration for Community Living, and a proposed 40% reduction in NIH funding.

The study finds that while motor and sensory function, regeneration, and neuroprotection continue to receive most funding, other community-identified concerns such as bowel and gastrointestinal health, autonomic dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, pressure sore injuries, and mental wellness remain vastly underfunded.

The authors propose dedicated funding streams for underrepresented, high-impact secondary complications like pressure wounds and GI management, and the formation of working groups to adapt to losses in SCI research funding. Improved standardization and transparency in grant labeling and classification, and the development of a dynamic, publicly accessible platform to track and visualize federally funded SCI research are also suggested.

Limitations include the lack of data preceding 2008 and the capture of only U.S. federal funding, omitting other private foundations and state governments. The authors suggest future efforts to promote greater open communication and data sharing among federal and nonfederal agencies could address these limitations.

CONCLUSION

The study by Gillespie et al. concludes that while federal funding for Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) research aligns with agency missions, there are critical gaps in matching community priorities, supporting infrastructure, and advancing translational research. To address these issues, the authors propose a centralized, living research dashboard as a key tool to promote efficiency, collaboration, and progress towards improving the quality of life for individuals with SCI.

They suggest forming a working group to tackle critical losses in SCI research funding.
