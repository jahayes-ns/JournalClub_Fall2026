Final Summary

Title: Electrical Epidural Stimulation (EES) Enhances Sensorimotor Recovery from Spinal Cord Injury (SCI): Understanding Potential Mechanisms

Research Question: The study aims to explore the potential mechanisms of Electrical Epidural Stimulation (EES) that promote sensorimotor recovery in individuals with spinal cord injuries (SCIs), focusing on plasticity mechanisms, afferent modulation, and genetic tools.

Methods: This review paper delves into various sensorimotor plasticity mechanisms that could enable EES to induce motor behaviors indicative of recovery, such as changes in local lumbar circuitry, the involvement of propriospinal interneurons, and the internal motor copy. The authors propose using chemogenetic tools to identify neural circuit changes induced by afferents activated by EES.

Key Findings: EES applied to the dorsal aspect of the lumbar spinal cord can potentially improve voluntary mobility across the knee and ankle in SCI patients. Recovery is likely a result of synergistic actions between local lumbar, propriospinal, and internal models. For incomplete lesions, the propriospinal network and descending reticulospinal command seem to play significant roles, while complete lesion recovery may be attributed to local lumbar circuit plasticity driven by afferent input.

Conclusions: EES could offer an effective therapy for enhancing sensorimotor recovery following SCI; however, the specific mechanisms behind this recovery remain to be fully understood. The authors suggest that genetic tools for afferent modulation will likely aid in further understanding these mechanisms of plasticity. This research may provide insights into potential strategies for improving motor function in individuals with SCI through targeted neural activation and learning-based approaches.

Keywords: Plasticity, Electrical Epidural Stimulation, Proprioceptive Afferent Connections, Propriospinal Circuitry, Internal Motor Copy, Chemogenetic Tools, Spinal Cord Injury.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This review paper published in Frontiers of Molecular Neuroscience focuses on epidural electrical stimulation (EES) and its potential role in enhancing recovery from spinal cord injury (SCI). The study suggests that while SCI often leads to long-term sensorimotor impairment, EES applied to the dorsal aspect of the lumbar spinal cord can induce motor behaviors indicative of recovery.

Introduced in 1976 to alleviate spasticity in Multiple Sclerosis patients, EES has since shown promise in improving voluntary mobility across the knee and ankle in SCI patients. The mechanisms underlying these improvements, however, remain largely unknown.

The review discusses several sensorimotor plasticity mechanisms that could potentially enable EES to promote recovery: 1. Changes in local lumbar circuitry 2. Involvement of propriospinal interneurons 3. The internal motor copy

The paper also explores genetic tools for afferent modulation as a promising method to aid in the discovery of EES's mechanisms of action. Keywords associated with this study include plasticity, electrical epidural stimulation, proprietal detours, monosynaptic connections, internal motor copy, efferent motor copy, and designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs).

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses two main hypotheses for how Electrical Spinal Cord Stimulation (ESCS) can aid in locomotion recovery for individuals with spinal cord injuries (SCI).

  1. Enhancement of Proprioceptive Afferent Connections: ESCS is believed to activate Ia muscle spindle afferents, boosting muscle recruitment and potentially improving locomotion by increasing overall force generated by affected muscles.

  2. Reorganization of Propriospinal Circuitry: ESCS may help reorganize propriospinal circuitry around the lesion site and within the central pattern generator (CPG) to promote rhythmic activity and hindlimb coordination, facilitating the relay of motor commands past the lesion site.

The paper also delves into other mechanisms involved in motor function and SCI recovery:

  1. Activation of Interneuronal Networks: Spinal interneurons are critical for motor function, especially in the context of locomotion. ESCS is suggested to help recruit these interneurons more effectively, improving motor function.

  2. Spatiotemporal Integration of the Internal Model with Peripheral Afferent Input Within Interneuronal Networks: The internal model plays a role in sensory prediction, real-time adjustments, and motor memory, potentially aiding in SCI recovery by correcting errors in the motor program and learning the correct motor output through spinal interneurons.

The paper highlights the presence of alpha-motoneurons as comparators for motor commands and outputs, the role of proprioceptive feedback in intrinsic spinal cord circuitry, and the existence of internal motor copy circuitry within local spinal circuits.

The authors aim to use chemogenetic tools to identify neural circuit changes induced by afferents activated by ESCS and understand the molecular mechanisms of plasticity underlying recovery from SCI. This research may provide insights into potential strategies for improving motor function in individuals with SCI through targeted neural activation and learning-based approaches.

CONCLUSION

The conclusion of the scientific paper suggests that Electrically Evoked Stimulation (EES) could be an effective therapy for enhancing sensorimotor recovery following Spinal Cord Injury (SCI). However, the specific mechanisms behind this recovery are not yet fully understood. This review proposes several plasticity mechanisms possibly triggered by EES through the activation of peripheral afferents. Recovery is likely a result of synergistic actions between local lumbar, propriospinal, and internal models. For incomplete lesions, the propriospinal network and descending reticulospinal command seem to play significant roles, while complete lesion recovery may be attributed to local lumbar circuit plasticity driven by afferent input. The authors suggest that genetic tools for afferent modulation will likely aid in further understanding these mechanisms of plasticity.
