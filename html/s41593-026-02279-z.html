Brain motion is driven by mechanical coupling with the abdomen

Garborg, Ghitti, Zhang, Ricotta, Frank, Mueller, Greenawalt, Turner, Kedarasetti, Mostafa, Lee, Costanzo, Drew | 2026

Final Summary

The research paper published in Nature Neuroscience investigates the mechanical coupling between the brain and abdominal cavity in mice using high-speed, multiplane two-photon microscopy. The primary finding is that abdominal muscle contractions drive brain motion through a hydraulic system mediated by the vertebral venous plexus (VVP), causing fluid flow across the brain surface and potentially explaining why quiescence during sleep is required for fluid flow through the glymphatic system. The study did not involve wild animals or field-collected samples.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a recent study published in Nature Neuroscience, researchers visualized motion of the dorsal cortex relative to the skull in awake head-fixed mice using high-speed, multiplane two-photon microscopy. The primary direction of brain motion was rostrally and laterally, correlated tightly with locomotion but not with respiration or cardiac cycle. The researchers found that this motion was driven by abdominal muscle contractions activating a hydraulic-like vascular connection between the nervous system and the abdominal cavity. Brain motion could also be induced by pressure applied to the abdomen.

The study suggests that brain motion may drive interstitial fluid through and out of the brain into the subarachnoid space, in the opposite direction of fluid flow seen during sleep. The results indicate a mechanical coupling between the brain and the abdominal compartment, and suggest that fluid flow in the brain could be coupled to body movements.

The authors hypothesize that one potential physiological purpose for this brain motion is to circulate interstitial fluid (ISF) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the brain. Understanding these fluid flows requires a detailed characterization of the mechanical dynamics of the brain. The study was conducted by researchers from Penn State Neuroscience Institute, Center for Neural Engineering, Department of Biomedical Engineering, Department of Engineering Science and Mechanics, Auckland Bioengineering Institute, Department of Physiology, Department of Mechanical Engineering, Center for Quantitative Imaging, Department of Biology, Department of Mathematics, and Department of Neurosurgery at the Pennsylvania State University.

RESULTS

The study investigates the relationship between abdominal muscle activity, intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), and brain motion in mice. The researchers propose that abdominal muscle contraction before locomotion drives brain motion through a hydraulic system mediated by the vertebral venous plexus (VVP). They found that increased IAP causes fluid flow across the brain surface, which could be the dominant driver of fluid flow in the awake brain.

A mathematical model was developed to simulate this process, showing a significant flow of fluid from the brain into the CSF-filled domain during a squeeze pulse. The filtration velocity and volumetric fluid exchange rate increased during the squeeze, with recovery occurring once the VVP mechanical stimulus was removed. The current model is simple and may need future improvements to accurately match the observed dynamics. The study suggests that brain motion could cause fluid flows out of the brain during VVP mechanical stimulus, potentially explaining why quiescence during sleep seems to be required for fluid flow through the glymphatic system. The permeability of brain tissue used in the simulations was 2×10−17 m2.

DISCUSSION

The discussion section of the paper suggests that the brain is mechanically coupled to the abdominal cavity through the vertebral venous plexus (VVP) in both humans and rodents. This coupling could generate brain motion within the skull and drive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow out of the brain into the subarachnoid space. Simulations suggest that this mechanical coupling can explain why injected tracers do not enter the cortex in awake animals but do so during sleep.

The study found that tension by spinal nerves during locomotion and respiration-linked forces are unlikely to have generated brain motion in the experiment, as observed brain motion occurred in the absence of changes in body configuration and during deep breathing under deep anesthesia. Simulations with a simplified brain geometry showed that brain motion of the observed magnitude could be induced by the force exerted by the VVP on the spinal cord.

However, there are several caveats to the study, including the fact that the mice were head-fixed, preventing normal forces generated by head motion from acting on the brain. Additionally, only the dorsal portion of the brain was imaged, and there may be movement and strain in subcortical areas and around the sagittal sinus. The model used a simplified, cylindrically symmetric anatomy and lacked active physiological regulation of the central nervous system.

The results indicate a potential link between the brain and visceral state, mediated by abdominal pressure. This work builds on previous research on brain motion driven by cardiac pulsations, respiration, and the Valsalva maneuver. Brain motion can be changed by pathologies such as intracranial hypertension, meningioma, and Chiari malformations, suggesting that brain motion could be used as a biomarker. Obesity, which elevates intra-abdominal pressure, may disrupt normal blood flow between the abdomen and spinal canal and/or lead to remodeling of the VVP.

The simulations also indicate the importance of accounting for the deformation of vascular compartments, such as the central sinus, in understanding brain waste clearance. Online content for the paper is available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02279-z.
