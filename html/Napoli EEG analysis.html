Final Summary

Title: Decoding Neuronal Criticality Firing Patterns for Large Brain-Based EEG Models

This pre-proof paper, published in NeuroImage (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.122082), aims to decode neuronal criticality firing patterns in large brain-based EEG models. The research focuses on understanding the underlying mechanisms of neural information processing during cognitive tasks, addressing limitations in extending the brain criticality hypothesis from cellular in-vitro models to whole brain in-vivo recordings.

The study employs advanced modeling techniques using Electroencephalography (EEG) data to analyze hierarchical arrangements of neural oscillations unique to cognitive tasks and psychophysiological impairments like hypoxia. This analysis allows for characterization and potential focus on critical events for open-world predictive state monitoring, particularly in human-machine interfaces.

The findings may contribute significantly to our understanding of brain function and potentially aid in the development of improved EEG models for clinical applications. The authors make their methods available on GitHub and encourage further exploration of the relationship between specific tasks and the hierarchical arrangements of neural oscillations. This research was funded by various institutions, including NASA Langley Research Center, National Institutes of Health, Air Force Research Laboratory Summer Faculty Fellowship Program, and the BREATHE Training Program. The authors declare no competing interests and have obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of NASA Langley Research Center. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and data and code supporting the study's findings are available upon request via the provided GitHub repository.

ABSTRACT

The abstract of this scientific paper discusses the challenges in extending the brain criticality hypothesis from cellular in-vitro models to whole brain in-vivo recordings, specifically focusing on the lack of evidence for multiple neuronal oscillatory groups, scale-invariance, and time resolution relevant to cognitive tasks. The paper also highlights limitations of existing methods such as cross-frequency coupling and the Kuramoto model, which while exhibiting common behaviors in critical processes, lack an explicit log-log plot to demonstrate scale invariance. The author emphasizes the need for a method that can address these issues simultaneously to fully understand how multiple neuronal oscillatory groups arrange or maintain stability over time during cognitive tasks.

The paper also mentions the limitations of microscopic scales in analyzing multiple neural circuits and oscillatory groups seen in vivo, and suggests a gap in the field regarding the extension of the brain criticality hypothesis from in vitro to in vivo demonstrations at the local field potential spatial scale. The Kuramoto index and detrended fluctuation analysis are used for comparison in spatial regions and time series within these models.

METHOD

The paper discusses a study that investigates neural oscillations in the brain using Electroencephalography (EEG). The method involves analyzing EEG data to decode brain connectivity by focusing on hierarchical arrangements of neural oscillations, which are unique to cognitive tasks and psychophysiological impairments like hypoxia. This analysis allows for characterization and potential focus on critical events for open-world predictive state monitoring, particularly in human-machine interfaces.

The study compares the distributions of these hierarchical arrangements between a non-hypoxic and hypoxic cohort, finding some unique patterns potentially specific to the non-hypoxic cohort. The authors make their methods available on GitHub and encourage further exploration of the relationship between specific tasks and the hierarchical arrangements of neural oscillations.
