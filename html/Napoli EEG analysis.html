Final Summary

Title: Decoding Neuronal Criticality Firing Patterns for Large Brain-Based EEG Models

Research Question: The study aims to advance understanding of neuronal criticality during cognitive tasks at millisecond to second time scales, by examining firing patterns within a large-scale EEG model and introducing a new method to provide evidence of power laws and scale-invariant relationships in multiple neuronal oscillatory groups.

Methods: The study uses Electroencephalography (EEG) data from 49 participants performing the Multiple Attribute Task Battery (MATB-II) under both non-hypoxic and hypoxic conditions. A novel method for analyzing EEG data is presented, which involves encoding neural models hierarchically and decoding the resulting time series. The study focuses on differences between in vivo and in vitro demonstrations of criticality, using the Kuramoto index for spatial region comparison and detrended fluctuation analysis for time series within it.

Key Findings: The study finds evidence for criticality, with multiple neuronal oscillatory groups self-organizing in a hierarchical arrangement that exhibits scale invariance. The distributions of these arrangements during the non-hypoxic and hypoxic cohorts across all tasks are scale-invariant based on the power law distribution. The study also finds unique hierarchical arrangements for cognitive processes, with differences between the hypoxic and non-hypoxic cohorts, and an increase in a particular hierarchical arrangement's occurrence for the hypoxic cohort.

Conclusions: The research advances our understanding of neuronal criticality during cognitive tasks by introducing a new method to analyze EEG data. The study encourages further exploration into the potential use of these hierarchical arrangements in machine learning-based models for characterizing cognitive tasks, and has implications for predictive state monitoring in human-machine interfaces. The research is funded by various institutions, including NASA Langley Research Center, National Institutes of Health, Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) Summer Faculty Fellowship Program, and BREATHE Training Program. Data and code supporting the findings are available upon reasonable request and can be found in a GitHub repository.

ABSTRACT

The abstract discusses a scientific paper that aims to advance the understanding of neuronal criticality during cognitive tasks at millisecond to second time scales. Current methods for demonstrating criticality have limitations, so a new method is introduced to provide evidence of power laws and scale-invariant relationships in multiple neuronal oscillatory groups within granular time scales. The research focuses on the dynamics of neuronal networks and the emergence of criticality in task-related cognitive signatures at the whole brain level.

The study expands criticality analysis from cellular models to local field potentials, addressing the number of interacting neuronal oscillatory groups, time resolution, and explicit log-log plots for evidence of scale-free behavior. While neural spike timings in vitro demonstrate log-log plots, the research is generalized using thermodynamic models like the Kuramoto model, which synchronizes multiple locations in whole-brain models. However, the Kuramoto model lacks an explicit log-log plot to demonstrate scale invariance. The study highlights differences between in vivo and in vitro demonstrations of criticality, with the Kuramoto index used for spatial region comparison and detrended fluctuation analysis for time series within it.

METHOD

The study presented in this paper explores the effects of mild hypoxia on cognitive tasks using Electroencephalography (EEG) data from 49 participants performing the Multiple Attribute Task Battery (MATB-II). The study introduces a novel method to analyze EEG data for characterizing brain connectivity and capturing critical events, particularly useful for predictive state monitoring in human-machine interfaces.

The method involves encoding neural models hierarchically and decoding the resulting time series. In this study, participants performed manual tracking tasks under non-hypoxic and hypoxic conditions. The data analyzed were collected during the manual tracking mode only.

The results of the study demonstrate evidence for criticality, with multiple neuronal oscillatory groups self-organizing in a hierarchical arrangement that exhibits scale invariance. The distributions of these arrangements during the non-hypoxic and hypoxic cohorts across all tasks are scale-invariant based on the power law distribution.

The study finds unique hierarchical arrangements for cognitive processes, with differences between the hypoxic and non-hypoxic cohorts. The diversity of these arrangements is lower in the hypoxic cohort, and there is an increase in a particular hierarchical arrangement's occurrence for the hypoxic cohort.

The study encourages further exploration into whether specific tasks create unique hierarchical arrangements and their potential use in machine learning-based models for characterizing cognitive tasks.
