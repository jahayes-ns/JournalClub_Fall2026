Final Summary

The review article "Adenosine, caffeine, and sleep–wake regulation: state of the science and perspectives" delves into the impact of adenosine and caffeine on sleep regulation, drawing from historical research to current understanding. The study examines how adenosine functions as a neurochemical signal for sleep homeostasis, increasing during wakefulness and decreasing during sleep, with caffeine serving as an antagonist to these receptors to promote wakefulness.

The authors investigate the effects of chronic versus acute caffeine consumption on sleep, delving into potential interactions between sleep homeostasis and circadian rhythms through adenosinergic signaling. They also discuss recent evidence suggesting that adenosine levels may influence circadian clock functioning, highlighting open questions in this emerging field of research.

The paper concludes by suggesting potential clinical applications for improving sleep-related disorders and stressing the need for further study to better understand the precise regulation and influence of adenosine and caffeine on sleep, particularly in relation to chronic consumption and circadian clock mechanisms. The paper does not generate new data, with no conflicts of interest declared among the authors: Carolin Franziska Reichert, Hans-Peter Landolt, and Tom Deboer.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This review article, titled "Adenosine, caffeine, and sleep–wake regulation: state of the science and perspectives," explores the influence of adenosine and caffeine on sleep regulation over centuries. The study began with researching the effects of varying doses of caffeine on sleep and wakefulness behavior around a century ago. Adenosine was later identified as a potential ligand for receptors where caffeine binds as an antagonist to reduce sleepiness.

The review discusses the current understanding of adenosine and caffeine's mechanisms in model organisms and humans regarding their impact on sleep. It investigates whether chronic caffeine consumption affects sleep differently compared to acute intake, evaluating the evidence for a direct role in sleep homeostatic mechanisms.

Recent evidence suggests that adenosine levels may also affect circadian clock functioning, leading to discussions about potential interactions between sleep homeostasis and circadian rhythms through adenosinergic signaling. The review concludes by discussing potential clinical applications of this knowledge for improving sleep-related disorders and highlighting open questions that need to be addressed to better understand the precise regulation and influence of adenosine and caffeine on sleep.

Keywords associated with the article include chronic caffeine, circadian, genetics, sleep deprivation, sleep homeostasis, and sleep–wake disorder.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the scientific paper discusses the roles of adenosine and caffeine in sleep regulation and circadian rhythms. Adenosine, a byproduct of ATP depletion during prolonged waking, is suggested to serve as a neurochemical feedback signal for sleep homeostasis, with levels increasing during wakefulness and decreasing during sleep. Caffeine, an antagonist of adenosine receptors, particularly the A2A subtype, promotes wakefulness by blocking these receptors.

The paper highlights that while daily modulation of adenosine levels might not directly reflect diurnal changes in sleepiness under normal conditions, it could still play a role when waking is prolonged beyond the normal daily duration. Caffeine consumption affects subjective and objective effects of sleep, sleep architecture, and the sleep EEG, depending on chronic caffeine intake, sleep pressure, and polymorphisms in the ADORA2A gene.

The paper also notes that longer sleep deprivation protocols may be necessary to determine if a single night of sleep deprivation is insufficient to influence core clock mechanisms in humans. Furthermore, the study of chronic adenosine antagonists, such as daily caffeine intake, and their impact on the circadian clock is a relatively new area of research with numerous unresolved questions.

The paper does not create or analyze new data, and all authors have declared no conflicts of interest. Contributions to the paper are attributed to Carolin Franziska Reichert, Hans-Peter Landolt, and Tom Deboer.
