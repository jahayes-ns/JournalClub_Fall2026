Final Summary

Title: Finding the Groove in Neural Space

The study "Finding the Groove in Neural Space" investigates the neural signatures of rhythm and tempo in human participants during rhythmic tapping tasks, aiming to understand how our brains process music. Research was conducted on two participants with spinal cord injuries using intracortical microelectrode arrays to record from the motor cortex.

Key findings suggest that motor cortex encodes position, velocity, and tempo-dependent differences simultaneously, and a continuous tempo axis exists in neural space that correlates with instantaneous tempo and finger velocity. Tactile feedback enhances motor cortex dynamics, while kinematic range and trajectory radius are moderately correlated within each condition but negatively correlated together. The research reveals a continuum of kinematic strategies during rhythmic tapping tasks, suggesting shifts from reactive to anticipatory timing mechanisms at lower and higher tempos, respectively.

The study suggests that the motor cortex encodes multiple rhythm-related features directly, providing insights into neural mechanisms underlying rhythm production and motor imagery. The research has implications for developing more effective brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and understanding human brain function. The preprint is available on medRxiv with DOI: 10.64898/2026.02.26.26347169.

ABSTRACT

This study investigates the neural signature of rhythm and tempo in human participants during rhythmic tapping tasks. The research utilized intracortical microelectrode arrays to record from the motor cortex. Findings reveal low-dimensional rotational neural dynamics, with radii varying in tempo-dependent manners and axes related to kinematic properties. Shifts from punctuated low tempo taps to smoother high tempo taps displayed a spectrum of kinematic and neural behavior. Interestingly, tactile feedback strengthened rotational dynamics despite reduced kinematic range. Tempo preparation was encoded orthogonally in the motor cortex, and switching tempos occurred with smooth neural transitions discernible only in higher dimensions. The study suggests that motor cortex encodes multiple rhythm-related features directly.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the universal phenomenon of music cognition across human cultures, focusing on the ability to extract beats or pulses from songs and align movements for music and dance. This process involves coordination of various neural structures, including the medial motor and premotor areas, basal ganglia, and cerebellum.

When people tap at a comfortable tempo, they produce beats with intervals between 500 and 600 ms (approximately 120 beats per minute, BPM), known as the spontaneous motor tempo, which is consistent with the preferred tempo of music across cultures. Recent neurophysiological studies in monkeys suggest that the neural representation during rhythmic tapping to visual metronomes depends on the neural population dynamics in the medial premotor cortex (MPC).

The study presented in this paper aims to investigate the neural signatures of rhythm in the hand representation of motor cortex in two human participants with spinal cord injuries, using intracortical microelectrode arrays. The research focuses on the dynamics associated with spontaneous and entrained tapping, the representation of continuously varying tempo, the interaction between kinematics and sensory feedback, and the dimensionality required to flexibly change between tempos.

RESULTS

The research study explores the neural dynamics of rhythmic behavior, focusing on the motor and tactile cortex's role in producing and encoding rhythm. Participants tapped at different tempos with or without feedback while neural activity was measured and analyzed.

Key findings include: 1. Motor cortex encodes position, velocity, and tempo-dependent differences simultaneously. 2. A continuous tempo axis exists in neural space that correlates with instantaneous tempo and finger velocity, and tactile feedback enhances motor cortex dynamics. 3. Kinematic range and trajectory radius are moderately correlated within each condition but negatively correlated together, suggesting lower neural firing rates in the no-feedback condition might cause smaller radii. 4. Participants could maintain tempo during preparatory periods with auditory cues, with a weak effect of tempo and interaction between tempo and period. Linear discriminant analysis revealed smooth untangled trajectories while alternating tempos. 5. The neural spaces computed for each task were similar, suggesting shared neural mechanisms across tasks. High decoding performance was achieved using a common 5-dimensional space, though individual task spaces rarely performed as well. The dimensionality of the signal saturated at approximately 10 dimensions.

In summary, tactile feedback plays a crucial role in kinematic control during rhythmic tapping tasks, and a common neural mechanism may persist across various tapping tasks.

DISCUSSION

The study expands on the understanding of rhythmic representation in human motor cortex during rhythmic tapping. Key findings include:

  1. Rotational dynamics observed in human motor cortex are modulated by both tapping range and amplitude, consistent with observations across various behaviors and animal locomotion.
  2. A single rotation is associated with each movement, suggesting these rotations are a core feature of motor cortex independent of movement type.
  3. The relationship between neural activity and kinematics is maintained regardless of the context.
  4. Contrary to previous NHP literature, the stable point appears to be at the apex of the tap cycle instead of the tap event itself, possibly due to changes in sensory feedback associated with moving at different tempos.
  5. A continuum of kinematic strategies was revealed indicating different cognitive approaches to rhythmic tapping, suggesting a shift from reactive to anticipatory timing mechanisms at lower and higher tempos respectively.
  6. The presence of feedback significantly impacts the magnitude of the observed dynamics, providing evidence that sensory signals are integrated into motor cortex.
  7. Surprisingly, no modulation was detected during the preparation phase of the tempo preparation task, which may be related to specific instructions or training effects.
  8. Both the single tempo and mixed tempo experiments raise questions about how we know when to tap and change tempo, with potential wait signals corresponding to the dwell period.

Limitations include reliance on data from a singular participant, although results are consistent with those of a replicated single-tempo experiment and previous NHP literature.

METHODS

This scientific study explores the use of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) to help individuals with disabilities control a computer using their brain activity. The researchers implanted microelectrode arrays in the motor cortex of four participants and connected them to a head-mounted device to record neuronal activity during various tasks, such as tapping at different tempos and imagining taps.

The study employs joint principal component analysis (jPCA) to identify patterns within brain activity data that can decode the intended actions of participants. Additionally, kinematic tracking was used to record hand and finger movement during tapping tasks, validating decoding results and studying the relationship between brain activity and motor output.

The research demonstrates that jPCA can decode intended actions in individuals with disabilities using BCIs and provides insights into neural mechanisms underlying rhythm production and motor imagery. The study has implications for developing more effective BCIs and understanding human brain function, with data and analysis available at BRAIN Initiative Data Archive (https://dabi.loni.usc.edu/dsi/XXXX) and code on GitHub (https://github.com/CorticalBionics/Groove).
