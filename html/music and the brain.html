Final Summary

Title: Finding the Groove in Neural Space

Research Question: This study investigates the neural dynamics of rhythmic hand tapping in humans, focusing on how the motor cortex encodes position, velocity, and tempo during tapping tasks with or without feedback, and the dimensionality required to flexibly change between tempos.

Methods: The research was conducted on two human participants with spinal cord injuries who had intracortical microelectrode arrays implanted in their motor cortex. The study involved various experiments such as measuring natural rhythm, single tempo, ramping tempo, feedback, imagination and continuation, mixed tempo, and employed techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Jacobi PCA (jPCA), and Chebychev filters. Data is available at the BRAIN Initiative Data Archive, and code can be found on GitHub under CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

Key Findings: During rhythmic hand tapping, both position and velocity are encoded simultaneously in the motor cortex, with distinct rotational patterns reflecting kinematic and tempo differences. A separate axis encoding tempo was found to be significant, particularly in predicting instantaneous tempo. Tactile feedback was discovered to influence these neural dynamics. Removing tactile feedback led to increased kinematic range but did not significantly affect the stability of taps. In other experiments, preparing for a tempo without tapping showed no effect on inter-tap intervals or neural dynamics.

Conclusions: The motor cortex directly encodes multiple rhythm-related features, with distinct rotational patterns for position, velocity, and tempo during rhythmic hand tapping tasks. Tactile feedback plays a role in modulating these neural dynamics. These findings could have implications for understanding motor control, learning, and rehabilitation. However, limitations include the majority of data coming from one participant, and some differences with previous non-human primate literature.

ABSTRACT

The study explores the neural signature of rhythm and tempo in humans by recording from their motor cortex during rhythmic tapping tasks. Results reveal low-dimensional rotational neural dynamics varying with tempo, linked to kinematic properties. Kinematic behavior evolved from punctuated low tempo taps to smoother high tempo ones. Contrary to expectation, increased tactile feedback strengthened rotational dynamics despite reduced kinematic range. Tempo preparation was encoded in an orthogonal dimension, and switching tempos was facilitated by smooth neural transitions discernible in higher dimensions. This research suggests that the motor cortex directly encodes multiple rhythm-related features.

INTRODUCTION

This introduction summarizes the study of music cognition and its neural mechanisms, focusing on the extraction and alignment of beats or pulses in music by humans, which is a shared phenomenon across cultures. The research investigates how the brain coordinates various neural structures to attend, analyze, and generate motor plans for music and dance. Tapping movements are commonly used to interrogate these motor plans, as people tend to produce beats with intervals between 500-600ms (approximately 120 beats per minute) when asked to tap at a comfortable tempo.

The study explores the neural representation of rhythm in the hand representation of motor cortex using intracortical microelectrode arrays implanted in two human participants with spinal cord injuries. The research aims to understand the dynamics associated with spontaneous and entrained tapping, the representation of continuously varying tempo, the interaction between kinematics and sensory feedback, and the dimensionality required to flexibly change between tempos. RESULTS

This study investigates the neural dynamics of rhythmic hand tapping in humans, focusing on how the motor cortex encodes position, velocity, and tempo during tapping tasks with or without feedback.

  1. During rhythmic hand tapping, both position and velocity are encoded simultaneously in the motor cortex, with distinct rotational patterns reflecting kinematic and tempo differences. A separate axis encoding tempo was found to be significant, particularly in predicting instantaneous tempo. Tactile feedback was discovered to influence these neural dynamics.

  2. Removing tactile feedback led to increased kinematic range but did not significantly affect the stability of taps. Interestingly, neural dynamics were smaller when tapping in air compared to tapping on a surface.

  3. The study found positive correlations within each feedback condition for a single tempo task, but a negative correlation when combined. In the absence of feedback, there was a decrease in firing rates in sensory and motor arrays due to the lack of tactile inputs during the No Feedback phase.

  4. In other experiments, preparing for a tempo without tapping showed no effect on inter-tap intervals or neural dynamics. When participants alternated between two beats with integer ratios, the trajectories smoothly transitioned between large and small radii corresponding to the primary and secondary tempos, but there was ambiguity in the neural dynamics at points where the tempos overlapped.

  5. Neural spaces were conserved across tasks, although with some misalignments. Decoding finger position across tasks was possible, but performance was better when decoders were trained on their own task space. A combined 5-dimensional space showed good cross-task decoding performance.

The study offers valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying rhythmic tapping tasks and suggests that shared neural mechanisms persist across tasks, but unique aspects exist for each task. These findings could have implications for understanding motor control, learning, and rehabilitation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, researchers expand on the literature regarding the representation of rhythm in human and non-human primate (NHP) motor cortex. The findings suggest that similar rotational dynamics are observed during rhythmic tapping in humans, with these rotations modulated by tapping range and amplitude. These rotational dynamics have been observed across various behaviors and in the spinal cord of locomoting animals. A single rotation was found for each movement, indicating a core feature of motor cortex independent of movement type. The authors also discovered a continuum of kinematic strategies for rhythmic tapping, potentially representing a shift from reactive to anticipatory timing mechanisms involving different cortical regions.

A surprising finding was that the magnitude of neural dynamics depended on the presence of feedback, suggesting sensory signals are integrated into motor cortex. Additionally, the study revealed a lack of modulation during the preparation phase of tempo tasks and limited behavioral variability in the continuation phase, raising questions about the neural mechanisms for knowing when to tap and change tempos. Excursions orthogonal to the rotational plane were interpreted as a wait signal corresponding with the dwell period.

Limitations of this work include the majority of data coming from one participant, but consistent results between participants support the robustness and consistency of the data. One distinct difference in results from previous NHP literature is that the stable point appears to be at the apex of the tap cycle instead of the tap event itself.

METHODS

The study is a preprint on brain activity and neural data processing in individuals with implanted electrodes. Experiments include measuring natural rhythm, single tempo, ramping tempo, feedback, imagination and continuation, mixed tempo, and computing various metrics from neural data. Techniques used are PCA, jPCA, and Chebychev filters. Data is available at the BRAIN Initiative Data Archive and code is on GitHub under CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. The preprint can be accessed via medRxiv.
