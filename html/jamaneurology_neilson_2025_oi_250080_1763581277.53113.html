Obstructive Sleep Apnea, Positive Airway Pressure, and Implications of Early Treatment in Parkinson Disease

Lee, Neilson, Montaño, May, Sicard, Cho, Iliff, Elliott, Lim, Scott | 2025

Final Summary

In this observational study led by Lee Neilson et al., the association between Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and Parkinson's Disease (PD) was investigated using data from the US Department of Veterans Affairs Corporate Data Warehouse EHR database. The research question focused on determining whether OSA is an independent risk factor for PD, and whether early treatment with Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) could potentially modify this association.

The study included a cohort of 11,310,411 veterans with a mean age of 60.5 years, and 1,552,505 were diagnosed with OSA. Over six years, veterans with OSA had 1.61 additional cases of PD per 1,000 people compared to those without OSA, even after adjusting for various factors such as body mass index and vascular comorbidities. This association was stronger in female veterans.

Notably, the study found that early treatment with CPAP significantly reduced case numbers, suggesting that effective screening measures and protocols for consistent adherence to CPAP could have a significant impact on brain health. The key findings indicate that OSA is associated with an increased risk of PD, and this risk can be attenuated by OSA treatment with positive airway pressure (CPAP).

The study concludes that OSA may be an independent risk factor for PD and could potentially be modified by early treatment with CPAP. Future studies are needed to confirm and extend these findings, with a focus on establishing causality and elucidating the underlying mechanisms. The limitations of the study include that adherence to CPAP beyond administrative codes was not assessed, and the decision to wear or tolerate CPAP may influence its effectiveness.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a study by Lee Neilson et al., the association between Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and Parkinson's Disease (PD) was examined using data from the US Department of Veterans Affairs Corporate Data Warehouse EHR database. The study included 11,310,411 veterans with a mean age of 60.5 years, and 1,552,505 were diagnosed with OSA. Over six years, veterans with OSA had 1.61 additional cases of PD per 1,000 people compared to those without OSA. The results held up after adjusting for various factors such as body mass index and vascular comorbidities. Notably, the association was stronger in female veterans. Early treatment with Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) significantly reduced case numbers, suggesting that effective screening measures and protocols for consistent adherence to CPAP could have a significant impact on brain health. The study concludes that OSA may be an independent risk factor for PD and could potentially be modified by early treatment with CPAP.

METHODS

In this observational study, the association between Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and the risk of incident Parkinson Disease (PD) was examined among US veterans. The study utilized an electronic health record (EHR)-based cohort design with data obtained from the CDW under the Portland VA Health Care System institutional review board using a waiver of participant consent. OSA was defined by the presence of ICD-10 code G47.33, and a random sample yielded a positive predictive value (PPV) of 94.0%. The primary outcome was incident PD, which was defined based on specific criteria with a PPV of 78.6%. The study found that OSA was associated with an increased risk of incident PD diagnosis, even after adjusting for competing risk of death, age, body mass index, and comorbidity burden. This risk was attenuated by OSA treatment with positive airway pressure (CPAP).

RESULTS

The scientific paper presents an analysis of a cohort of 11,310,411 participants, including 1,109,543 female veterans. Of these, 1,552,505 (13.7%) had obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), with 144,643 veterans having documented CPAP usage. The cohort was followed up for a mean of 4.9 years. Veterans with OSA had a greater cumulative incidence of Parkinson's disease (PD) compared to the OSA- group, beginning at the 2-year follow-up. The number of additional cases of PD per 1000 people due to OSA ranged from 0.51 to 1.61 at different time points during the follow-up.

OSA was associated with an increased risk of PD, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.92 compared to no OSA. A significant impact of CPAP on PD risk was observed in male veterans, with an HR of 0.68, while this was not significant for female veterans. The reduced risk of PD seen in the primary analysis persisted after adjusting for health care utilization in CPAP users.

The study also found that those with OSA had more outpatient visits prior to the index date compared to those without OSA, and the risk of incident PD persisted even after adjusting for this difference. A sensitivity analysis using a stricter definition for CPAP usage showed that greater adherence to CPAP did not change the risk of developing PD. Finally, the study found a statistically significant reduction in the rates of falls, fractures, and mortality in those with early CPAP use following the diagnosis of PD.

DISCUSSION

In a cohort study, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was found to be associated with an increased risk of Parkinson's Disease (PD). The association was evident as early as two years after diagnosis and grew stronger over time. The study utilized multiple high-specificity definitions for OSA, controlled for key covariates, and accounted for potential confounders, making it a significant advancement in the field compared to prior studies. This study also demonstrated that continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment within two years of an OSA diagnosis reduced the risk of developing PD. The benefits of CPAP were observed across multiple long-term milestones such as falls, fractures, and mortality. However, it is important to note the limitations of the study, including that adherence to CPAP beyond administrative codes was not assessed, and the decision to wear or tolerate CPAP may influence its effectiveness. Future studies are needed to confirm and extend these findings, with a focus on establishing causality and elucidating the underlying mechanisms.
