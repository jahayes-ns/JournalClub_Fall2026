Final Summary

The review "Neurologic mechanisms regulating cough in health and disease," published by the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology in 2026, explores the neurology behind both acute and chronic cough. The research question focuses on understanding the role of vagal sensory neurons and brainstem circuits in governing cough and the urge-to-cough sensation, particularly in chronic cough disorders.

The study methods involve a comprehensive review of literature, including clinical studies, brain imaging, and genetic research related to chronic cough hypersensitivity. The key findings reveal that chronic cough is associated with peripheral sensory nerve fiber sensitivity and central neural mechanisms amplification, leading to excessive coughing in response to low levels of thermal, mechanical, or chemical exposure.

Brain imaging studies suggest that chronic cough may be a disorder of reduced inhibitory control, implying potential for therapies restoring sensorimotor balance. The conclusions highlight the clinical significance of chronic cough and significant therapeutic advances in recent years for managing it. However, the lack of uniformity in responsivity to trialed drugs indicates the need for therapies directed at specific endotypic mechanisms or responsive treatable traits for effective cough management.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The review "Neurologic mechanisms regulating cough in health and disease" explores the neurology behind cough, a crucial defense mechanism. The study focuses on vagal sensory neurons and brainstem circuits that govern both reflexive and higher brain functions regulating cough and its associated urge-to-cough sensation.

The paper highlights that while acute cough is usually self-limiting, chronic cough can persist for prolonged periods, negatively impacting a person's quality of life. It emphasizes the clinical significance of cough hypersensitivity, which underpins both acute and persistent cough disorders. The review discusses the diverse neurological mechanisms implicated in disordered cough within clinical settings.

The article was published in 2026 by the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology and is available under a Creative Commons license.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the scientific paper discusses the mechanisms behind both acute and chronic cough, a common symptom in various respiratory and non-respiratory conditions. Acute cough is often caused by upper and lower respiratory tract infections, allergies, or environmental irritants, with inflammation being the primary mechanism. Chronic cough can be associated with several lung diseases like asthma, COPD, and pulmonary fibrosis, as well as extrapulmonary conditions such as rhinosinusitis and GERD.

The paper emphasizes the role of the vagus nerve in chronic cough, with disorders or dysfunctions potentially leading to persistent cough. Understanding these mechanisms could aid in developing targeted treatments for different cough endotypes. The Anatomic-Diagnostic Protocol has been developed to identify causes of chronic cough, but up to 40% of cases remain refractory despite extensive investigations. Genetic factors have been identified in some cases, such as the inherited autosomal recessive disease CANVAS causing neuropathy and spasmodic cough.

The paper also explores chronic cough hypersensitivity, a condition characterized by excessive coughing triggered by low levels of thermal, mechanical, or chemical exposure. Peripheral sensory nerve fibers are sensitive to various inflammatory mediators and can be directly activated or sensitized by these triggers. Central neural mechanisms also contribute, with amplified central sensory processing and changes in the central modulation of cough motor behaviors observed.

Brain imaging studies suggest that chronic cough may be a disorder of reduced inhibitory control, with patients showing an inability to volitionally suppress cough evoked by inhaled stimuli. The paper proposes that therapies restoring sensorimotor balance could be effective at controlling excessive cough.

CONCLUSION

The conclusion of the scientific paper emphasizes that while acute cough is generally self-limiting and does not require active intervention, chronic cough poses a significant healthcare challenge. Significant therapeutic advances have been made in recent years for managing chronic cough, with several drugs targeting peripheral and central neural mechanisms currently in clinical trials. These include purinergic P2X3 receptor antagonists, sensory neuronal-specific sodium channel blockers, menthol-related compounds, k opioid receptor agonist/mu-opioid receptor antagonist nalbuphine, and drugs targeting N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors or neurokinin 1 receptors.

However, the paper also notes a lack of uniformity in responsivity to trialed drugs, suggesting that cough itself may not be a homogeneous condition but rather composed of distinct endotypes. This implies that finding a universally effective cough therapy is unlikely and that therapies directed at specific endotypic mechanisms or responsive treatable traits may be needed for effective cough management.
