Review: Neurologic mechanisms regulating cough in health and disease

Mazzone, Pflaumer, Richards | 2026

Final Summary

This review titled 'Neurologic Mechanisms Regulating Cough in Health and Disease', published in 2026 by the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology, investigates the neurobiology of chronic cough, which can significantly impact quality of life. The research question focuses on understanding the vagal sensory neurons, brainstem, and higher brain circuits responsible for cough regulation, particularly in chronic cases.

The methods employed involve a comprehensive review of existing literature on the subject matter, focusing on acute and chronic cough, cough hypersensitivity, and neurological mechanisms contributing to disordered cough in clinical settings. The key findings suggest that various pathologies can cause chronic cough, including genetic conditions like CANVAS, and side effects of medications like ACE inhibitors.

The study also highlights the complexity of cough, with heterogeneous neurologic mechanisms contributing to disordered cough in clinical settings. Key findings indicate a need for personalized management of cough, moving away from viewing it as a simple symptom towards recognizing its potential primary problem status.

In conclusion, while acute cough can contribute to pathogen spread and requires targeted therapies with proven efficacy, significant advances have been made in managing chronic cough, with various drugs targeting peripheral and central neural mechanisms undergoing clinical trials. However, the study highlights a lack of uniformity in patient responses to these therapies, suggesting that cough may not be a homogeneous condition but rather comprised of distinct endotypes requiring tailored treatments. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The review titled 'Neurologic Mechanisms Regulating Cough in Health and Disease' explores the neurobiology of cough, a vital defensive response that can lead to significant clinical burden when chronic or hypersensitive. Acute cough is typically self-limiting, while chronic cough can persist for decades, affecting quality of life. The review focuses on vagal sensory neurons and complex brainstem and higher brain circuits responsible for the reflexive, behavioral, and cognitive regulation of cough. It highlights that heterogeneous neurologic mechanisms contribute to disordered cough in clinical settings. The review was published in 2026 by the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology under an open access CC BY license.

INTRODUCTION

The paper by Mazzone et al. delves into the complex physiology and clinical aspects of chronic cough, a persistent condition affecting quality of life for many patients. Chronic cough is often linked to vagal nerve mechanisms and can be caused by various pathologies, some genetic like Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome (CANVAS). Refractory chronic cough remains a challenge despite intensive investigations.

Chronic cough can also be a side effect of treatments like ACE inhibitors for hypertension, with mechanisms involving altered metabolism of sensory and inflammatory peptides. Genetic factors may contribute to susceptibility to these side effects.

The paper explores neurological mechanisms behind cough hypersensitivity, a state characterized by excessive coughing in response to low levels of thermal, mechanical, or chemical exposure. Therapies aiming to restore sensorimotor balance could be effective in controlling excessive cough. Understanding different cough mechanisms will be crucial for managing various respiratory and extrapulmonary conditions associated with persistent or troublesome cough.

CONCLUSION

The conclusion of this scientific paper emphasizes the complexity of cough as a symptom, particularly in chronic cases. Acute cough is often self-limiting but can also contribute to pathogen spread, suggesting a need for targeted therapies with proven efficacy, which are currently lacking. On the other hand, significant advances have been made in managing chronic cough, with various drugs targeting peripheral and central neural mechanisms undergoing clinical trials.

However, the study highlights a lack of uniformity in patient responses to these therapies, suggesting that cough may not be a homogeneous condition but rather comprised of distinct endotypes requiring tailored treatments. The paper advocates for personalized management of cough, moving away from viewing it as a simple symptom and towards recognizing its potential primary problem status, reflecting the advancements made in understanding the diverse neural processes involved in cough and cough hypersensitivity.
