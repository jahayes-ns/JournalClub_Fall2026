Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This document appears to be a research article published in Critical Care Medicine, a peer-reviewed medical journal. The article describes a study on the monitoring of cord tissue oxygen (pscto2) at the site of spinal cord injury in patients with acute spinal cord injuries.

The study was conducted on 26 patients with acute spinal cord injuries, who were monitored for pscto2 using an intraspinal pressure catheter and tissue oxygen monitor. The researchers also measured other parameters such as intraspinal pressure, spinal cord perfusion pressure, glucose, lactate, pyruvate, and temperature.

The study found that changes in pscto2 were associated with changes in Fio2 (fraction of inspired oxygen), Pao2 (partial pressure of arterial oxygen), intraspinal pressure, spinal cord perfusion pressure, and sedation level. The researchers also found that increasing the Fio2 from 0.38 to 0.82 resulted in a significant increase in pscto2, while decreasing the Fio2 had the opposite effect.

The study also found that pscto2 at the injury site was significantly lower than at a site below the injury. The researchers suggest that this could be due to ischemia or hypoxia at the injury site, and that monitoring pscto2 may provide valuable information for the management of patients with acute spinal cord injuries.

Overall, the article presents an innovative approach to monitoring the oxygenation of the injured spinal cord, which could potentially improve outcomes for patients with acute spinal cord injuries. However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and to determine the clinical utility of pscto2 monitoring in this population.

RESULTS

The study involved 26 patients, primarily males under 60 years old, with cervical, thoracic, and conus injuries. The patients were monitored for an average of 85 hours following a posterior or combined anterior-posterior surgery. Five patients experienced cerebrospinal fluid leaks, one had a wound infection, and various nonprobe-related complications occurred such as pneumonia, urosepsis, and pressure ulcers.

The cord tissue oxygen signal was analyzed, revealing both cardiac and respiratory frequency components. Altering the Fio2 significantly influenced cord tissue oxygen levels, with a sigmoid rise when increased and an exponential fall when decreased. A Michaelis-Menten saturation curve relationship was observed between increasing arterial oxygen partial pressure and cord tissue oxygen. The injury site had lower tissue oxygen than the cord below, with no correlation between the two.

Cord tissue oxygen was found to correlate significantly with intraspinal pressure, spinal cord perfusion pressure, tissue glucose, and tissue lactate/pyruvate ratio. Fever and blood transfusions also impacted cord tissue oxygen levels. Changes in cord metabolism were the most frequent cause of changes in tissue oxygen, followed by alterations in intraspinal pressure or spinal cord perfusion pressure.

Cerebrospinal fluid drainage had a variable effect on tissue oxygen, with no significant change on average. Lastly, patients with motor-complete outcomes spent more hours at low tissue oxygen values compared to those with motor-incomplete outcomes. An inverted U-shaped relationship was observed between limb motor score and the average tissue oxygen tension from the injury site preceding each motor examination in eight motor-incomplete patients at presentation.

DISCUSSION

The study by Visagan et al. demonstrates that monitoring tissue oxygen from the spinal cord injury site is feasible and correlated with neurological status, physiology, metabolism, and long-term outcome. Time spent below tissue oxygen thresholds was associated with poorer outcomes, similar to brain injury. The Licox oxygen probe was used, which detects oxygen diffusion from the adjacent cord or cerebrospinal fluid when the cord is compressed against the dura. The study provides a foundation for future research on whether intervening to alter tissue oxygen levels could be an effective new therapy for spinal cord injury. However, limitations include small patient numbers and complications such as cerebrospinal fluid leak through probe skin exit sites. Further studies are needed to investigate the impact of interventions on neurological outcome and determine optimal management guidelines for spinal cord injury.
