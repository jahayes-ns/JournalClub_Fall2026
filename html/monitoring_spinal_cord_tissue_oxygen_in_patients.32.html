Monitoring Spinal Cord Tissue Oxygen in Patients With Acute, Severe Traumatic Spinal Cord Injuries

Visagan, Hogg, Gallagher, Kearney, Zoumprouli, Papadopoulos, Saadoun | 2022

Keywords: Licox, microdialysis, monitoring, perfusion pressure, spinal cord injury, tissue oxygen

Final Summary

This scientific study investigates the monitoring and analysis of cord tissue oxygen (pscto2) levels in patients with spinal cord injuries (SCI). The research involves inserting an oxygen electrode at the injury site, enabling continuous monitoring.

The study discovered that changes in intraspinal pressure, spinal cord perfusion pressure, Fio2, spinal cord metabolism, sedation, and temperature influence pscto2 levels. Increasing Fio2 from 0.38 to 0.82 significantly increased pscto2 levels, while decreasing it from 0.89 to 0.41 resulted in a decrease. A significant correlation between Pao2 and pscto2 was observed, with higher Pao2 levels resulting in higher pscto2 levels.

The study analyzed data from 26 patients, primarily male, under 60 years old with SCI at varying levels. The cord tissue oxygen signal showed alterations influenced by FiO2, and the injured cord spends significantly more hours at low tissue oxygen values in patients with motor-complete compared to motor-incomplete outcomes at follow-up.

The study concludes that monitoring pscto2 provides valuable information about spinal cord status and may guide treatment decisions. It suggests further research is needed to fully understand the relationship between pscto2 levels and SCI outcomes, with potential therapeutic implications for interventions such as altering Fio2 or spinal cord perfusion pressure. The study was supported by various research foundations and institutions.

RESULTS

The study recruited 26 patients, primarily males under 60 years old with spinal cord injuries (SCI) at varying levels. Most patients had grade A or B injuries upon admission and underwent posterior surgery only or a combination of anterior-posterior approaches. Monitoring data was analyzed over an average of 85 hours per patient, with follow-ups ranging from 6 to 28 months.

Five patients experienced cerebrospinal fluid leaks and one had a wound infection during the study, but no spinal cord damage, hematoma, or meningitis were reported. Complications included pneumonia, urosepsis, pressure ulcers, pulmonary embolus, dysphagia, and others.

The cord tissue oxygen signal showed major cardiac and minor respiratory frequency components, with alterations in FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen) influencing cord tissue oxygen levels. Increasing the FiO2 by 0.48 caused a sigmoid rise in cord tissue oxygen to 71.8% above baseline within 8 minutes, while decreasing it by 0.44 led to an exponential fall in cord tissue oxygen to 79.0% below baseline within 6 minutes.

The injury site had lower tissue oxygen than the cord below, with no correlation between the two. Cord tissue oxygen was found to correlate significantly with intraspinal pressure, spinal cord perfusion pressure, tissue glucose, and tissue lactate/pyruvate ratio. Changes in intraspinal pressure or spinal cord perfusion pressure were often accompanied by drops in tissue oxygen, but factors such as changes in cord metabolism, FiO2, sedation, and cord temperature also contributed to changes in tissue oxygen levels.

Lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drainage had varying effects on tissue oxygen, with no significant change observed in nine of 11 patients. Lastly, the injured cord spends significantly more hours at low tissue oxygen values in patients with motor-complete compared to motor-incomplete outcomes at follow-up.

DISCUSSION

The study by Visagan et al. demonstrates that monitoring tissue oxygen (pscto2) from the spinal cord injury site is feasible and correlates with neurologic status, metabolism, and long-term outcome. Time spent below pscto2 thresholds was associated with poorer outcomes, similar to brain injury. The study used a Licox oxygen probe to monitor surface tissue oxygen, which showed sensitivity to cord tissue oxygen tension but less so when the cord is not compressed against dura.

The findings suggest that fluctuations in cord tissue oxygen may influence spinal cord function and that hypoxia or hyperoxia could potentially impair motor function. Interventions such as increasing FiO2 or spinal cord perfusion pressure may improve pscto2 and could potentially be therapeutic. The study provides a basis for future research to determine whether interventions to alter pscto2 are effective new therapies in managing spinal cord injury.

The probe insertion procedure requires surgery, and the main complication is cerebrospinal fluid leak through the probe skin exit sites. An alternative is to insert probes into the lumbar cerebrospinal fluid, but this does not effectively reduce intraspinal pressure or improve cord tissue oxygen at the injury site.

In conclusion, spinal cord tissue oxygen monitoring allows prompt detection and treatment of hypoxia after spinal cord injury, and further studies are needed to investigate whether such interventions improve neurologic outcome.
