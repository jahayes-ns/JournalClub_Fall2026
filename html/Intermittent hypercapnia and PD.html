The influence of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid flow and clearance in Parkinson’s disease and healthy older adults

Erhardt1, Mayer2, Lin3,4, Richardson3,5, Shaff2, Vakhtin2, Caprihan2, van der Horn2,6, Hoffman2, Phillips2, Grigg-Damberger5, Wang2, Gough2, Hobson7, Deligtisch5, Suarez Cedeno5, Sugar5, Ryman2,5 | 2025

Final Summary

This study, published in npj Parkinson's Disease, investigated the influence of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow and brain waste clearance in both healthy older adults and individuals with Parkinson's disease (PD). The research aimed to explore the potential use of intermittent hypercapnia as a disease-modifying treatment for PD, building on previous findings showing reduced clearance of brain waste by the glymphatic system is associated with neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in PD.

The study involved 63 participants, with 30 PD patients and 33 healthy controls (HC). Participants underwent Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) while experiencing intermittent hypercapnia, which involves exposure to low levels of CO2 in ON-OFF cycles. The study found that each CO2 exposure elicits a large-amplitude BOLD signal, indicating an increase in cerebrovascular activity, followed by a large-amplitude CSF inflow signal.

Although there was no significant difference between the degree of coupling or magnitude of response between groups, PD patients exhibited significantly reduced CSF inflow response to CO2 relative to HC (p = 0.012). In Study 2, an extended intermittent hypercapnia paradigm increased clearance of proteins and peptides implicated in neurodegeneration, such as alpha-synuclein, Neurofilament Light (NfL), Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), Aβ1-42, and Aβ1-40.

The study concluded that intermittent hypercapnia is safe and feasible for future clinical trials. However, further research is needed to understand the potential of this technique in managing neurodegenerative diseases like PD, establish optimal parameters, investigate individual differences, conduct long-term interventions, and assess its impact on clinical progression of symptoms. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a study published in npj Parkinson's Disease, researchers investigated the influence of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow and brain waste clearance in both healthy older adults and individuals with Parkinson's disease (PD). The study demonstrated that intermittent hypercapnia elicited vasomotion-induced CSF inflow in both groups, but the magnitude of this inflow was reduced in PD patients compared to healthy controls.

Intermittent hypercapnia, which involves exposure to low levels of CO2 in ON-OFF cycles, also increased the appearance of various brain byproducts, such as alpha-synuclein and tau protein, in both groups, suggesting that intermittent hypercapnia may be a potential treatment for clearing waste products from the brain. The study highlights the potential use of intermittent hypercapnia as a disease-modifying treatment for PD.

The research builds on previous findings showing that reduced clearance of brain waste by the glymphatic system is associated with neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in PD, and suggests that augmenting vasomotion may increase clearance of proteins implicated in neurodegeneration and serve as a disease-modifying intervention.

RESULTS

In this study, researchers investigated the effect of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow and its potential alteration in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients. The study involved 63 participants, with 30 PD patients and 33 healthy controls (HC).

Participants underwent Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) while experiencing intermittent hypercapnia. This paradigm consisted of cycles of increased CO2 levels followed by an OFF period. The study found that each CO2 exposure elicits a large-amplitude BOLD signal, indicating an increase in cerebrovascular activity, followed by a large-amplitude CSF inflow signal (Fig. 3).

The change in GM cerebrovascular activity (negative GMd/dt BOLD) and CSF inflow were highly correlated across participants, but no significant differences were found between groups regarding the degree of coupling, optimal lag, or magnitude of response. However, PD patients exhibited significantly reduced CSF inflow response to CO2 relative to HC (p = 0.012).

In Study 2, the researchers evaluated whether an extended intermittent hypercapnia paradigm could increase clearance of proteins and peptides implicated in neurodegeneration. The study found a significant positive effect on plasma total alpha-synuclein, Neurofilament Light (NfL), Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), Aβ1-42, and Aβ1-40 following the extended intermittent hypercapnia. The observed effects were significant at 45, 90, and 150 minutes post-intermittent hypercapnia for GFAP and NfL.

The study concluded that intermittent hypercapnia is safe and feasible for future clinical trials. However, further research is needed to understand the potential of this technique in managing neurodegenerative diseases like PD.

DISCUSSION

The study explores the use of intermittent hypercapnia as a method to activate and enhance the glymphatic system, responsible for clearing waste products from the brain. Intermittent hypercapnia led to an increase in CSF inflow and plasma biomarkers associated with neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's.

However, the study has limitations, such as quantifying CSF inflow only at the fourth ventricle/central canal level. CO2 administration methods varied across studies, highlighting the need for determining optimal CO2 concentrations, oscillation frequencies, session durations, and frequency of administration.

Individual differences in physiological responses might be influenced by factors like baseline cerebrovascular reactivity, disease stage, or underlying metabolic states. Future work should test optimal parameters while considering these factors to maximize therapeutic potential.

Long-term interventions are necessary to establish the necessary frequency of administration for clinical efficacy. Further research is needed on how intermittent hypercapnia can clear different pathogenic forms, its prophylactic and treatment uses, and impact on clinical progression of symptoms.

The study indicates that intermittent hypercapnia is safe and well-tolerated but requires safety and feasibility studies in more ecologically valid contexts. Future work may also investigate intermittent hypercapnia as a means to increase the sensitivity of blood-based biomarkers by increasing brain-derived proteins in the blood.

METHODS

This text appears to be a scientific research article that describes a study on the effect of hypercapnia (increased carbon dioxide levels) on the brain, specifically investigating the coupling between gray matter (GM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics.

The study involved 15 participants, 7 with Parkinson's disease (PD) and 8 healthy controls (HC). The participants underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) while being exposed to different levels of carbon dioxide. The fMRI data were processed to measure the GM response to hypercapnia (ETCO2-CSF), CSF response to hypercapnia (ETCO2-CSF), and the coupling between the GM and CSF (GMd/dt-CSF).

The study found that there was significant coupling between GMd/dt and CSF inflow in both PD and HC groups. However, the PD group showed a weaker coupling compared to the HC group. The study also investigated plasma biomarkers related to Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, but the results were not discussed in detail.

The safety and feasibility of the procedure were assessed, with participants rating their dizziness, stress, and discomfort levels throughout the study procedures.

Overall, the study suggests that there may be differences in brain dynamics between individuals with Parkinson's disease and healthy controls when exposed to increased carbon dioxide levels, and these findings could have implications for future clinical trials evaluating intermittent hypercapnia.
