Final Summary

The study investigates the influence of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow and clearance in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients and healthy older adults. The research question focuses on evaluating if intermittent hypercapnia can stimulate glymphatic clearance, a process that removes waste products from the brain, particularly in PD patients.

The methods involved two studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and blood samples to examine the response of gray matter (GM) and CSF to hypercapnia, as well as measuring plasma biomarkers such as Aβ1-40, Aβ1-42, α-synuclein, GFAP, NfL, and pTau217 in both groups.

Key findings reveal that intermittent hypercapnia elicited large-amplitude cerebrovascular activity followed by a large-amplitude CSF inflow signal, with a high correlation between the change in GM cerebrovascular activity (GMd/dt) and CSF inflow. However, PD patients exhibited significantly reduced CSF inflow response to intermittent hypercapnia compared to healthy controls. The extended intermittent hypercapnia paradigm increased the levels of plasma total alpha-synuclein, NfL, GFAP, Aβ1-42, and Aβ1-40 following the paradigm, with large effect sizes for GFAP and NfL.

The conclusions suggest that intermittent hypercapnia is a feasible and safe method for eliciting CSF inflow and potentially increasing the clearance of proteins and peptides implicated in neurodegeneration, but PD patients exhibit reduced CSF response to intermittent hypercapnia compared to healthy controls. The study proposes future work to compare protein and peptide clearance in a larger cohort of Parkinson's disease patients relative to healthy controls, determine optimal CO2 concentrations or targeted changes in ETCO2, and evaluate individual differences in physiological responses. The safety and feasibility of the study procedures should also be assessed in a more ecologically valid context outside of the scanner.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study investigates the influence of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow and clearance in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients and healthy older adults. Intermittent hypercapnia, a low level CO2 exposure in ON-OFF cycles, was found to elicit vasomotion-induced CSF inflow in both groups. However, the magnitude of vasomotion-induced CSF inflow was reduced in PD patients compared to controls. The study also revealed that intermittent hypercapnia increased the appearance of several brain byproducts, including alpha-synuclein and tau proteins, in the plasma of both groups, suggesting potential clearance from the brain. This finding highlights the potential use of intermittent hypercapnia as a disease-modifying treatment for PD. The misfolding and aggregation of alpha-synuclein are key pathological factors contributing to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in PD. Dysfunction of the glymphatic pathway, which relies on vasomotion to drive CSF through the interstitial space and clear waste from the brain, may play a key role in the pathophysiology of PD.

RESULTS

In this study, two studies were conducted to investigate the effects of intermittent hypercapnia on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow and biomarkers in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients.

Study 1: A total of 63 participants (33 healthy controls and 30 PD patients) underwent a ~5-minute intermittent hypercapnia paradigm while undergoing MRI BOLD imaging. The results showed that intermittent hypercapnia elicited large-amplitude cerebrovascular activity followed by a large-amplitude CSF inflow signal, and there was a high correlation between the change in GM cerebrovascular activity (GMd/dt) and CSF inflow. However, PD patients exhibited significantly reduced CSF inflow response to intermittent hypercapnia compared to healthy controls.

Study 2: In this study, participants underwent three sessions of intermittent hypercapnia while undergoing MRI BOLD imaging and blood collections at different time points. The results showed that the extended intermittent hypercapnia paradigm increased the levels of plasma total alpha-synuclein, NfL, GFAP, Aβ1-42, and Aβ1-40 following the paradigm, with large effect sizes for GFAP and NfL.

Overall, these results suggest that intermittent hypercapnia is a feasible and safe method for eliciting CSF inflow and potentially increasing the clearance of proteins and peptides implicated in neurodegeneration, but PD patients exhibit reduced CSF response to intermittent hypercapnia compared to healthy controls.

DISCUSSION

This study, published in npj Parkinson's Disease in 2025, explores the use of intermittent hypercapnia as a potential method to stimulate glymphatic clearance, a process that removes waste products from the brain. The researchers found an increase in cerebrospinal fluid inflow and plasma biomarkers such as α-synuclein, NfL, GFAP, Aβ1-42, Aβ1-40, and pTau217 during intermittent hypercapnia. While some of these biomarkers can be found in peripheral organs, the most robust effects were observed in GFAP and NfL, suggesting central nervous system origin.

The study suggests that intermittent hypercapnia could facilitate clearance of proteins and peptides implicated in neurodegeneration, including those associated with Parkinson's disease. Future work should investigate its potential benefits for Alzheimer's disease patients. The paper proposes that a larger change in ETCO2 (end-tidal carbon dioxide) may correspond to a larger increase in cerebral blood flow and corresponding CSF inflow, but techniques for administering CO2 varied across studies.

The study found all participants were medicated during the scan, suggesting future work should evaluate differences between medicated and unmedicated patients. Given the small sample size, further work is necessary with larger samples to evaluate associations between mechanisms of action and changes in plasma biomarkers.

Future studies should compare protein and peptide clearance in a larger cohort of Parkinson's disease patients relative to healthy controls, determine optimal CO2 concentrations or targeted changes in ETCO2, and evaluate individual differences in physiological responses. Additionally, the mechanisms activated by intermittent hypercapnia should be explored, such as during NREM sleep, and other potential mechanisms like failure of the blood-brain barrier and release from peripheral organs should be ruled out.

The study presents initial evidence that intermittent hypercapnia is safe and well-tolerated, but safety and feasibility studies are necessary in a more ecologically valid context outside of the scanner. Future work can also evaluate if intermittent hypercapnia may be useful for quantifying the efficiency of the glymphatic pathway and increasing sensitivity of blood-based biomarkers by increasing brain-derived proteins in the blood.
