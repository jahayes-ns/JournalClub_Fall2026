Human spinal interneurons repair the injured rat spinal cord through synaptic integration

Zholudeva, Agrawal, Fortino, Hurley, Kwong, Pelonero, Vila, Williams, McDevitt, Lane, Srivastava | 2026

Final Summary

The study by Zholudeva et al. published in Science Translational Medicine (2026) aims to develop a novel approach for treating spinal cord injury (SCI) by engineering human cervical V2a-enriched spinal interneurons (SpINs) from optogenetic channelrhodopsin-2 expressing human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). The engineered SpINs were transplanted into a rat model of SCI, where they formed functional synaptic connections with injured motor networks and improved motor recovery post-SCI.

The study revealed that the transplanted cells matured into multiple excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations, specifically identifying SLITRK5 and PLXNA3 as genes associated with V2a SpINs synapsing with the host phrenic motor network. The research holds significant potential for SCI therapy by combining regenerative medicine with intrinsic neuroplasticity.

Two separate studies explored the potential of human neural progenitor cells (hNPSCs) for treating SCI and improving diaphragm function during respiratory challenges. In the first study, hV2a interneurons were generated from iPSCs and transplanted into injured rat spinal cords, demonstrating functional recovery of locomotion upon optogenetic control. The second study found a positive correlation between the proportion of neurons in each transplant and diaphragm response under respiratory challenge.

Limitations discussed include potential risks of ectopic migration, variability in cell survival after transplantation, and the influence of local injury environment on differentiation and maturation of transplanted cells. Despite these challenges, the research offers promising avenues for developing therapies based on hNPSCs transplantation in treating spinal cord injuries and improving diaphragm function during respiratory challenges.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In the paper by Zholudeva et al., published in Science Translational Medicine (2026), the researchers developed a method to engineer human V2a-enriched spinal interneurons (SpINs) from an optogenetic channelrhodopsin-2 expressing human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) line.

After maturation, these SpINs were transplanted into a rat model of cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). The research team demonstrated that the engineered SpINs could form functional synaptic connections with injured motor networks in the host. In-vitro and post-transplantation studies using optogenetics, multiunit electrophysiology, pseudorabies virus tracing, and single-cell/single-nucleus transcriptomic analyses confirmed the maturation of the SpINs into multiple excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations. The study revealed specific genes associated with V2a SpINs synapsing with the host phrenic motor network, such as SLITRK5 and PLXNA3. This research indicates that human cervical V2a-enriched SpINs can establish functional host-transplant connectivity and potentially improve motor recovery post-SCI.

INTRODUCTION

The paper focuses on spinal cord injury (SCI), a devastating event without curative treatments. Current methods like rehabilitation and neuromodulation improve quality of life but do not address the underlying tissue damage. Regenerative medicine offers promising strategies for neural repair, but only partial functional improvement has been achieved. Combining regenerative medicine with intrinsic neuroplasticity may offer a more successful approach to SCI therapy.

Spinal interneurons (SpINs), essential for normal function and plasticity after injury or disease, are identified as potential candidates for plasticity and recovery, particularly VSX2-expressing V2a interneurons. These neurons are crucial for locomotor rhythm, respiratory circuits, and can be recruited into phrenic motor pathways after SCI.

The study differentiated a population of cervical VSX2-positive V2a-enriched SpINs from optogenetically engineered human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) and demonstrated their functional integration with the injured spinal cord, specifically in the phrenic circuit. The research aims to show that V2a-enriched SpINs can form functional synaptic connectivity with the host and donor tissue.

RESULTS

The combined texts detail two separate scientific studies that explore the potential of human neural progenitor cells (hNPSCs) for treating spinal cord injuries and improving diaphragm function during respiratory challenges.

  1. In the first study, hV2a interneurons were generated from iPSCs and transplanted into injured rat spinal cords. The transplanted cells maintained their identity and functionality, evoking locomotion upon optogenetic control. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that the majority of the hV2a SCIs had a cervical rostrocaudal identity. This research has significant implications for spinal cord injury treatments and neurological disorders.

  2. In the second study, human neural progenitors were transplanted into the cervical spinal cord injury site of female rats. Transplanted hNPSCs integrated with injured host axons, demonstrated improved diaphragm activity during respiratory challenges, and contained various cell types, including neuronal and glial-related or progenitor cells. The study found a positive correlation between the proportion of neurons in each transplant and diaphragm response under respiratory challenge. Synaptically integrated neurons were transcriptionally primed for establishing connections in the phrenic motor network, with adhesion molecules and genes related to synapse formation more highly expressed in the Exc5-2 cluster.

Both studies suggest promising avenues for developing therapies based on hNPSCs transplantation in treating spinal cord injuries and neurological disorders, as well as improving diaphragm function during respiratory challenges.

DISCUSSION

The research by Zholudeva et al., published in Sci. Transl. Med, focuses on repairing spinal cord injuries (SCI) in rats using human ventral-ventral ascending interneurons (V2a) derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). The transplanted neurons survived, integrated into host circuitry, and contributed to functional recovery of the diaphragm, particularly during increased respiratory demand. The study used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to analyze donor cells, revealing the formation of additional cell populations post-transplantation, including neurons and glia.

The research identified a set of synaptic targeting, organization, and signaling genes in transplanted neurons that integrated with the host spinal phrenic network. Some of these genes were previously expressed predominantly in lumbar V2a SpINs. Limitations discussed include potential risks of ectopic migration, variability in cell survival after transplantation, and the influence of local injury environment on differentiation and maturation of transplanted cells.

Additionally, a list of related research articles was provided, covering various aspects of spinal cord development, regeneration, injury, and disease models. These studies offer valuable insights into the complex mechanisms underlying these processes and potential therapeutic strategies for neural repair in spinal cord injuries.
