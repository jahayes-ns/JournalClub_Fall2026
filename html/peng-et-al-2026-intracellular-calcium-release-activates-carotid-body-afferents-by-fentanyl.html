Final Summary

Title: Intracellular Calcium Release Activates Carotid Body Afferents by Fentanyl through a Novel Kappa-Opioid Receptor (KOR)-G protein alpha q subunit (Gaq)-Phospholipase C (PLC)-Inositol Trisphosphate (IP3) Signaling Pathway

The research aimed to elucidate the cellular mechanisms underlying carotid body afferent activation by fentanyl, an opioid analgesic linked to respiratory depression. The study discovered a novel KOR-Gaq-PLC-IP3 signaling pathway in glomus cells of the carotid body that mediates afferent activation by fentanyl.

Methods employed included ex vivo recording of carotid body sensory nerve activity, primary culture of glomus cells, measurement of intracellular calcium concentration, heterologous expression of OPRK1 and Gaq in HEK293 cells, immunocytochemistry, immunoprecipitation assay, colorimetric assay for PLC activity, and competitive ELISA for IP3 levels. Data analysis included statistical tests for normality and homogeneity of variance, with appropriate parametric or nonparametric methods applied.

Key findings indicate that fentanyl induces Ca2+ release from intracellular stores in glomus cells, promoting KOR-Gaq complex formation and stimulating PLC, leading to elevated IP3 levels. This signaling pathway is crucial for CB afferent activation by fentanyl, potentially mitigating opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD).

The study's conclusions suggest that fentanyl activates the carotid body through a KOR-Gaq-PLC pathway, leading to calcium release from internal stores and activation of carotid afferent nerves. These findings reveal an unexpected interaction between opioids and oxygen-sensing circuits, offering strategies to reduce opioid-related respiratory complications and enhance opioid safety.

INTRODUCTION

The paper discusses Fentanyl's impact on respiratory depression (OIRD). It suggests that OIRD results from a dual mechanism: inhibition of brainstem respiratory neurons and activation of pulmonary C fibers. Carotid bodies, which monitor oxygen levels, are involved in this process. Fentanyl activates carotid body afferents via kappa-opioid receptors (KORs), and rats with intact carotid bodies experience mild respiratory depression, while those with denervated carotid bodies exhibit pronounced OIRD with apnea. The study also found that Fentanyl increases intracellular calcium concentration in glomus cells, the primary chemosensory elements of the carotid body, via a novel signaling cascade involving KOR, Gaq, phospholipase C (PLC), and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) receptors. Pharmacological blockade of any component of this pathway abolishes both the Fentanyl-induced calcium increase and carotid body afferent nerve activation. This research reveals a novel mechanism by which opioids modulate respiratory control.

METHODS

The study utilizes adult Sprague–Dawley rats to investigate the effects of fentanyl on carotid body sensory nerve activity and intracellular calcium concentration in glomus cells. Carotid body sensory nerve activity is measured ex vivo by recording action potentials from the sinus nerve, following collagenase treatment and perfusion with saline solutions containing various agonists/antagonists. Primary glomus cell cultures are prepared through enzymatic dissociation of carotid bodies and maintained in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with fetal bovine serum, insulin-transferrin-selenium, and penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine. Intracellular calcium concentration is measured using Fura-2 AM dye and calcium imaging. For heterologous expression studies, HEK293 cells are transfected with OPRK1 and Gaq plasmids, and immunocytochemistry is performed for visualization. An immunoprecipitation assay is used to investigate the interaction between fentanyl, OPRK1, and Gaq. To measure PLC activity and IP3 levels in carotid bodies, a colorimetric assay and competitive ELISA are employed, respectively. Data analysis includes statistical tests for normality and homogeneity of variance, with parametric or nonparametric methods applied as appropriate.

RESULTS

The provided text appears to be a scientific research article describing the mechanism by which the drug fentanyl activates the carotid body and induces carotid afferent nerve activation.

Fentanyl is an opioid analgesic that is used for pain management. In this study, the authors investigated how fentanyl activates the carotid body, a structure in the neck that plays a key role in the regulation of respiration by sensing oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.

The researchers found that fentanyl activates phospholipase C (PLC), which leads to an increase in intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) in glomus cells, the main cell type in the carotid body. This increase in [Ca2+]i is caused by the release of calcium from internal stores through activation of IP3 receptors. The authors also found that fentanyl activates Kappa opioid receptors (KOR), which then activate Gaq proteins, leading to PLC activation.

The study also showed that inhibition of either KOR or Gaq signaling blocks the increase in [Ca2+]i and carotid afferent nerve activation induced by fentanyl. The authors conclude that fentanyl activates the carotid body through a KOR-Gaq-PLC pathway, which leads to calcium release from internal stores and activation of carotid afferent nerves.

The research has important implications for understanding the mechanisms by which opioids affect respiratory function and could lead to the development of new treatments for respiratory disorders.

DISCUSSION

The study aimed to understand the calcium signaling pathway mediating fentanyl-induced activation of carotid body chemoreceptors. The results showed that fentanyl activates these receptors through kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) signaling, leading to glomus cell calcium influx and afferent activation. This mechanism, mediated by KOR and the Gaq pathway, serves to counteract opioid-induced respiratory depression.

Pharmacological inhibition of KOR, Gaq, phospholipase C (PLC), or inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) receptors suppressed both fentanyl-induced calcium signaling and carotid body afferent activity. A limitation of this study is that afferent nerve recordings were conducted in the presence of extracellular calcium, as its removal disrupts nerve conduction.

The findings suggest an unexpected interaction between opioids and oxygen-sensing circuits, revealing a novel respiratory control mechanism that may provide strategies to reduce opioid-related respiratory complications and enhance opioid safety.
