BDNF is necessary and sufficient for spinal respiratory plasticity following intermittent hypoxia

Baker-Herman, Fuller, Bavis, Zabka, Golder, Doperalski, Johnson, Watters, Mitchell | 2003

Final Summary

The scientific paper explores the role of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) in respiratory plasticity, specifically phrenic motor output following intermittent hypoxia. The research question revolves around identifying cellular/synaptic mechanisms of serotonin-independent respiratory plasticity and determining BDNF's role in phrenic long-term facilitation (pLTF).

Methods used include animal preparation, drug administration, induction of intermittent hypoxia, measurement of BDNF protein concentration using ELISA, quantification of BDNF mRNA levels via Quantitative RT-PCR, and various statistical analysis techniques.

Key findings reveal that intermittent hypoxia causes an increase in BDNF synthesis near phrenic motoneurons, which is necessary for pLTF. Intrathecal BDNF administration induces long-lasting facilitation of phrenic motor output, and Trk receptor activation is required for full expression of pLTF following intermittent hypoxia.

The study's conclusions suggest a direct link between intermittent hypoxia, BDNF synthesis, and spinal plasticity through the TrkB receptor. The findings could have significant implications for therapeutic strategies in treating clinical disorders such as Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), obstructive sleep apnea, and respiratory failure following spinal cord injury.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The preamble of this scientific paper discusses the central nervous system's role in regulating breathing through a flexible neural network, which adjusts respiratory motor output to accommodate life's varying needs. Over short periods, this flexibility is achieved by feedforward and feedback mechanisms, but for prolonged or permanent perturbations, the respiratory control system demonstrates plasticity or change in behavior based on experience.

In adult mammals, serotonin plays a significant role in various forms of respiratory plasticity, including sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), obstructive sleep apnea, and respiratory insufficiency following spinal cord injury. However, the cellular/synaptic mechanisms of serotonin-independent respiratory plasticity remain unknown.

One model of such plasticity is respiratory long-term facilitation (LTF), an enhancement of ventilation or respiratory motor output that lasts for hours after intermittent hypoxia. LTF in the phrenic nerve, which controls the diaphragm, requires activation of spinal serotonin receptors during but not after intermittent hypoxia. Although spinal serotonin receptor activation initiates pLTF, new protein synthesis is necessary to maintain it, and the identity of these proteins remains elusive.

This study focuses on neurotrophins such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), which play crucial roles in many forms of neuroplasticity. The research findings suggest that intermittent hypoxia triggers serotonin-dependent BDNF synthesis near phrenic motoneurons, and intrathecal BDNF administration induces long-lasting phrenic facilitation without hypoxia. Furthermore, BDNF synthesis is necessary for pLTF, as RNAi of BDNF translation abolishes pLTF. The authors propose that BDNF likely induces pLTF through activation of the high-affinity TrkB receptor, as inhibition of receptor tyrosine kinases abolished pLTF. In summary, this research indicates that BDNF synthesis is necessary and sufficient for pLTF after intermittent hypoxia.

RESULTS

The study investigates the role of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) in phrenic long-term facilitation (pLTF), a form of spinal plasticity triggered by intermittent hypoxia. The results suggest that:

  1. Intermittent hypoxia leads to an increase in BDNF synthesis in the ventral gray matter above the phrenic motor nucleus, and this increase is strongly correlated with the magnitude of pLTF.

  2. Intrathecal administration of BDNF elicits long-lasting facilitation of phrenic motor output, an effect that is blocked by a Trk receptor inhibitor, indicating TrkB receptor activation as the mechanism.

  3. The RNA interference (RNAi) technique was used to reduce BDNF mRNA translation following intermittent hypoxia in vivo, which prevented the usual increase in BDNF protein concentration. This indicates that endogenous BDNF is necessary for pLTF.

  4. Trk receptor activation is required for the full expression of pLTF following intermittent hypoxia.

In summary, this study suggests a direct link between intermittent hypoxia, BDNF synthesis, and spinal plasticity through the TrkB receptor.

DISCUSSION

This text appears to be a scientific article or research abstract about the role of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) in respiratory plasticity, specifically focusing on phrenic motor output, which controls the diaphragm muscle used for breathing.

The study finds that intermittent hypoxia (periods of reduced oxygen) causes an increase in BDNF synthesis and release within the phrenic motoneurons, likely facilitated by serotonin receptor activation. This increase in BDNF synthesis is necessary and sufficient for the development of phrenic motor output increase, also known as phrenic motor longitudinal facilitation (pLTF), which occurs in response to intermittent hypoxia.

The research suggests that BDNF may enhance excitatory synapses by increasing glutamate release, modifying NMDA receptors, altering glutamate receptor trafficking, depressing GABAergic transmission, or directly mediating membrane depolarization. However, it is unlikely to be the only protein involved in pLTF maintenance, and additional proteins may come into play as the duration and magnitude of pLTF change.

The findings have potential implications for understanding neurotrophins, their regulation, and their role in neuroplasticity. They could also have significant implications for therapeutic strategies in treating clinical disorders such as Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), obstructive sleep apnea, and respiratory failure following spinal cord injury.

METHODS

The scientific paper discusses the role of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) in the peri-limbic prefrontal cortex (pLFC) activation during intermittent hypoxia, or the "diving reflex." The methods used for the experiments include:

  1. Animal preparation with cannulas implanted into the pLFC for drug administration.
  2. Drug administration of methysergide, K-252a, and BDNF siRNA to investigate their effects on BDNF and pLTF activation during intermittent hypoxia.
  3. Induction of intermittent hypoxia in rats to simulate the "diving reflex."
  4. Measurement of BDNF protein concentration using ELISA and BDNF mRNA levels via Quantitative RT-PCR.
  5. Various statistical analysis techniques, including Student's t-test, ANOVA, and regression analysis, to compare experimental groups and make statistical inferences.

The study aims to investigate the effects of intermittent hypoxia and neurotrophins on respiratory motor control and plasticity, using rats as subjects. The research was supported by the Christopher Reeve Foundation, with ethical standards maintained throughout the experiments.
