A neuronal architecture underlying autonomic dysreflexia

Soriano, Hudelle, Mahe, Gautier, Teo, Skinnider, Laskaratos, Ceto, Kathe, Hutson, Charbonneau, Girgis, Casha, Rimok, Tso, Larkin-Kaiser, Gandhi, Amir, Kang, Vyza, Martin-Moraud, Lacour, Demesmaeker, Asboth, Barraud, Anderson, Bloch, Squair, Phillips, Courtine | 2025

Final Summary

The paper presents three interrelated studies that investigate autonomic dysreflexia (AD), a life-threatening condition often occurring after spinal cord injuries (SCI).

1) First, the research identifies key neuronal subpopulations involved in AD development through viral injections, optogenetics, chemical treatments, immunohistochemistry, FISH, 3D imaging, and snRNA-seq. Specific connections between these subpopulations, such as CalcaON neurons projecting to Vsx2ON neurons, were established.

2) Second, the study focuses on electrical stimulation (ES) of specific areas called "hemodynamic hotspots" in the lower thoracic region of the spinal cord to reduce AD severity and prevent worsening through improper targeting.

3) Third, a neuronal architecture investigation demonstrates daily autonomic neurorehabilitation can gradually eliminate AD and alter Vsx2ON neuron innervation responsible for AD in rats. Long-term application of the neurostimulation platform targeting the haemodynamic hotspot reduced the severity of orthostatic hypotension in participants with cervical SCI, with preliminary clinical outcomes indicating reduced AD severity in humans with chronic SCI.

The paper concludes by introducing an implantable system designed to restore hemodynamic stability post-SCI through electrical stimulation of lumbosacral nerves. The device modulates sympathetic nervous system activity and improves blood pressure regulation, showing success in animal models and one human participant.

The research demonstrates the potential for enhancing quality of life in individuals with SCI by mitigating AD complications like stroke and organ damage, while recommending further testing and refinement before clinical implementation. Additionally, an intervention showed no significant effect during phase one but exhibited improvement during phase two compared to the control group, prompting suggestions for further research.

Overall, these studies aim to understand neural mechanisms underlying various neurological disorders and develop potential treatments for them.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The combined preamble from your question summarizes three studies that explore different aspects of autonomic dysreflexia (AD), a potentially life-threatening condition often occurring after spinal cord injuries (SCI).

1) A study identified key neuronal subpopulations involved in AD development: SCHoxa7::Nfib::Vsx2, SCHoxa10::Zfhx3::Vsx2, CalcaON, Vsx2ON, ChAT–Cre, and others. These subpopulations have specific anatomical and functional connections, such as CalcaON neurons projecting to Vsx2ON neurons in the lumbosacral spinal cord, which then connect to sympathetic preganglionic neurons in the lower thoracic spinal cord.

2) Another study focused on electrical stimulation (ES) of specific areas of the spinal cord called "hemodynamic hotspots" in the lower thoracic region. Stimulation of these hotspots can produce pressor responses (increases in blood pressure) and reduce the severity of AD, while improper targeting could worsen the condition.

3) A third study investigates the effectiveness of autonomic neurorehabilitation in reducing AD in individuals with SCI using a rat model. Daily sessions of this rehabilitation led to the gradual elimination of AD and changes in the innervation of Vsx2ON neurons responsible for AD. The long-term application of a neurostimulation platform targeting the haemodynamic hotspot reduced the severity of orthostatic hypotension in participants with cervical SCI, and preliminary clinical outcomes provide evidence that daily application of this therapy reduces the severity of AD in humans with chronic SCI.

DISCUSSION

The paper presents an implantable system designed to restore hemodynamic stability post-spinal cord injury, utilizing electrical stimulation of lumbosacral nerves to modulate sympathetic nervous system activity and improve blood pressure regulation. The device, comprising a pulse generator, electrodes, sensors, and wireless communication, was tested on animal models and one human participant, showing successful modulation of the sympathetic nervous system and improved blood pressure regulation in both cases.

The results demonstrate safety, feasibility, and potential for enhancing quality of life in individuals with spinal cord injuries by mitigating autonomic dysreflexia complications like stroke and organ damage. Future testing and refinement are needed before clinical implementation.

In a separate study, an intervention showed no significant effect on the targeted biological process during phase one but exhibited improvement during phase two compared to the control group. The authors attribute this discrepancy to potential variations in the study population or methodological factors and recommend further research to validate the findings and explore clinical applications.

METHODS

The combined methods sections of the scientific papers discuss various techniques used to study spinal cord functions and autonomic dysreflexia (AD).

  1. In one paper, methods include viral injections, optogenetics, chemical treatments, tissue processing, immunohistochemistry, Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), 3D imaging, hemodynamic assays, electrophysiology, and single-cell RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq).

  2. Another paper focuses on two clinical trials using off-label and purpose-built implantable systems to restore haemodynamic stability in individuals with autonomic failure. The system consists of a purpose-built IPG (ARCIM Thoracic System), wearable hub, and either off-label paddle leads or novel purpose-built paddle leads.

  3. A research article on neuronal architecture involved in AD describes four nodes: SCLUMBAR::Vsx2, SCTHORACIC::Vsx2, ChatON preganglionic sympathetic neurons, and the superior cervical ganglion (SCG). Experiments use chemogenetic and optogenetic techniques to manipulate these neurons.

  4. The effects of Electrical Stimulation (ES) on the recovery of AD in animals with spinal cord injuries are discussed in another paper. ES is applied to specific areas of the spinal cord to restore communication between certain neurons and improve blood pressure regulation.

  5. One study compares the prevalence of autonomic dysreflexia in individuals with spinal cord injuries using data from the Spinal Cord Injury Community Survey (SCICS). The results showed a significant difference between groups, with 38.4% of SCI participants experiencing AD.

These papers aim to understand neural mechanisms underlying various neurological disorders and develop potential treatments for them.
