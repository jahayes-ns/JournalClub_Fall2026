Astrocyte activation in the ventrolateral medulla modulates breathing and arousal states Luiz Marcelo Oliveira

Oliveira, Miranda, Lim, Severs, Takakura, Moreira, Li, Kalume, Ramirez

Final Summary

This scientific paper investigates the role of astrocytes, brain cells that regulate physiological functions, in modulating breathing and arousal states during sigh generation in alert mice, specifically focusing on those located in the ventral respiratory column (VRC). The study reveals that a specific subset of Aldh1l1 cells within the VRC are activated before sighing and can be recruited by hypoxia (low oxygen levels). Activating these astrocytes through chemogenetic or optogenetic methods increased the probability of inducing arousals accompanied by sighs. The research suggests that medullary astrocytes play a crucial role in modulating sigh generation and arousal transitions during hypoxia, thereby contributing to the ventilatory and arousal response to low oxygen levels.

The study was conducted on mice through viral injections to express genetically encoded calcium indicators or channelrhodopsin-2, followed by optical stimulation and recording of neural activity and respiratory parameters. The methods included two-photon astrocytes calcium imaging, electrophysiology and cross-correlation analyses, CaMPARI photoconversion in freely behaving mice, histology, in situ hybridization, statistical analysis, and data reporting. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper explores the role of astrocytes, brain cells that regulate various physiological functions, in modulating breathing and arousal states, specifically during sigh generation in alert mice. The study focuses on astrocytes located in the ventral respiratory column (VRC), a crucial region for breathing regulation.

The researchers found that a specific subset of Aldh1l1 cells within the VRC are activated before sighing and can be recruited by hypoxia (low oxygen levels). Activating these astrocytes using chemogenetic or optogenetic methods increased the probability of inducing arousals accompanied by sighs. The study also demonstrated that activating Aldh1l1 astrocytes led to increased calcium transients in catecholaminergic neurons in the VRC, immediately preceding arousal with sighs.

The findings suggest that medullary astrocytes play a crucial role in modulating sigh generation and arousal transitions during hypoxia, thereby contributing to the ventilatory and arousal response to low oxygen levels. This research sheds light on the importance of astrocytes in maintaining respiratory and arousal homeostasis.

RESULTS

The study explores the interaction between astrocytes and neurons in the ventral respiratory column (VRC) of mice, focusing on their role in respiratory control and arousal.

  1. Optogenetic stimulation of Aldh1l1-positive astrocytes in the VRC activated 58.7 ± 10.4 neurons expressing a red fluoroprotein, suggesting physiological activation. This was observed during wakefulness, NREM, and REM sleep, leading to increased respiratory frequency (f) and tidal volume (V), but without inducing sighs or arousal.
  2. Contrastingly, astrocyte-driven sigh generation and arousal in the VRC require P2Y1 signaling. Optogenetic activation of VRC astrocytes reliably evoked sighs, which was preserved following selective blockade of Kir4.1 channels but abolished after combined administration of the Kir4.1 blocker VU0134992 and P2Y1 receptor antagonist MRS2279.
  3. Astrocytes in the VRC were also found to be activated during hypoxia, leading to an increase in astrocyte Fos expression.

In summary, this research suggests that there is functional signaling from astrocytes to neurons within the VRC, playing a role in regulating respiration and arousal, but this signaling pathway may differ depending on the specific conditions (hypoxia vs. normoxia) and the presence or absence of certain signaling molecules like P2Y1 and Kir4.1 channels.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the role of astrocytes in hypoxia and arousal followed by sighs was explored in freely behaving mice, with a focus on astrocyte populations in the VRC and RTN. Astrocytes in the preBötC increased ventilation (V) and induced arousal, especially during NREMS and REMS, leading to sighs. Astrocyte stimulation in the RTN did not induce sighs or arousal but increased eupneic breathing. The RTN plays a critical role in central chemoreception, enhancing respiratory response to CO₂ changes. Astrocytes and catecholaminergic C1 neurons interact in the VRC, where astrocyte activation without C1 neurons still evoked sighs and arousal albeit with delay. Pharmacological experiments identified purinergic signaling through astrocytes as essential components of this pathway. The depletion of around 77% of astrocytes in the VRC did not abolish the hypoxic ventilatory response, suggesting a complementary role for central medullary astrocytes in oxygen sensing by the carotid bodies and modulating adaptive responses to low oxygen environments. The study supports the notion that astrocytes are active participants in regulating rhythmic behaviors, chemoreception, arousal, and sleep/wake cycles.

METHODS

The scientific paper details an experiment where mice were injected with viruses to express genetically encoded calcium indicators (GCaMP8m) or channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), and then implanted with optic fibers and electrodes for optical stimulation and recording of neural activity and respiratory parameters. The study aimed to investigate the role of specific genes in the ventral medulla oblongata (VMO) in regulating respiratory rhythm.

The experiment included baseline recordings, optogenetic stimulations, blocking potassium channels (Kir4.1) and P2Y1 receptors, and measuring sigh frequencies. Activating certain neurons increased the frequency of sighs, suggesting a role in respiratory rhythm regulation. Blocking Kir4.1 and P2Y1 receptors decreased sigh frequencies, further supporting this role.

The methods used include two-photon astrocytes calcium imaging, electrophysiology and cross-correlation analyses, CaMPARI photoconversion in freely behaving mice, histology, in situ hybridization, statistical analysis, and data reporting.

Detailed procedures involved two-photon astrocytes calcium imaging using a Bruker Investigator microscope, electrophysiology and cross-correlation analyses with specific ROIs and definitions of sighs, CaMPARI photoconversion with fluorescence intensity ratios to classify neurons, histology including brain fixation, sectioning, and immunohistochemistry using specific antibodies. In situ hybridization utilized the RNAscope™Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent kit for detection of Aldh1l1 and Fos.

Statistical analysis was performed using Sigma Stat version 11.0 software, with tests including t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA as appropriate. Data were reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

All data generated or analyzed in this study are included in the main text or supplementary information, with raw data available upon request from the corresponding authors.
