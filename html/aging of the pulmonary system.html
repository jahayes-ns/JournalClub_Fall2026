The crucial role of normative ageing in respiratory pathogenesis

Weight, Tatler, Huckstepp*, Adcock, Barnes, van Beek, Christopher, Clifford, Dhasmana, Hart, Hashmat, Noble, Sayers, Short, Stinson, Calimport†, Bentley†

Final Summary

In this review, an international consortium led by Caroline M Weight et al. explores the impact of normal aging on respiratory function, focusing on structural changes in the aging respiratory system that affect lung function and identifying genetic and molecular factors contributing to age-related loss of respiratory capacity. The study discusses various aspects of aging in the lung, including changes in cellular secretions, alterations in antimicrobial peptides, changes in the extracellular matrix, and increases in reactive oxygen species.

Key findings include a reduction in defense mechanisms such as mucociliary clearance, coughing, and macrophage function due to aging, leading to increased susceptibility to diseases like sleep apnea, COPD, and IPF. The authors emphasize the need for further study of human tissue samples free from current diseases to clarify the genetic and molecular mechanisms behind lung aging.

Animal models are extensively used in research on lung aging, with many crucial areas still unexplored, such as the ageing of the mucociliary system in humans. The authors suggest that managing lung aging requires a holistic, multiprofessional approach due to its psychosocial and physical implications, and they recommend early interventions to maintain lung health and slow or reverse age-related decline.

In conclusion, the paper highlights the importance of understanding how biological aging affects the respiratory system, recognizing disease-accelerated aging markers, and developing interventions to promote health across all age groups, addressing future population health challenges. The review also discusses naturally occurring airborne substances like radon and their impact on lung health, particularly in older adults.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In this review, an international consortium explores the impact of normal aging on respiratory function. The study covers structural changes in the aging respiratory system that affect lung function and identifies genetic and molecular factors contributing to age-related loss of respiratory capacity. Understanding how biological aging affects the respiratory system and recognizing disease-accelerated aging markers are essential for developing interventions to promote health across all age groups, addressing future population health challenges. The review is authored by members of the ICCARP respiratory working group led by Caroline M Weight, Amanda L Tatler, Robert T R Huckstepp, and Barry L Bentley.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the effects of aging on the respiratory system, focusing on factors like physiological changes, genetics, epigenetics, and inflammation that contribute to age-related impairments in lung function. These factors impact sleep-disordered breathing, particularly in older individuals and those with neurodegenerative diseases.

The paper further delves into the concept of "inflammaging," or chronic low-grade inflammation associated with aging, which contributes to many age-related disorders. It explores various aspects of aging in the lung, such as changes in cellular secretions, alterations in antimicrobial peptides, changes in the extracellular matrix, and increases in reactive oxygen species.

The paper suggests that targeting chronic inflammation and immune dysfunction could be a promising approach for preventing and treating age-related lung diseases. Senotherapies, drugs that target the aging process, are discussed as potential treatments, including senomorphics and senolytics. However, only a few clinical studies have been reported to date.

The paper also mentions the challenges in clinical studies of geroprotective therapies due to the slow progression of these diseases and the need for identifying clinical biomarkers of lung aging. It highlights the importance of understanding the effects of aging on the lung and the role of inflammaging in age-related lung disorders, and the need for further research in this area.

Additionally, the paper discusses naturally occurring airborne substances like radon and their impact on lung health, particularly in older adults.

DISCUSSION

The discussion section of the paper highlights that respiratory system aging leads to reduced function and increased susceptibility to diseases like sleep apnea, COPD, and IPF. This decline is attributed to a reduction in defense mechanisms such as mucociliary clearance, coughing, and macrophage function, increased respiratory infections, decreased autophagy, replicative senescence, and chronic inflammation.

The understanding of lung aging primarily comes from studies on other organ systems or later-onset diseases rather than direct assessments from lung tissue. Furthermore, much of the genetic and molecular mechanisms are derived from COPD patients, raising questions about whether these mechanisms fully replicate normative lung aging. The authors emphasize the need for further study of human tissue samples free from current diseases to clarify this uncertainty.

Animal models are extensively used in research on lung aging, with many crucial areas still unexplored, such as the ageing of the mucociliary system in humans. To address these gaps, there is a need for comprehensive characterization of upper and lower respiratory tract mucociliary function across different life stages to uncover molecular mechanisms behind decline and its influence on respiratory regulation.

Advances in genetic studies have identified potential causal genes associated with lung impairment; however, distinguishing between direct effects of biological aging and indirect effects through other age-related pathologies remains challenging. The paper suggests defining pulmonary fibrosis driven by senescence as an ageing-related pathology to meet the criteria for an ageing-related disease as defined by the ICCARP.

The authors also highlight that managing lung aging requires a holistic, multiprofessional approach due to its psychosocial and physical implications. They suggest that early interventions to maintain lung health and slow or reverse age-related decline are valuable given the substantial burden of established or worsening lung disease.

Lastly, the paper discusses the need for earlier and longer intervention during respiratory infections in older adults while balancing the risks associated with steroids and antibiotics.
