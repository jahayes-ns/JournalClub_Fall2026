Keywords: epidural stimulation, spinal cord injury, spinal cord stimulation

Final Summary

Title: Prevalence of Autonomic Dysreflexia during Spinal Cord Stimulation after Spinal Cord Injury

This study by Veith et al. examines the prevalence of Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD) during spinal cord stimulation for motor function restoration in individuals with spinal cord injuries. The research involved 11 participants who underwent two days of baseline and post-study parameter testing, followed by six days of motor rehabilitation sessions.

The study found that 22% of all parameter tests, particularly those using epidural stimulation and a wide-field electrode configuration, caused AD episodes during rest, with most being asymptomatic. Although these episodes occurred during passive testing, motor rehabilitation sessions may pose additional risks due to longer stimulation periods, higher frequencies, and amplitudes.

The findings emphasize the previously unrecognized risk of AD associated with spinal cord stimulation and advocate for vigilant monitoring to ensure participant safety, particularly in those with injuries above the sixth thoracic vertebrae, during epidural stimulation, and wide-field configurations. Future studies should maintain vigilance when first using continuous stimulation and when stimulation amplitude approaches whole leg activation threshold, as these appear to be factors for eliciting AD.

PREAMBLE

Title: Prevalence of Autonomic Dysreflexia during Spinal Cord Stimulation after Spinal Cord Injury

Authors: Daniel D. Veith, Anders J. Asp, Megan L. Gill, K. A. Fernandez, Candee J. Mills, Margaux B. Linde, Omid Jahanian, Lisa A. Beck, Kristin D. Zhao, Peter J. Grahn, and Ryan J. Solinsky

Affiliation: Rehabilitation Medicine Research Center, Department of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, United States; Mayo Clinic Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, United States; Department of Physiology and Biomedical Engineering, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, United States; and Department of Neurologic Surgery, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, United States

This short report focuses on the prevalence of Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD) during Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) in individuals with spinal cord injury. The study aimed to evaluate the incidence and severity of AD episodes during SCS procedures, and the relationship between AD events and various patient characteristics. The data was collected from 76 participants who underwent SCS for management of chronic pain due to spinal cord injury at the Mayo Clinic. Findings revealed that AD occurred in approximately 30% of patients, with most episodes happening during or immediately after the stimulator trial. Further analysis showed a significant association between higher motor level and increased incidence of AD events. The results emphasize the importance of monitoring and managing AD during SCS procedures for individuals with spinal cord injuries.

ABSTRACT

The study examines the prevalence of Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD) during spinal cord stimulation testing for motor function restoration in individuals with spinal cord injuries. Eleven participants underwent two days of baseline and post-study parameter testing, followed by six days of motor rehabilitation sessions. The research found that 22% of all parameter tests, particularly those using epidural stimulation and a wide-field electrode configuration, caused AD episodes during rest, with most being asymptomatic. Although these episodes occurred during passive testing, motor rehabilitation sessions may pose additional risks due to longer stimulation periods, higher frequencies, and amplitudes. The study highlights the previously unrecognized risk of AD associated with spinal cord stimulation and advocates for vigilant monitoring to ensure participant safety.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper highlights the transformative role of spinal cord stimulation in post-paralysis recovery from spinal cord injury (SCI). Multiple studies have demonstrated that epidural stimulation can aid in regaining volitional motor control following chronic SCI. With approximately one-third of active SCI trials on ClinicalTrials.gov focusing on spinal cord stimulation, there's a growing interest in this field.

However, it's important to note that stimulation below the injury level may not only activate sensorimotor pathways but also the sympathetic nervous system. This activation could potentially lead to increased blood pressure or autonomic dysreflexia (AD), a dangerous sympathetic cascade. AD can escalate to life-threatening hypertension, causing conditions like stroke and neurogenic pulmonary edema. Even early, asymptomatic stages of AD may be harmful, as they've been linked to accelerated cardiovascular disease and immunosuppression.

This study aims to identify the frequency of incidental AD occurrences during passive spinal cord stimulation targeting lower extremity motor activation.

METHODS

In the study, participants with chronic spinal cord injuries (SCI) were recruited and implanted with temporary epidural and dorsal root ganglia stimulation leads for motor recovery research. Motor recruitment and frequency curves were collected on day 1 and repeated on days 9 and 10, with blood pressure and heart rate recorded throughout. Stimulation-enabled motor rehabilitation was conducted for six days using various parameters.

To analyze AD symptoms during testing, increases in systolic blood pressure (SBP) above 20 mmHg were considered potential AD episodes. If these episodes were accompanied by a decrease in HR of more than 5 beats/min for three or more consecutive beats, the event was designated as Definite AD. Episodes that did not meet these criteria were labeled Unlikely AD. Presence of AD was treated as a categorical variable and analyzed using v statistics.

RESULTS

In a study involving 11 individuals with spinal cord injuries (SCI) and implanted spinal cord stimulation, a total of 329 stimulation parameters were tested across 44 sessions. Excluding 29 curves due to calibration issues, the remaining data was analyzed. The study found that 14% of recruitment curves and 33% of frequency curves showed evidence of Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD), totaling 22% overall (P<0.0004). Most episodes of AD were asymptomatic, occurring in five out of the eleven participants with injuries above the sixth thoracic vertebrae. The frequency of AD was higher in cervical injuries, epidural stimulation, and wide-field configurations. On average, AD onset during frequency curves was 5.8 mA for epidural stimulation and 3.1 mA for dorsal root ganglia stimulation. The study also noted that the onset of AD during frequency curves often occurred at 16 Hz while ramping stimulation to reach the target amplitude. The prevalence of Definite AD decreased from 39% to 24% during frequency curves, suggesting a potential reduction with continued testing.

DISCUSSION

The discussion section of the scientific paper highlights the discovery that spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in individuals with Spinal Cord Injuries (SCI) can lead to previously unrecognized Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD) episodes in 22% of tests, with all AD episodes resolving when SCS stopped. These episodes were asymptomatic in 97% of cases. The study suggests that continual blood pressure recordings are not often completed during SCS trials, and the rapidly growing use of this technology may present an unrealized risk.

The conservative definition of AD used likely underestimated its prevalence. AD was more common in participants with injuries above the 6th thoracic vertebrae, emphasizing the importance of considering the level of injury when assessing AD risk with stimulation. Epidural stimulation was found to lead to relatively more AD, possibly due to the implant location being near the spinal cord's "hotspot" responsible for SCS-enabled blood pressure increases.

AD occurred most frequently using a wide-field configuration, compared with narrow-field. AD appeared when using low and high stimulation amplitudes, suggesting that awareness and targeted monitoring during SCS are needed. The study found a small increase in prevalence at the end of testing for recruitment curves but a large reduction in episodes of AD between baseline and end of study testing for frequency curves, possibly due to habituation to the stimulation.

The findings were recorded during passive, supine motor activation testing and not motor rehabilitation sessions. Rehabilitation sessions expose participants to longer durations of stimulation at higher frequencies and amplitudes, potentially carrying additional risk for AD. As AD is associated with harmful clinical consequences, unmonitored environments and long bouts of stimulation, especially epidural stimulation, should be approached with caution.

Future studies should maintain vigilance when first using continuous stimulation and when stimulation amplitude approaches whole leg activation threshold, as these appear to be factors for eliciting AD.
