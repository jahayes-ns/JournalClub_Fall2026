Aquaporin-4 mis-localization slows glymphatic clearance of α-synuclein and promotes α-synuclein pathology and aggregate propagation

Braun, Simon, Jang, Sanderson, Swierz, Sevao, Pincus, Schaser, Elliott, Lim, Unni, Schindler, Keene, Latimer, Iliff | 2024

Keywords: Glymphatic, aquaporin-4, AQP4, Astrocytes, Perivascular endfeet, α-syntrophin, α-synuclein, propagation, PFFs, Lewy body dementia

Final Summary

In this preprint study, researchers explore the link between mislocalization of aquaporin-4 (AQP4), glymphatic clearance of alpha-synuclein, and the progression of synucleinopathies such as Parkinson's disease and Dementia with Lewy bodies. The study found that neocortical Lewy body pathology is associated with perivascular AQP4 mislocalization in human postmortem brain tissue. In a mouse model, the loss of perivascular AQP4 localization impaired glymphatic function, affecting the clearance of alpha-synuclein from brain tissue to cerebrospinal fluid, and exacerbated pathogenic alpha-synuclein aggregation and propagation. The findings suggest that glymphatic impairment may contribute to the development and progression of synucleinopathies, providing insights into the clinical linkage between sleep disruption and synucleinopathy. However, the study is a preprint and has not yet undergone peer review.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In this preprint, researchers studied the association between mislocalization of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) and the glymphatic clearance of alpha-synuclein in a human postmortem case series. They found that AQP4 mislocalization was linked to neocortical Lewy body pathology. In mice, they discovered that alpha-synuclein is removed from the brain via perivascular pathways; however, impaired glymphatic clearance of alpha-synuclein to the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) occurred due to a loss of perivascular AQP4 localization. Additionally, they observed that this loss of perivascular AQP4 localization promoted the development and propagation of alpha-synuclein aggregates. The study was conducted by Molly Braun et al., and Jeffrey J. Iliff is the corresponding author.

ABSTRACT

This scientific paper proposes a link between sleep disruption, glymphatic function, and the progression of synucleinopathies, a subclass of neurodegenerative conditions characterized by α-synuclein aggregates. The study found that mislocalization of AQP4, a water channel essential for glymphatic function, is associated with neocortical α-synuclein pathology in human post-mortem frontal cortex. In a mouse model, the loss of perivascular AQP4 localization was shown to impair the clearance of α-synuclein from interstitial to cerebrospinal fluid and increase α-synuclein aggregates. The authors suggest that this mislocalization may contribute to the development and propagation of synucleinopathies, such as Lewy Body Dementia and Parkinson's disease. This is a preprint and has not yet undergone peer review.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the accumulation of soluble proteins into insoluble aggregates, a common feature in neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's, Huntington's, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Parkinson's. Sleep disruption early in the disease course is thought to be a biological driver of this protein aggregation. Intercellular propagation of these aggregates contributes to the progressive neuroanatomical spread of pathology in several neurodegenerative conditions.

The paper focuses on α-synuclein aggregates, particularly in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, and how they propagate through various mechanisms such as synaptic transmission, extracellular vesicle release, and damaged/dead cell release. The glymphatic pathway, a brain-wide network of perivascular spaces that supports the clearance of interstitial proteins, is proposed to modulate the dynamics of α-synuclein aggregation and extracellular aggregate propagation.

Glymphatic function increases during sleep, is impaired by acute sleep disruption, and is regulated by the circadian cycle. Impairment of glymphatic function due to sleep disruption, aging, or following brain injury may promote the process of α-synuclein aggregate propagation.

The study aims to evaluate whether neocortical Lewy body pathology is associated with perivascular AQP4 mis-localization in a human post mortem case series and if loss of perivascular AQP4 localization impairs the perivascular glymphatic clearance of α-synuclein in a transgenic mouse model. Finally, it aims to evaluate whether impairment of glymphatic function accelerates the propagation of α-synuclein aggregates in a seeding/propagation mouse model.

METHODS

The scientific paper describes a detailed protocol for various experiments involving biological samples and mice, including immunofluorescence, Western blot, anesthesia, and intraparenchymal injections. The methods employed are:

  1. Perfusion Fixation and Freezing (PFA): This method is used for labeling large blood vessels in brains. After post-fixation, cryoprotection, freezing, and storage at -80°C, the sections were made.

  2. CSF Collection and ELISA Assays: Baseline and post-PFA CSF samples were collected from mice for α-synuclein level quantification using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

The data analysis involved confocal and widefield fluorescence imaging, image processing using ImageJ FIJI software, statistical analysis using Graphpad Prism 7, and presenting results with mean ± S.E.M., followed by non-parametric comparisons between datasets using Mann-Whitney U tests.

RESULTS

This text appears to be a scientific research paper about the role of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) in the clearance of alpha-synuclein, a protein associated with Parkinson's disease and other synucleinopathies.

The text discusses two studies: one on human neuropathological case series and another on mice. In the human study, it is found that frontal cortical perivascular AQP4 polarization is reduced in the presence of aging and Alzheimer's disease pathology. The association protein α-syntrophin (SNTA1) plays a role in maintaining AQP4's perivascular localization, and its deletion in mice abolishes this localization.

In the mouse study, it is found that glymphatic function is impaired in Snta1-/- compared to wild type mice, and deletion of Snta1 exacerbates amyloid pathology in two different mouse models of amyloidosis. The text then goes on to examine the perivascular glymphatic clearance of alpha-synuclein from the brain interstitium to the CSF, and finds that loss of perivascular AQP4 localization reduces this clearance.

Additionally, it is found that in a mouse model of synucleinopathy propagation involving synthetic alpha-synuclein pre-formed fibrils (PFFs), Snta1-/- mice injected with PFFs have markedly increased numbers of aggregates compared to wild type animals, suggesting that loss of perivascular AQP4 localization and impaired perivascular CSF-ISF exchange exacerbates pathogenic alpha-synuclein aggregation and propagation.

DISCUSSION

This discussion section summarizes a study that explores the potential link between glymphatic impairment, AQP4 mis-localization, and the progression of synucleinopathies like Parkinson's disease (PD) and Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). The research suggests that human neocortical Lewy body pathology is associated with a loss of perivascular AQP4 localization. In animal models, this mis-localization of AQP4 impairs glymphatic function, affecting the clearance of a-synuclein from brain tissue to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The study also demonstrates that these changes are sufficient to promote the propagation of pathological a-synuclein aggregates. The findings suggest that glymphatic impairment may contribute to the development and progression of synucleinopathies, providing insights into the clinical linkage between sleep disruption and synucleinopathy. The study acknowledges its limitations, such as the cross-sectional nature of human post-mortem studies and the need for further research on the relationship between sleep, AQP4, and synuclein biology in mice and humans.
