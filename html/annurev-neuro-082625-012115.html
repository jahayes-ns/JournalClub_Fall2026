ABSTRACT

This review summarizes recent research on the lower brainstem's role as a key hub linking visceral physiology with brain state regulation. The nucleus of the solitary tract (NST) functions as a central gateway, translating cardiovascular, immune, and digestive signals into a sleep drive. The parabrachial nucleus (PBN) plays a crucial role in processing threat-related inputs to promote arousal. Various populations of GABAergic neurons in the medulla induce motor suppression and sleep, while cholinergic neurons in the nucleus ambiguas and catecholaminergic cells in the ventrolateral medulla and locus coeruleus regulate sleep-wake states together with somatic and autonomic motor activity. These findings establish the brainstem as a master coordinator aligning global brain states with peripheral bodily functions, rather than just a collection of reflex centers.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction discusses three key regions in the brain - Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS), Parabrachial Nucleus (PBN), and Medulla - that work together to regulate sleep-wake cycles, arousal, and motor activity.

  1. The NTS is a central integrator for diverse peripheral physiological signals and plays a significant role in sickness-induced sleep.
  2. The PBN serves as a danger detection hub, coordinating survival behaviors through integration of interoceptive, somatosensory, and chemosensory inputs. It promotes arousal and is divided into medial (mPBN) and lateral (lPBN) regions.
  3. The Medulla produces muscle atonia during REM sleep, with GABAergic/glycinergic neurons in the ventromedial medulla (VMM) being key mediators of motor suppression.

Additional neural circuits and components that regulate these functions include:

  1. The Locus Coeruleus (LC), a region in the dorsal pons containing norepinephrine (NE) neurons that integrate inputs to modulate behavioral states, particularly promoting wakefulness.
  2. The Reticular formation (RF), a network of neurons that plays a critical role in maintaining wakefulness by activating cortical areas and thalamic nuclei.
  3. The Thalamus serves as a relay station for sensory information and helps regulate consciousness and sleep-wake cycles.
  4. The Hypothalamus contains various neuronal groups that regulate sleep-wake cycles, autonomic function, and endocrine responses, with the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) being the primary circadian pacemaker in mammals.
  5. The Brainstem reticular activating system (RAS), a network of neurons that extends from the brainstem to the cerebral cortex and plays a crucial role in maintaining wakefulness by activating cortical areas.
  6. Peripheral autonomic neurons, activated by LC-NE neurons, regulate somatic and autonomic motor functions during wakefulness.
