Exercise and hypoxia independently stimulate platelet liberation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor

Thomas, Caldwell, Macleod, Abraham, Carr, Gasho, Brewster, Bruce, Monteleone, Duffy, Howe, Tremblay, Jenkins, Talbot, Stembridge, Ainslie, Gibbons | 2025

Final Summary

This study, published in The Journal of Physiology, aims to investigate the effects of exercise and hypoxia on Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and Platelet Factor 4 (PF4), two key factors involved in neuroplasticity and platelet activation. Twelve healthy adults participated in a repeated-measures cross-over design, performing maximal exercise at sea level and high altitude (3800 m).

The research questions focus on determining if the brain is the primary source of circulating BDNF, whether its release is enhanced by platelet activation through the cerebral circulation, and how exercise and hypoxia differently affect free BDNF and PF4 levels in the brain circulation. The study used a linear mixed-effects model to analyze the data, and GraphPad Prism for figure generation.

Key findings include: 1. At sea level, maximal exercise doubled free BDNF without significant changes in total BDNF or platelet saturation. After exercise, there was no uptake or release of free BDNF across the brain. 2. At high altitude, hypoxia led to increased free BDNF in venous blood compared to arterial, suggesting a promotion of free BDNF release from the brain during hypoxia. 3. PF4 release was correlated with BDNF release, suggesting that platelet activation may contribute to the neurotrophic effect of exercise.

The study's conclusions suggest that exercise and hypoxia independently and in combination increase platelet activation, which stimulates the production or release of BDNF within the cerebrovasculature. The findings indicate that platelet activation plays a role in the increase in free BDNF demonstrated in this study, and both BDNF and PF4 concentrations return to baseline within 5 minutes of exercise cessation. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In this study, published in The Journal of Physiology, researchers investigated the effects of exercise and hypoxia on brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and platelet factor 4 (PF4), two key factors involved in neuroplasticity and platelet activation. The study involved 12 healthy adults who performed maximal exercise at sea level and high altitude (3800 m).

The key findings were: 1. At sea level, exercise doubled free BDNF and PF4 concentrations without a veno-arterial difference. 2. At high altitude, there was a net release of BDNF across the brain, and the exercise-induced doubling in concentration persisted with a pronounced veno-arterial difference. 3. PF4 release was correlated with BDNF release, suggesting that platelet activation may contribute to the neurotrophic effect of exercise. This study provides insight into the role of platelets in liberating BDNF during exercise and hypoxia, which may have implications for understanding the neuroprotective effects of exercise.

INTRODUCTION

This research study investigates the sources and release of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) across the cerebral circulation during exercise and hypoxia. Twelve healthy individuals participated in a repeated-measures cross-over design, involving high-intensity cycling at sea level and after 6–8 days at high altitude. Blood samples were collected using catheters during various stages of exercise.

The study aims to determine if the brain is the primary source of circulating BDNF and whether its release is enhanced by platelet activation through the cerebral circulation. To do this, researchers measured total, free (platelet-poor plasma), and per-platelet BDNF concentrations, and PF4 as an index of platelet activation across the brain.

The study's findings, not yet provided in the given text, are analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, considering time (rest, light cycling, exercise), circulation (arterial, venous), and participants as random effects. Missing samples were assumed missing at random. After light cycling, the post-light cycling sample served as the baseline for further analyses. The study employs GraphPad Prism for figure generation and uses statistical methods such as Pearson’s correlations to examine relationships between certain biomarkers.

RESULTS

In this study, participants performed maximal exercise at sea level and high altitude. At sea level, maximal exercise doubled free BDNF without significant changes in total BDNF or platelet saturation. After exercise, there was no uptake or release of free BDNF across the brain. Hypoxia led to increased free BDNF in venous blood compared to arterial, suggesting a promotion of free BDNF release from the brain during hypoxia. The pattern of PF4 responses across the brain was similar to free BDNF, with both increasing during exercise and correlating with platelet loss/aggregation. The study indicates that maximal exercise and hypoxia differently affect free BDNF and PF4 levels in the brain circulation.

DISCUSSION

This study finds that exercise and hypoxia independently and in combination increase platelet activation, which may stimulate the production or release of BDNF within the cerebrovasculature. The source of increased circulating BDNF during exercise is primarily from platelets, with only a small percentage (2.5%) of their BDNF cargo being released. Platelet activation during hypoxic exercise leads to substantial free BDNF release in the brain, potentially due to platelet aggregation. The study measures PF4 as an indicator of platelet activation, which increases with both exercise and hypoxia, and is correlated with pBDNF release. The findings suggest that platelet activation plays a role in the increase in free BDNF demonstrated in this study. The study also finds that BDNF and PF4 concentrations return to baseline within 5 minutes of exercise cessation.
