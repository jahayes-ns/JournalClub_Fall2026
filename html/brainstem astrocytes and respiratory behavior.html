Final Summary

This study, currently under peer review at Nature Communications, investigates the role of astrocytes in regulating arousal and sighing, particularly in the ventral respiratory column (VRC) of mice. The research found that specific Aldh1l1 cells within the VRC are activated before sighs and can be recruited by hypoxia. ABSTRACT

This scientific paper investigates the role of astrocytes, brain cells that regulate various physiological functions, in regulating arousal and sigh generation, particularly in the ventral respiratory column (VRC) of mice. The study found that a specific subset of Aldh1l1 cells in the VRC are activated before sighs and can be recruited by hypoxia. Activation of these astrocytes increased the likelihood of evoking arousals with sighs, and also increased calcium transients in catecholaminergic neurons in the VRC prior to arousal with sighs. The findings suggest that medullary astrocytes modulate sigh generation and arousal transitions, and are important for the ventilatory and arousal response to hypoxia.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper highlights the importance of breathing regulation for maintaining life, with a focus on the brainstem network that controls respiration. This network includes specialized neurons and astrocytes, particularly the pre-Bötzinger Complex (preBötC) in the ventral respiratory column (VRC), which serves as the primary rhythm generator for breathing. Astrocytes play a crucial role in preserving network stability, managing transitions between breathing phases, and responding to oxygen and carbon dioxide levels. They are also involved in regulating the sleep-wake cycle and cognitive functions. Recent studies suggest that astrocytes contribute to generating sighs through the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which activates P2Y1 receptors in the preBötC. The paper aims to investigate the role of VRC astrocytes in regulating state-dependent breathing and arousal, particularly during Non-REM and REM sleep, revealing them as key regulators of arousal and sighing.

RESULTS

This study explores the role of astrocytes in the ventral respiratory column (VRC) and their interaction with C1/A1 neurons for arousal and sigh generation in mice. Photostimulation of astrocytes enhanced C1/A1 neuronal activity during both wakefulness and sleep, without affecting respiratory frequency or tidal volume. Optogenetic activation of astrocytes in the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN) increased both respiratory frequency and tidal volume during both states. The study suggests that astrocyte-driven sigh generation and arousal within the VRC depend on purinergic P2Y1 signaling. Furthermore, astrocytes in the VRC were found to be activated during hypoxia. The methods employed include viral injections, optical fiber implantation, and calcium imaging with CaMPARI. The study provides new insights into astrocyte regulation of arousal and sigh generation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the authors explored the role of astrocytes in hypoxia-induced arousal and sigh generation in freely behaving mice, focusing on astrocyte populations in the VRC and RTN. The research builds upon previous studies showing that astrocytes regulate breathing, arousal, and sighing through purinergic signaling.

The authors found that astrocyte activation in the preBötC increased tidal volume (VT) and induced arousal followed by sighs, particularly during NREMS and REMS, with an increase in neck muscle tone reflecting arousal. Arousal was consistently followed by a sigh, as previously described. Conversely, astrocyte stimulation in the RTN did not induce sighs or arousal but increased eupneic breathing frequency (fR) and VT.

The results suggest that astrocytes in different brain regions play differential roles in central control of breathing, chemosensation, arousal, and sleep/wake transitions. The authors hypothesized that astrocytes in the VRC interact with catecholaminergic C1 neurons, which are part of the arousal circuitry but not necessary for arousal and sigh generation.

The study identified purinergic signaling through astrocytes as essential components of this pathway, with combined inhibition of Kir4.1 channels and P2Y1 receptors abolishing both arousal and sigh generation. The authors also found that a significant depletion of astrocytes (around 77%) in the VRC did not abolish the hypoxic ventilatory response to hypoxia, suggesting that CB inputs require the participation of central astrocytes in the preBötC to evoke the coordinated and rapid activation of sighs and arousal.

The findings support the notion that disturbances in sighing and arousal could contribute to the failure to arouse in sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), as SIDS victims show gliosis in the preBötC region. The study supports the idea that central medullary astrocytes play an important role in complementing oxygen sensing by the CB and in modulating adaptive responses to low oxygen environments.

METHODS

The experimental methods combined optogenetics, fiber photometry, and electrophysiology to investigate the role of astrocytes in respiratory sigh generation in mice. Viral vectors were injected into the Ventral Respiratory Column (VRC), followed by optical fiber and electrode implantation for calcium imaging, EMG recordings, and optogenetic stimulations. Drugs like VU0134992 and MRS2279 were microinjected to modulate neural activity. Data analysis was done using various software, with two-photon astrocytes calcium imaging, electrophysiology, and cross-correlation analyses conducted. CaMPARI photoconversion was performed in freely behaving mice, followed by histological and immunohistochemical procedures. All data from the study is available in the main text or supplementary information. The aim of the study was to measure the frequency of naturally generated sighs after drug applications and correlate cell activity with sigh generation through electrophysiology and two-photon cross-correlation analyses.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

In this scientific research, researchers investigated the role of astrocytes in regulating neuronal activity and arousal responses during sighs. They used optogenetics to stimulate a subpopulation of brainstem astrocytes in transgenic mice, specifically Aldh1l1 astrocytes in the ventral respiratory column (VRC).

Activating these astrocytes altered respiratory patterns and increased sighing, suggesting a key role in linking neural activity, arousal, and respiratory control. They observed an increase in cells expressing a red fluoroprotein after applying 405 nm light, while a control group without the application of 405-nm light showed partially and fully photoconverted CaMPARI neurons.

Furthermore, depleting C1/A1 neurons using 6-OHDA and administering Kir4.1/P2Y1 antagonists into the VRC eliminated astrocyte-driven arousal and sighs. The data reported are mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistics performed using T-test or Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. The source data are provided as a Source Data file. The findings contribute to a better understanding of the neuroscience behind arousal responses and respiratory control, potentially leading to therapeutic applications.
