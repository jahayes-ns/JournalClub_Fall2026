Moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning to enhance cerebrovascular function in the elderly: a randomized controlled trial

Randy, Perrin, Ghaith, Kohlbrenner, Flore, Champigneulle, Guinot, Doutreleau, Verges, Brugniaux, Marillier | 2025

Keywords: cerebrovascular function, cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide, intermittent hypoxia conditioning, vascular aging

Final Summary

This scientific paper led by Hugo Randy et al., from the University of Grenoble Alpes, aimed to investigate the impact of an 8-week moderate Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) program on cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVRCO2) in elderly individuals. The study was a randomized controlled trial involving 31 healthy elderly participants aged between 60-80 years, with no chronic cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory diseases. Participants underwent three sessions per week over an 8-week period, and CVRCO2 was assessed using transcranial doppler ultrasound. Contrary to expectations, the study found no significant improvement in CVRCO2 parameters for the IHC group compared to the control group at any time point. The study's limitations include technical issues with blood pressure measurements and a small sample size due to stringent inclusion criteria. Suggested avenues for future research involve investigating alternative methodologies, such as combining exercise and IHC, to improve CVRCO2 responsiveness in the elderly population.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

Title: Moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning to enhance cerebrovascular function in the elderly: a randomized controlled trial

Summary: This scientific paper, led by Hugo Randy et al., from the University of Grenoble Alpes, explores the impact of moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning on improving cerebrovascular function in the elderly. The study is a randomized controlled trial and was conducted at CHU Grenoble Alpes, France. The authors investigated two senior groups who were subjected to either intermittent hypoxic conditioning or a control group without intervention. The key findings are yet to be revealed but the research aims to enhance our understanding of how moderate intermittent hypoxic conditioning could potentially benefit elderly individuals by improving their cerebrovascular function.

ABSTRACT

The study aimed to investigate the effects of Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) on cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVRCO2) in elderly individuals over short- and mid-term periods. Eighteen participants were randomly assigned to IHC or control groups, undergoing 8 weeks of moderate exposure to either intermittent hypoxia or sham hypoxia. MCA reactivity was assessed through transcranial doppler ultrasound. The results showed no significant improvement in CVRCO2 parameters for the IHC group compared to the control group at any time point, contradicting initial expectations. This study is the first to examine the effects of IHC on CVRCO2 over the short- and mid-term in elderly individuals.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper introduces a study investigating the potential of Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) as a strategy to enhance cerebrovascular function in elderly individuals, addressing the increased vulnerability of this population to cerebrovascular dysfunction. The paper cites previous research demonstrating benefits of IHC for cardiovascular system, metabolism, and cognitive function across various populations.

IHC is believed to improve endothelial function by stimulating the synthesis of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) isoforms. Human studies have reported significant correlations between reductions in arterial blood pressure (ABP) and NO bioavailability. Both acute and repeated elevations in shear stress improve flow-mediated dilation, a marker of peripheral vascular function.

In the cerebrovascular domain, hypoxic exposure has been found to induce vasodilation and increase mean middle cerebral artery blood velocity (MCAv). However, the effects of repeated IHC exposures on elderly populations have yet to be explored. The study aims to determine whether IHC can enhance cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVRCO2) in elderly individuals and if the effects are sustained over time. The researchers hypothesize that IHC will enhance CVRCO2 within a few days post-intervention, with effects persisting for at least two months post-intervention.

METHODS

This study is a single-blind, parallel group, randomized controlled trial involving 31 healthy Caucasian elderly individuals aged between 60-80 years, with a BMI less than 30 kg/m2 and no chronic cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory diseases (except for common aging conditions like hypertension and treated obstructive sleep apnea). Participants were recruited through elderly associations in Grenoble, France and by word of mouth. They underwent a clinical examination before the study, including a 12-lead resting electrocardiogram and spirometry.

Participants were randomly assigned (1:1 allocation ratio) to either an intermittent hypoxic conditioning (IHC) group or a sham normoxic control group (CTL). They completed three sessions per week over an 8-week period, with each session lasting approximately 63 minutes. The IHC sessions involved breathing hypoxic gas mixtures for 5-minute cycles interspersed with 3-minute normoxic periods, while CTL participants breathed room air for the same duration.

Cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVRCO2) was evaluated by measuring changes in middle cerebral artery velocity (MCAv) in response to fluctuations in end-tidal pressure of carbon dioxide (PetCO2). Participants breathed through a mouthpiece connected to a respiratory flow head and a gas analyzer, enabling continuous monitoring of PetCO2. The CVRCO2 assessment was performed according to standard protocols, with participants abstaining from alcohol, caffeine, and vigorous physical activity for at least 24 hours prior to each experimental session.

Data were sampled at 1 kHz using a digital converter and recorded for offline analysis. CVRCO2 was determined in both absolute and relative terms. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi, with a sample size of 14 participants per group required to achieve a statistical power of 80%. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation after checking normality assumption using Shapiro-Wilk test. The study was approved by a national ethics committee and registered at clinicaltrials.gov.

RESULTS

In a study involving 31 participants, only 18 were included in the final analysis due to difficulties in MCA insonation and non-adherence to the conditioning protocol. The Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) protocol was well-tolerated with no adverse events reported. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of anthropometric, cardiovascular, and pulmonary function parameters. One participant in the IHC group had obstructive sleep apnea and two had treated glaucoma.

Cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide (CVRCO2) remained unchanged in both groups during hypercapnia and hypocapnia, with no significant differences between the groups or time points. The calibrated hyperventilation task produced similar physiological responses in both groups, and absolute and relative CVRCO2 values remained stable throughout the study.

DISCUSSION

This single-blind, randomized controlled study aimed to investigate the effect of an 8-week moderate Intermittent Hypoxic Conditioning (IHC) program on cerebrovascular function in healthy elderly individuals. Contrary to expectations, cerebrovascular function, as assessed using Cerebral Vasoreactivity to CO₂ (CVRCO₂), was not altered by the intervention. The study's results suggest that the hypoxic dose may have been insufficient or the mechanism engaging cerebrovascular adaptations might be different than initially hypothesized. The study's limitations include technical issues with blood pressure measurements, assumptions about the constancy of MCA cross-sectional area during CVRCO₂ assessments, and a small sample size due to stringent inclusion criteria.

CONCLUSION

The study aimed to evaluate the effect of an 8-week IHC program on cerebrovascular function in the elderly but did not find a positive influence of such intervention. Due to limitations in the sensitivity of traditional CVRCO2 measurements, alternative methodologies are suggested for future research. Further studies could investigate the combined effects of exercise and IHC on improving CVRCO2 responsiveness.
