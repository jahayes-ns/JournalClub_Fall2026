Final Summary

The study by Shevtsova et al., published in eLife, investigates the recovery of locomotor function following thoracic spinal cord injuries (hemisection or contusion) in rats using computational models of rat spinal locomotor circuits. The research question focuses on understanding the reorganization of neural connectivity underlying locomotor control during post-injury recovery.

The methods involve developing a computational model simulating the primary control of locomotion, including rhythm and pattern generation, interlimb coordination, and speed-dependent gait expression in rats. The authors varied a parameter 'α' to determine the stability of solutions and used Sobol sensitivity indices to identify which connectivity changes most strongly influenced post-injury gait.

Key findings indicate that post-injury gait expression is significantly impacted by the drive to sublesional lumbar rhythm generators (RGs) and lumbar commissural interneuron connectivity (V0/V3). The balance between V0 and V3 connectivity is critical for maintaining post-injury gait expression, rather than their absolute strengths individually. LPN pathways and cervical CINs had only minor effects on model output within the tested perturbation range.

In a hemisection injury, the drive to the ipsilesional hindlimb RG is the main contributor to gait asymmetries, while after a contusion injury, connection weights of only 5% of their pre-injury values for LPNs are sufficient to reproduce observed changes in interlimb coordination and gait expression.

The conclusions highlight that injury symmetry partly dictates the location and type of plasticity supporting recovery, with a shared requirement for reactivation of sublesional lumbar RGs together with balanced lumbar commissural connectivity being crucial for post-injury gait expression. The study offers insights into potential therapeutic targets for spinal cord injuries by restoring sublesional lumbar RG and commissural function, tailored to injury symmetry.

The research was funded by multiple grants from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Kentucky Spinal Cord Injury Research Trust, with significant contributions from key researchers including Natalia A Shevtsova, Andrew B Lockhart, Ilya A Rybak, David SK Magnuson, and Simon M Danner. The study's findings could potentially contribute to a better understanding of spinal cord injury and locomotion in rats, with implications for future treatments and rehabilitation strategies.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Shevtsova et al., published in eLife, presents a computational model of rat spinal locomotor circuits and their plastic reconfiguration following lateral hemisection or contusion injuries. The model successfully mirrors the gait parameters observed experimentally, suggesting the emergence of detour circuits after lateral hemisection and plasticity of left-right and sensory inputs below the injury after a midline contusion. The model proposes that recovery in both cases relies on re-engaging sublesional lumbar rhythm-generating circuits, with these networks being key determinants of post-injury gait expression. This research offers insights into the reorganization within spared and injured spinal circuitry following thoracic spinal cord injuries and suggests potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the recovery of locomotor function following incomplete spinal cord injuries, particularly focusing on thoracic lateral hemisection or contusion injuries in rats. This recovery is accompanied by reorganization of spared neural pathways and circuitry within the spinal cord. The primary control of locomotion lies within the spinal circuitry, which produces rhythm and pattern, and controls interlimb coordination. These circuits are disrupted by thoracic spinal cord injuries but remain largely intact below and above the lesion.

Following recovery from these injuries, rats exhibit reduced maximal speed and altered speed-dependent gait patterns. Post-hemisection recovery allows for the expression of pre-injury gaits, albeit with reliance on the contralesional limb as lead in non-alternating gaits. In contrast, mild-to-moderate contusion injuries result in a more pronounced speed reduction, loss of non-alternating gaits, and emergence of novel alternating gait patterns.

To investigate potential reorganization of neural connectivity underlying locomotor control following recovery from these injuries, the authors developed computational models of the spinal circuitry that controls speed-dependent interlimb coordination and gait expression in rats. These models offer insights into the organization and function of spinal locomotor circuitry, providing a foundation for studying post-injury plasticity.

The paper aims to investigate potential reorganization of neural connectivity underlying locomotor control following recovery from symmetrical contusion and asymmetrical hemisection injuries using these computational models. The goal is to gain insights into the mechanisms that underlie functional recovery and the influence of injury symmetry on those changes.

RESULTS

The paper by Shevtsova et al., published in eLife 2025, investigates factors influencing frequency-dependent gait expression in spinal cord injury models. Key findings include:

  1. Post-injury gait expression is significantly impacted by the drive to sublesional lumbar rhythm generators (RGs) and lumbar commissural interneuron connectivity (V0/V3).

  2. The balance between V0 and V3 connectivity is critical for maintaining post-injury gait expression, rather than their absolute strengths individually. Coordinated scaling of these pathways preserves overall locomotor behavior.

  3. Disrupting the balance between V0 and V3, or altering them in opposite directions, leads to substantial deviations from baseline patterns.

  4. LPN pathways and cervical CINs had only minor effects on model output within the tested perturbation range.

The study focuses on spinal cord injuries like hemisection and contusion, highlighting that:

  • In a hemisection injury, the drive to the ipsilesional (same-side) hind/lumbar flexor RG center plays a dominant role in determining locomotor outcomes.
  • After a contusion injury, the drive to sublesional lumbar flexor RG centers is the dominant determinant of locomotor outcomes after recovery.

The research emphasizes the importance of restoring activation of sublesional lumbar RG circuits and strengthening commissural pathways in shaping post-injury locomotor patterns for potential therapeutic approaches to spinal cord injuries.

DISCUSSION

The discussion in the paper focuses on central nervous system responses to spinal cord injuries, specifically hemisection and contusion. The research used computational models to investigate how these injuries affect neural circuits responsible for locomotion.

For a hemisection injury, the model suggests that asymmetry in the drive to the ipsilesional hindlimb RG (on the same side as the injury) compensates for the asymmetry of long propriospinal connections and is the main contributor to gait asymmetries. For a contusion injury, the model indicates that connection weights of only 5% of their pre-injury values for long propriospinal interneurons (LPNs) are sufficient to reproduce observed changes in interlimb coordination and gait expression.

Experimental evidence supports plasticity resulting in decreased LPN connectivity after a mild contusion injury, which has a disruptive effect on interlimb coordination. On the other hand, silencing of a proportion of either ascending or descending LPNs after moderate contusion injury improves interlimb coordination and key locomotor indices.

The paper highlights the significant role of various connections, including ascending LPNs and commissural interneurons, in both pre- and post-injury locomotion. However, the model does not explicitly account for reorganization of afferent feedback connectivity or body/limb biomechanics.

Future work involves creating neuromechanical models that incorporate phasic afferent feedback, its reorganization after injury, and its interaction with body and limb biomechanics. Additionally, experimental characterization of post-injury reorganization in lumbar circuits and their interacting pathways is needed to validate and distinguish circuit reorganizations that promote locomotor recovery from those that are maladaptive and limit it.

CONCLUSION

The study by Danner et al. (2023) reveals distinct deficits in interlimb coordination and gait expression following two spinal cord injury models during overground locomotion. These deficits extend beyond walk–trot speeds and differ between the two injury models.

A computational model of spinal locomotor circuitry was developed to understand post-injury recovery mechanisms. The model recapitulated locomotor characteristics observed after recovery from lateral hemisection, suggesting plastic changes responsible for recovery involved new pathways that bypass the lesion to drive the hindlimb rhythm-generating circuits (RGs). In contrast, recovery after a symmetrical midline contusion injury involved strengthening of local left–right circuitry within each enlargement.

The study concludes that injury symmetry partly dictates the location and type of plasticity supporting recovery, with a shared requirement for reactivation of sublesional lumbar RGs together with balanced lumbar commisural connectivity being crucial for post-injury gait expression. Clinical approaches aimed at restoring sublesional lumbar RG and commissural function may be more effective when tailored to injury symmetry, while the further development of computational approaches could lead to individualized models for better characterization of injury profiles and therapeutic strategy development.

METHODS

In this scientific paper, researchers investigated post-injury gait in spinal cord models. They varied the parameter 'α' between 0.45 and 1.05 (with a step size of 0.01 for intact condition) and between 0.9 and 1.0 for hemisection and contusion conditions. Each bifurcation diagram consisted of average phase differences across 20 ramp cycles, with increased noisy currents.

To determine the stability of solutions, they computed local linear approximations at each ramp cycle's endpoint and used tangent slopes as indicators: a slope less than 1 signified a stable solution, while a slope greater than 1 (with a negative second derivative) indicated an unstable one. If the behavior could not be determined, it was deemed indeterminate.

Bifurcation diagrams were visualized in a phase space with normalized phase differences on axes and 'α' as the control parameter. For a local sensitivity analysis, model parameters were perturbed multiplicatively on a logarithmic scale, using an XGBoost gradient-boosted tree model to approximate the mapping of perturbed parameters to Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) values. Sobol sensitivity indices were estimated to identify which connectivity changes most strongly influenced post-injury gait. These reported total-order indices include all interaction terms and are interpreted as variance contributions within a local neighborhood based on the perturbation band [80%,125%], not as global sensitivities across the entire parameter space.
