Final Summary

This Original Article, published in CNS Neuroscience & Therapeutics in 2026, investigates the impact of chronic light exposure on anxiety-like behaviors in mice and the role of the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) input to the central amygdala (CeA). The study was conducted by Jia-Ni Jing, Xing Tan, Chou Xu, Chao Yuan, Hao Fan, and Wei-Zhong Wang, and was funded by grants from the National Natural Science Foundation of China.

The researchers exposed male C57BL/6J mice to either chronic light or regular light cycles for 8 weeks, with some undergoing viral vector injections to manipulate neuron activity in the CeA and other brain regions. Behavioral tests were performed to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behaviors, and results demonstrated that chronic light exposure led to increased excitatory activity in both the RVLM and CeA, with a more significant increase observed in the RVLM.

The study identified a reciprocal excitatory projection between the RVLM and CeA, suggesting its crucial role in emotional dysregulation caused by environmental light stress. Chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulation of the RVLM→CeA pathway significantly alleviated anxiety-like behaviors, highlighting the essential role of this circuit in mediating emotional responses to aberrant light exposure.

Additionally, the study found that chronic light increased sympathetic output, elevated plasma catecholamine levels, and disrupted circadian rhythm, linking stress susceptibility under chronic light exposure to neuroendocrine homeostasis disturbances. However, the study primarily focuses on the sympathetic arm of the RVLM, and further attention is needed for the role of parasympathetic activity.

The research was limited by not addressing physical or pathological disconnection effects under light exposure conditions, being conducted exclusively in male mice, and not publicly releasing the data due to privacy or ethical restrictions. Future studies are necessary to validate the RVLM→CeA bidirectional feedback loop hypothesis and determine whether findings generalize to female subjects.

Title: Linking Environmental Perturbations to Affective Dysfunction: The Role of Brainstem-Amygdala Pathway in Light-Induced Anxiety and Hypertension
