Final Summary

In the study by Barioni et al., published in Science Advances, the research question focuses on identifying a novel oxygen sensing mechanism in the spinal cord contributing to cardiorespiratory responses during hypoxia. The study investigates the endogenous oxygen sensitivity of spinal cord sympathetic preganglionic neurons (SSPNs), which are found to be sensitive to changes in oxygen levels and have a direct connection to various end organs and blood vessels.

The methods used include two main preparations: in-situ thoracic spinal cord and a triple perfused preparation that includes brainstem, carotid bodies, and thoracic spinal cord. Pharmacological and gene knockout experiments were performed to determine the specific oxygen sensing mechanisms.

Key findings reveal that nitric oxide synthase 1 (NOS1) plays a crucial role in SSPN oxygen sensitivity. Hypoxia induces c-fos expression in ChAT+ IML SSPNs, and blocking NOS1 with L-NAME abates hypoxic responses. The study also investigates the role of NOX, which catalyzes NADPH and O2 into ROS like superoxide and H2O2, and TRPA1 and TRPM4 as important downstream components of the SOS mechanism.

The study's conclusions suggest a novel oxygen sensing system (SOS) in the central sympathetic nervous system (CNS), involving NOS1 and NOX2 in spinal cord sympathetic preganglionic neurons. This newly identified oxygen sensor mechanism enhances sympatho-respiratory activity in response to CNS asphyxia-like stimuli, potentially conferring a life-saving advantage during hypoxia. The research has potential clinical implications for various respiratory and sympathetic responses related to conditions such as obesity, atherosclerosis, diabetes, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, sleep apnea, acute mountain sickness, subdued carotid body activity, intermittent hypoxia-induced cardiorespiratory plasticity, and severe asphyxia-like conditions.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study by Barioni et al., published in Science Advances, uncovers a novel oxygen sensing mechanism in the spinal cord that contributes to cardiorespiratory responses during hypoxia. The research reveals that spinal thoracic sympathetic preganglionic neurons respond to decreased blood oxygenation (hypoxemia) and are suppressed by hyperoxia, independently of surrounding astrocytes. This newly identified oxygen sensor mechanism enhances sympatho-respiratory activity in response to CNS asphyxia-like stimuli, potentially conferring a life-saving advantage.

The proposed oxygen sensing system (SOS) employs neuronal nitric oxide synthase 1 (NOS1) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase (NOX). In hyperoxia, NOS1 acts as an oxygen-dependent sink for NADPH, while in hypoxia, the catabolism of NADPH by NOS1 decreases, increasing its availability to NOX and initiating reactive oxygen species–dependent processes such as transient receptor potential channel activation. This mechanism suggests that SOS may play a crucial role in physiological regulation during chronic disease, spinal cord injury, and cardiorespiratory crisis.

INTRODUCTION

This introduction summarizes a scientific paper focusing on the role of carotid bodies as primary oxygen sensors in mammals, driving breathing, sympathetic activity, and blood pressure responses during acute hypoxia. While carotid body denervation reduces but does not eliminate these responses, particularly in sympathetic networks. The study aims to identify the central sympathetic oxygen sensor, with potential candidates being cells in the hypothalamus and brainstem such as the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM). These cells are suggested to activate brainstem respiratory and spinal thoracic preganglionic sympathetic neurons.

The paper also discusses potential mechanisms of oxygen sensing, including astrocytes, heme oxygenase, Nap channels, and adenosine 5′-monophosphate–activated protein kinase. However, it mentions the possibility that the spinal cord may also be sensitive to oxygen.

Historical data suggests the existence of oxygen-sensitive spinal "vasomotor centers," but controversy exists due to various in vivo mechanisms at play. The paper aims to overcome these limitations using artificially perfused in situ and en bloc preparations to confirm that spinal cord sympathetic preganglionic neurons are oxygen sensitive, and the study further investigates their sensitivity, critical function, cellular identity, and mechanism.

RESULTS

The study by Barioni et al., published in Science Advances, investigates the endogenous oxygen sensitivity of spinal cord sympathetic preganglionic neurons (SSPNs). The research suggests that Nitric Oxide Synthase 1 (NOS1) plays a crucial role in this sensitivity.

The study found that hypoxia induces c-fos expression in ChAT+ IML SSPNs, indicating their oxygen sensitivity. Hypoxic responses persist under a blocking cocktail for common excitatory gliotransmitters and neurotransmitters, suggesting the oxygen sensor likely resides within neurons rather than astrocytes.

The study also revealed that blocking NOS1 with L-NAME abates hypoxic responses, implying a direct role for NOS1 in SSPN oxygen sensing. However, findings are inconsistent with a direct role for NO in SSPN oxygen sensing.

The authors propose a novel mechanism where NOS1 acts as an oxygen-dependent sink for NADPH, determining its availability for other NADPH-dependent molecules. Lower PO2 levels increase the availability of NADPH.

The study also investigates the role of NOX, which catalyzes NADPH and O2 into NADP+ and ROS like superoxide and H2O2. These ROS can activate cells, providing a mechanism for activating SSPNs during hypoxia.

Experiments using the in situ spinal cord preparation showed that NOX inhibitors suppress g-SNA (sympathetic nerve activity), while NOX agonists enhance it. A SOD mimetic increased g-SNA activity, suggesting the involvement of ROS in SOSs (spontaneous sympathetic outbursts).

The study further reveals that TRPA1 and TRPM4 are important downstream components of the SOS mechanism. TRPA1 expression is concentrated in the IML, and two TRPA1 antagonists provoke g-SN activity and abolish the hypoxic response in the thoracic in situ spinal cord preparation. TRPM4 antagonist 9-PNT also abolished hypoxic g-SN responses in situ, suggesting a role for both channels in transducing ROS to g-SN excitability.

Overall, the study highlights the complexity of SOSs and provides insights into their potential molecular underpinnings.

DISCUSSION

The study discusses a novel oxygen sensing mechanism in the central sympathetic nervous system (CNS), involving nitric oxide synthase 1 (NOS1) and NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) in spinal cord sensory neurons (SSPNs). SSPNs are sensitive to changes in oxygen levels and have a direct connection to various end organs and blood vessels, contributing to cardiorespiratory control during hypoxic stress.

The research used two main preparations: in-situ thoracic spinal cord and a triple perfused preparation that includes brainstem, carotid bodies, and thoracic spinal cord. Methods include pharmacological and gene knockout experiments to determine the specific oxygen sensing mechanisms. The findings suggest potential clinical implications for various respiratory and sympathetic responses related to conditions such as obesity, atherosclerosis, diabetes, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, sleep apnea, acute mountain sickness, subdued carotid body activity, intermittent hypoxia-induced cardiorespiratory plasticity, and severe asphyxia-like conditions.

The study also indicates that SSPNs may help prolong survival mechanisms during CNS hypoxia caused by acute ischemia, hemorrhage, or sudden cardiorespiratory arrest, and are important after high spinal cord injury when the classic arterial baroreflex is incapacitated.

The research methods involve in-situ thoracic spinal cord preparation, c-fos/ChAT immunohistochemistry, isolated perfused carotid body preparation, and various other techniques to isolate primary oxygen-sensitive neurons, block network transmission, demonstrate calcium's necessity in SOS neuronal responses to hypoxia, and fix the tissue for immunohistochemistry.
