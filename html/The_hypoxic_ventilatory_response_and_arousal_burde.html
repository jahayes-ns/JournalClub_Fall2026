Final Summary

Title: Hypoxic Ventilatory Response and Arousal Burden Predict Ventilatory Long-Term Facilitation in Obstructive Sleep Apnea Patients

In a 2025 study, Mateika et al. investigated the relationship between hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR), arousal burden, and ventilatory long-term facilitation (VLTF) in individuals with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The research involved 91 OSA participants subjected to two mild intermittent hypoxia protocols during wakefulness.

The study found that HVR and arousal index significantly predicted the magnitude of Progressive Augmentation (PA), while for VLTF, the HVR in combination with either arousal burden or arousal index showed strong correlations. Contrarily, markers of apnea severity such as hypoxic burden did not improve the ability to predict the magnitude of PA or VLTF. The study concluded that arousal burden might be a more accurate predictor of the magnitude of PA or VLTF than hypoxic burden in individuals with OSA.

The research was conducted at four institutions and received funding from the United States Department of Veterans Affairs, National Institute of Heart, Lung and Blood, and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine Foundation. The paper highlights the potential for further studies exploring whether similar findings occur in other physiological responses such as autonomic and cardiovascular responses. Acknowledgements include contributions from Wen-Hsin Hu and Harrison W. Mateika. There is a conflict of interest declaration by one author, who has received consulting fees from several companies related to OSA treatments.

ABSTRACT

This meta-analysis investigates the predictors of Progressive Augmentation (PA) and Ventilatory Long-Term Facilitation (vLTF), two forms of respiratory plasticity enhanced in individuals with Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA). The study involved 91 OSA participants subjected to two mild intermittent hypoxia protocols during wakefulness.

The analysis found that the Hypoxic Ventilatory Response (HVR) and arousal index significantly predicted the magnitude of PA, while for vLTF, the HVR in combination with either arousal burden or arousal index showed strong correlations. Contrarily, markers of apnea severity, such as hypoxic burden, did not improve the ability to predict the magnitude of PA or vLTF. Keywords: arousal, intermittent hypoxia, obstructive sleep apnoea, respiratory plasticity.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction section of this scientific paper summarizes research on the effects of mild intermittent hypoxia during wakefulness on humans, particularly those with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The study focuses on two forms of respiratory plasticity elicited by this exposure: Progressive Augmentation (PA) and Ventilatory Long-Term Facilitation (vLTF). PA is a gradual increase in the ventilatory response to hypoxia over an intermittent hypoxia protocol, while vLTF is characterized by a prolonged sustained increase in minute ventilation following brief periods of hypoxia.

Both forms of plasticity have been observed in healthy individuals and those with OSA, spinal cord injury, and other conditions. The magnitude of these plasticities may be linked to the hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR), which predicts PA during intermittent hypoxia exposure (Panza et al., 2023). Additionally, the HVR combined with the administered hypoxic burden predicts vLTF magnitude (Panza et al., 2023).

The paper further discusses potential factors influencing the magnitude of PA and vLTF, including genetic factors or neural plasticity initiated by nightly exposure to intermittent hypoxia. The study aims to explore whether modifications in chemoreflex sensitivity and arousal threshold, coupled with parameters characterizing OSA severity, predict the magnitude of PA and vLTF in OSA patients during wakefulness.

METHODS

The study investigates the impact of mild intermittent hypoxia during sleep and wakefulness on various physiological parameters. It involves 91 participants and employs two different protocols (P1 and P2) during wakefulness experiments to induce hypoxia.

The research models ventilatory responses during sleep, computes loop gain, arousal threshold, and progressive augmentation. It also calculates hypoxic and arousal burdens during sleep using MATLAB programs. During wakefulness experiments, the study measures hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR), progressive augmentation, and ventilatory long-term facilitation (LTF).

A best subset regression analysis is conducted to identify models predicting the magnitude of progressive augmentation and ventilatory LTF using various anthropometric and physiological measures. The study aims to understand relationships between sleep apnea, hypoxia during wakefulness, and body responses.

Two versions of a questionnaire are compared to assess their impact on predictive outcomes for sleep apnea severity. The best subset regression analysis is used to select the optimal model based on highest adjusted R2, lowest mean square error, and lowest Mallows’ Cp statistic. The study's dependent variable is either peak airflow (PA) or ventilatory limb time failure (vLTF), with independent variables determined using best subset regression analysis. Significance was set at P < 0.05, and data were presented as means ± standard deviation.

RESULTS

The study investigates the effects of mild intermittent hypoxia on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) patients. Anthropometric variables, endotypic markers, and OSA severity indicators were measured from each participant. The results showed a best subset regression analysis predicting the magnitude of progressive augmentation (PA) using novel indicators of apnea severity, including hypoxic ventilatory response, hypoxic burden during sleep, arousal threshold, and body mass index (BMI). PA was found to be solely predicted by the hypoxic ventilatory response.

For long-term facilitation (LTF) prediction, a subset including the hypoxic ventilatory response, hypoxic burden during wakefulness, arousal burden, hypoxic burden during sleep, and loop gain was identified as best predicting LTF magnitude. LTF was predicted by a linear combination of the hypoxic ventilatory response and arousal burden.

Using established indicators of apnea severity, the best subset for PA prediction included the hypoxic ventilatory response, ventilatory response to arousal, apnea duration, arousal index, BMI, and age. The magnitude of PA was predicted by a combination of the hypoxic ventilatory response and arousal index.

For LTF prediction using established indicators of apnea severity, the best subset included the hypoxic ventilatory response, hypoxic burden during wakefulness, ventilatory response to arousal, loop gain, and arousal index. LTF was predicted by a combination of the hypoxic ventilatory response, loop gain, and arousal index.

DISCUSSION

In a 2001 research paper, the factors influencing the magnitude of Physiologic Arousal (PA) and Ventilatory Laryngospasm (vL) in individuals with Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) were investigated. The study consisted of two parts, one during wakefulness and another during sleep.

During wakefulness, it was found that the hypoxic burden activated peripheral chemoreceptors, potentially inducing feedback to brainstem neuronal groups involved in the initiation of PA or vL. However, during sleep, the hypoxic burden did not significantly contribute to predicting the magnitude of PA or vL.

Instead, arousal indices such as arousal threshold, ventilatory response to arousal, arousal index, and arousal burden were found to be more accurate predictors. The arousal burden, which encompasses all potential intermittent stimuli known to activate neuronal groups involved in both arousal and the initiation of laryngospasm, was a better predictor than the hypoxic burden alone.

The authors suggest that repeated daily nocturnal exposure to intermittent arousal plays a significant role in enhancing the magnitude of PA and vL. This finding aligns with known mechanistic pathways involved in both transient arousal from sleep and the initiation of laryngospasm in animals.

In conclusion, the study suggests that arousal burden might be a more accurate predictor of the magnitude of PA or vL than hypoxic burden in individuals with OSA, as it better reflects the strength and duration of intermittent stimuli activating neuronal pathways involved in these phenomena. Further studies are needed to explore whether similar findings occur in other physiological responses such as autonomic and cardiovascular responses. The data for this study was collected from experiments completed in the laboratory of Jason H. Mateika, with contributions from multiple authors.
