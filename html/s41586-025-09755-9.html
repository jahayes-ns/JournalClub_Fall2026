Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study investigates the role of adenosine in the mechanism of action of ketamine, known for its rapid antidepressant effects. Ketamine increases extracellular adenosine levels in the mouse brain, particularly in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, mediated through A1 and A2A receptors. The study also explores ketamine analogues like deschloroketamine (DCK) and 3′-Cl-ketamine, which induce stronger and longer-lasting adenosine surges than ketamine and exhibit antidepressant-like effects at lower doses. These findings suggest the potential for developing novel, more effective antidepressants with fewer side effects. Additionally, the study suggests a non-NMDAR mechanism for the adenosine surge and highlights intermittent hypoxia as a promising non-pharmacological therapy for depression, leveraging adenosine signaling to deliver rapid antidepressant effects with minimal side effects.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific papers delve into various aspects of neurotransmission, depression, and their potential implications in synaptic plasticity. Key findings include:

  1. Mild hypobaric hypoxia might be related to neuropeptide Y in mice (Tebano et al., 2015).
  2. Adenosine A2A receptors are crucial for maintaining normal Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels and BDNF-induced potentiation of synaptic transmission in the mouse hippocampus (Tebano et al., 2008; Fontinha et al., 2008).
  3. BDNF enhancement of long-term potentiation requires adenosine A2A receptor activation by endogenous adenosine (Fontinha et al., 2008).
  4. GluN2A mediates ketamine's rapid antidepressant-like responses (Su et al., 2023).
  5. New insights into synaptic plasticity and depression come from stress and rapid-acting antidepressants (Duman et al., 2016).
  6. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has proven effective in treating treatment-resistant depression (Somani & Kar, 2019).
  7. Caffeine's effects on synaptic transmission and plasticity in the mouse hippocampus depend selectively on adenosine A1 and A2A receptors (Lopes et al., 2019).
  8. The actions of caffeine in the brain are detailed in Fredholm et al. (1999).

These studies reveal the intricate relationship between neurotransmission, depression, and substances that can potentially impact synaptic plasticity, providing valuable insights for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

METHODS

The scientific paper discusses research on the role of adenosine signaling in the brain using a genetically encoded adenosine sensor (GRABAdo1.0). The experiments involved cell culture, fiber photometry, in vivo two-photon imaging, and behavioral assays.

Key findings include: 1. Ketamine increases adenosine levels in the mouse prefrontal cortex by inhibiting ectonucleotidases like CD73, peaking around 5 minutes after administration and persisting for several hours. 2. Ketamine induces a dose-dependent increase in locomotor activity, suggesting potential antidepressant effects. 3. Synthesized ketamine analogues also increase adenosine levels, but there's no significant correlation between the degree of NMDA receptor inhibition and the extent of adenosine modulation by these analogues. 4. Acute intermittent hypoxia increases adenosine levels in the mPFC and alleviates depressive-like phenotypes, suggesting a potential role for adenosine signaling in the therapeutic effects of aIH. 5. Ketamine directly modulates mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux, leading to increased 13C-labeling of key TCA cycle intermediates.

The findings suggest that ketamine and its analogues may have antidepressant effects through adenosine signaling and mitochondrial TCA cycle modulation, and that acute intermittent hypoxia could potentially alleviate depressive-like phenotypes through similar mechanisms.
