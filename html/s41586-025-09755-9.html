Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study investigates the role of adenosine in the mechanism of action of ketamine, a drug used to treat depression and anxiety disorders. The study found that ketamine induces an increase in extracellular adenosine levels in the mouse brain, particularly in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, which is critical for its antidepressant effects. Ketamine's parent compound, rather than its metabolites, is directly responsible for triggering this adenosine release.

The study also synthesized and screened 31 ketamine derivatives, finding some to be more effective at increasing extracellular adenosine levels in the prefrontal cortex than ketamine itself. Two of these derivatives, DCK (2C-DCK) and 3′-Cl-ketamine, showed promising antidepressant-like effects in behavioral tests.

The study suggests that targeting adenosine signaling in the prefrontal cortex could be a viable strategy for developing new antidepressants, particularly DCK and its derivatives due to their stronger and longer-lasting adenosine surges than ketamine at equivalent doses. The enhanced potency of DCK compared to ketamine was observed in mouse models, indicating its potential efficacy and fewer side effects.

The study also explores the role of adenosine signaling in Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) and intermittent hypoxia (aIH), suggesting that aIH is a promising non-pharmacological therapy for depression with rapid antidepressant effects and minimal side effects.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific papers focus on the role of neurotransmitters, particularly adenosine receptors, in mood and anxiety disorders, with a significant emphasis on their effects in mice, especially in the hippocampus.

  1. Tebano et al. (2008) found that adenosine A2A receptors are essential for maintaining normal levels of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and BDNF-induced potentiation of synaptic transmission in the mouse hippocampus.

  2. Fontinha et al. (2008) discovered that BDNF enhancement of long-term potentiation requires activation of adenosine A2A receptors by endogenous adenosine.

  3. Su et al. (2023) identified GluN2A as a mediator of ketamine's rapid antidepressant-like responses.

  4. Duman et al. (2016) explored synaptic plasticity's role in depression, providing new insights from stress and rapid-acting antidepressants.

  5. Somani & Kar (2019) reviewed the efficacy of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation in treatment-resistant depression.

  6. Lopes et al. (2019) studied the physiological effects of caffeine on synaptic transmission and plasticity in the mouse hippocampus, demonstrating that these effects depend on adenosine A1 and A2A receptors.

  7. Fredholm et al. (1999) explained the actions of caffeine in the brain, including factors contributing to its widespread use. These papers collectively contribute to a deeper understanding of adenosine receptors' role in mood disorders, antidepressant drugs, and purinergic signaling.

METHODS

The scientific paper discusses a study using genetically engineered sensors to investigate the role of ATP/ADP ratios in neural activity and behavior, potentially providing insights into various neurological and psychiatric disorders.

The study used mice to investigate adenosine signaling in different brain regions such as the basal forebrain, lateral habenula, and prefrontal cortex. The researchers manipulated adenosine levels by injecting mice with adenosine analogs like ketamine and observed changes in behavior related to sleep, reward, and depression.

Methods used included fiber photometry, behavioral tests, and ex vivo electrophysiology. The research found that adenosine signaling in the basal forebrain regulates sleep homeostasis while adenosine signaling in the lateral habenula controls reward devaluation and depression.

Extended Data Figures 1-10 present findings related to various substances' effects on locomotor activity, release of neurotransmitters like glutamate and dopamine, intracellular calcium signaling, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle in brain mitochondria. These figures also compare different ketamine analogues' effects on NMDA receptor-mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents (NMDAR-eEPSCs) and adenosine responses in the prefrontal cortex, as well as their brain tissue concentrations.

Lastly, Extended Data Fig. 10 demonstrates how acute intermittent hypoxia alleviates depressive-like phenotypes through adenosine signaling. The study was supported by funding sources such as the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and the National Natural Science Foundation of China.
