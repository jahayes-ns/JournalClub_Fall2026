Final Summary

The research paper published in Science Translational Medicine by Zholudeva et al., (2026) investigates the use of human V2a spinal interneurons (SpINs), specifically from a human induced pluripotent stem cell line, for repairing injured rat spinal cords. The study demonstrates that these transplanted cells can anatomically and functionally integrate into rat respiratory circuits, leading to improved motor recovery post-spinal cord injury (SCI).

The researchers engineered V2a-enriched SpINs from a human induced pluripotent stem cell line and transplanted them into a rat model of cervical SCI. The key findings suggest that these cells exhibited spontaneous and light-evoked activity, formed functional connections with spinal motor neurons, and synaptically connected with the injured phrenic motor network. Transplantation of these human V2a SpINs demonstrated feasibility for potential reparative treatments for SCI by providing and receiving synaptic connections with injured motor networks.

The study's findings support further investigation into the potential advantages of transplanting V2a interneuron-enriched donor cells for spinal cord repair, despite limitations such as variability in cell survival after transplantation and the possibility that differentiation or maturation of transplanted cells may be influenced by the local injury environment. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a scientific paper published in Science Translational Medicine (Zholudeva et al., 2026), researchers demonstrated that human spinal interneurons, specifically V2a-enriched SpINs, can repair injured rat spinal cords through synaptic integration. The study involved engineering these cells from a human induced pluripotent stem cell line and transplanting them into a rat model of cervical spinal cord injury (SCI).

The researchers showed that V2a-enriched SpINs exhibited spontaneous and light-evoked activity, formed functional connections with spinal motor neurons, and synaptically connected with the injured phrenic motor network. Using optogenetics, multiunit electrophysiology, pseudorabies virus tracing, single-cell, and single-nucleus transcriptomic analyses, they confirmed the maturation of these transplanted cells into multiple excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations.

Key findings suggest that these human cervical V2a-enriched SpINs can establish functional host-transplant connectivity and improve motor recovery post-SCI by providing and receiving synaptic connections with injured motor networks, thereby demonstrating the feasibility of this approach for potential reparative treatments for SCI.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper focuses on spinal cord injury (SCI) and potential therapeutic approaches. Current treatments like rehabilitation and neuromodulation improve quality of life but only harness plasticity in spared neural tissue and do not address underlying damage. In contrast, regenerative medicine offers promising pharmacological, gene, and cell-based strategies that enhance neural repair, albeit achieving partial functional improvement. The paper suggests combining regenerative medicine with intrinsic plasticity for a more successful SCI therapy.

Spinal interneurons (SpINs) are crucial for normal spinal network function and plasticity after injury or disease. VSX2-expressing V2a interneurons, excitatory ipsilaterally projecting neurons critical for locomotor rhythm and coordination, are identified as potential candidates for spontaneous and therapeutically mediated plasticity and recovery. These interneurons contribute to respiratory circuits and have been shown to be recruited into phrenic motor pathways after SCI.

The study differentiated a population of VSX2-positive V2a-enriched SpINs from optogenetically engineered human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) and demonstrated their functional integration with the injured spinal cord. The researchers focused on the cervical spinal respiratory network, the phrenic circuit, to demonstrate the ability of V2a-enriched SpINs to form functional, host-donor, and donor-host synaptic connectivity. However, transplantation of well-defined cervical human SpINs, such as V2a interneurons, and rigorous evaluation of functional integration and posttransplantation maturation have not been performed in this study.

RESULTS

The combined results of these three scientific papers involve studies on the transplantation of human neural cells for spinal cord injury treatment.

  1. Researchers characterized human V2a SpINs using scRNA-seq, identifying five distinct cell clusters and finding that these cells have potential to form synapses and integrate into host networks. Transplantation of these cells in mice showed their survival, confluency, anatomical integration with the host injured cord, and synaptic integration with denervated spinal networks. The long-term goal is to develop a cell therapy for spinal cord injuries using V2a SpINs.

  2. A study used genetically modified hiPSC neurons, specifically V2a SpINs, transplanted into injured rat spinal cords. Optogenetic techniques were employed to activate the human cells, resulting in observed activity. Functional innervation of injured host axons into transplanted human V2a SpINs improved muscle activity in response to respiratory challenges compared with vehicle controls, suggesting a promising strategy for restoring lost motor functions after spinal cord injury.

  3. Another study focused on transplanting neural progenitor cells into mice with spinal cord injuries to restore diaphragm muscle function. The study found that synaptically integrated neurons play a key role in functional recovery of the diaphragm muscle, and subtype Exc5-2 of interneurons is most likely to synaptically connect after transplantation.

These findings suggest that transplanting human neural cells could be beneficial for restoring motor functions and improving functional outcomes after spinal cord injury. However, further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms underlying these approaches and optimize their efficacy for therapeutic applications.

DISCUSSION

The research paper by Zholudeva et al., published in Sci. Transl. Med., investigates the use of human V2a spinal interneurons (SpINs) for repairing injured rat spinal cords. The study found that transplanted cells can anatomically and functionally integrate into rat respiratory circuits, leading to improved respiratory recovery after spinal cord injury (SCI).

The research involved cell culture, immunocytochemistry, in vitro electrophysiology, surgeries for spinal cord injury, transplantation, anterograde brainstem injection, retrograde transsynaptic tracing, and terminal electrophysiology. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that about 50% of the donor cells expressed neuronal markers two months post-transplantation.

The study found that PLXNA3 and SLITRK5 genes were predominantly expressed in a subset of transplanted lumbar V2a SpINs (Vsx2+), contributing to synaptic integration with the host spinal phrenic network. The data sets generated during this study have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus and are publicly available.

The researchers suggest that these findings support further investigation into the potential advantages of transplanting V2a interneuron-enriched donor cells for spinal cord repair. However, the study has limitations such as variability in cell survival after transplantation and the possibility that differentiation or maturation of transplanted cells may be influenced by the local injury environment. The authors suggest addressing these limitations in future studies to assess cell distribution, survival, and circuit integration at clinically relevant scales.

The research was supported by grants from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and other organizations. Some authors are inventors on a US Patent application related to the use of V2a spinal interneurons for neural repair, and one author is a scientific cofounder, shareholder, and director of Tenaya Therapeutics. The manuscript was reviewed and edited by multiple authors.
