Final Summary

In the study by Zholudeva et al., published in 2026, researchers investigated the potential of human spinal interneurons (specifically, V2a-enriched ones) for repairing injured rat spinal cords using optogenetically engineered human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). Engineered cells were found to demonstrate spontaneous and light-evoked activity, forming functional connections with spinal motor neurons. Post-transplantation, these human spinal interneurons synaptically integrated with the injured phrenic motor network, reflecting a functional neuronal relay.

Key findings include maturation of the transplanted cells into multiple excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations within the host network, as well as identification of genes specific to V2a spinal interneurons synapsing with the host phrenic motor network, such as SLITRK5 and PLXNA3. The study suggests a promising approach for treating spinal cord injuries using endogenous human cells. The research team plans to make human V2a spinal interneurons available through a material transfer agreement with Gladstone Institutes.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a groundbreaking study, Zholudeva et al. (2026) investigated the potential of human spinal interneurons in repairing injured rat spinal cords. They engineered V2a-enriched spinal interneurons from an optogenetic channelrhodopsin-2 expressing human induced pluripotent stem cell line and transplanted them into a rat model of cervical spinal cord injury (SCI).

In vitro recordings showed that the engineered cells demonstrated spontaneous and light-evoked activity, forming functional connections with spinal motor neurons. Post-transplantation, these human spinal interneurons were found to synaptically integrate with the injured phrenic motor network, as revealed by pseudorabies virus tracing from the diaphragm.

The study confirmed that transplanted human spinal interneurons could both receive and provide functional synaptic connections with injured motor networks, reflecting a functional neuronal relay. Single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic analyses confirmed maturation into multiple excitatory interneuron subtypes and glial populations within the host network.

The research team identified genes specific to V2a spinal interneurons synapsing with the host phrenic motor network, including SLITRK5 and PLXNA3, suggesting a promising approach for treating SCI with endogenous human cells.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper introduces a study focused on spinal cord injury (SCI), which currently lacks curative therapeutic interventions. Existing treatments such as rehabilitation and neuromodulation can improve patient quality of life but do not treat the underlying tissue damage. Regenerative medicine offers promising strategies for neural repair, but they have only achieved partial functional improvement.

The study proposes a more successful approach by combining regenerative medicine with intrinsic neuroplasticity. Spinal interneurons (SpINs), particularly VSX2-expressing V2a interneurons, are identified as potential candidates for plasticity and recovery after SCI. These interneurons play crucial roles in normal spinal network function and plasticity.

The research differentiates a population of cervical identity V2a-enriched SpINs from optogenetically engineered human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) and demonstrates their functional integration with the injured spinal cord, specifically in the phrenic circuit. The study aims to show the ability of these V2a-enriched SpINs to form functional synaptic connectivity in host and donor tissues following transplantation.

RESULTS

The research papers discuss the development of novel therapies for spinal cord injuries using human neuron transplantation.

  1. In the first study, published in Science Translational Medicine, researchers created functional human motor neurons (MNs) from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which were then modified to express ChR2 for optical control. The MNs were transplanted into injured rhesus macaques, leading to their survival, integration with the host spinal cord, and synaptic connections. Optical stimulation of these cells recovered paralysis in the animal's limbs.

  2. The second study, published in a different scientific journal, focused on neuron transplantation in mice to restore diaphragm muscle function after spinal cord injury. The transplanted cells were derived from the spinal interneuron-enriched (SpIN) population and contained various cell types, including astrocyte progenitors, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), and neurons. The study found that a higher number of neurons in the transplant contributed to better functional recovery, with synaptically connected human neurons belonging to a single cluster (Exc5) enriched for receptor-mediated signaling and synaptic connectivity genes.

Both studies suggest promising avenues for future research towards effective treatments for spinal cord injuries in humans. However, further investigations are required to confirm these findings and ensure long-term safety and efficacy.

DISCUSSION

The combined discussion from the scientific papers involves studies that focus on neural repair strategies, primarily using human V2a spinal interneurons (SpINs) derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC). These studies aim to improve diaphragm function in mice with cervical spinal cord injuries.

One study, published in Science Translational Medicine by Zholudeva et al., demonstrated that human V2a SpINs could repair injured rat spinal cords through synaptic integration. This was shown using optogenetics, transsynaptic tracing, and in vivo electrophysiological recordings. The study also revealed that about 50% of the donor cells expressed neuronal markers two months post-transplantation, and nearly all synaptically integrated donor neurons belonged to a specific transcriptomic cluster associated with physiological recovery.

Other research discussed in the text includes using electrical stimulation to promote the excitability and plasticity of neural stem cells derived from embryonic stem cells (ESC), creating an atlas of mouse spinal cord cell types, mapping connectivity in injured and transplanted spinal networks, investigating inflammatory reactions in various species after spinal cord injury, studying degeneration of phrenic motor neurons and its effects on diaphragm activity, and research on spinal cord reconstitution with homologous neural grafts.

These studies highlight the potential benefits of transplanting V2a interneuron-enriched donor cells for treating neurological disorders. The datasets generated during the study have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE316101 and GSE316030) and will be publicly available upon publication, with code used for transcriptomic data processing and analysis deposited on Zenodo. Human V2a spinal interneurons can be made available through a material transfer agreement with Gladstone Institutes.
