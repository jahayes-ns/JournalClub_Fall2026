Adenosine and ATP Link PCO2 to Cortical Excitability via pH

Dulla, Dobelis, Pearson, Frenguelli, Staley, Masino | 2005

Final Summary

The 2005 scientific paper published in Neuron investigates the relationship between carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, pH, and neuronal function outside the brainstem, specifically in the hippocampus. The research aims to understand CO2's profound effects on neural function, with potential implications for epilepsy and other neurological disorders.

The study hypothesizes that tissue acidification caused by changing CO2 levels alters neuronal excitability by affecting extracellular adenosine and ATP concentrations. The key findings suggest that high CO2 levels inhibit ecto-ATPases, increase ATP receptor activation, decrease adenosine A1 receptor activation, leading to a decrease in extracellular adenosine levels and neuronal inhibition. Conversely, low CO2 levels (hypocapnia) increase burst frequency via adenosine A1 receptors and ATP receptors.

These findings offer a mechanism for the bidirectional effects of CO2 on neuronal excitability in the forebrain. The study's conclusions imply that CO2 plays a crucial role in regulating extracellular adenosine levels and neuronal excitability, particularly in the hippocampus. Further research is necessary to confirm these findings and explore their physiological and clinical significance, with potential implications for understanding epilepsy initiation by hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia.

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper, published in Neuron in 2005, explores the relationship between carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, pH, and neuronal function outside the brainstem. The research was conducted on hippocampal slice preparations.

Increased CO2 levels (decreasing pH) were found to enhance the concentration of the endogenous neuromodulator adenosine and inhibit excitatory synaptic transmission. This process involves adenosine A1 and ATP receptors and is dependent on decreased extracellular pH.

Conversely, reduced CO2 levels led to a decrease in extracellular adenosine concentration and an increase in neuronal excitability via adenosine A1 receptors, ATP receptors, and ecto-ATPase. The study suggests that CO2-induced changes in neuronal function are pH-dependent and involve modulation of adenosine and ATP levels. These findings offer a mechanism for the bidirectional effects of CO2 on neuronal excitability in the forebrain.

INTRODUCTION

The scientific paper introduces a study focused on understanding the mechanisms behind carbon dioxide (CO2) induced changes in neural excitability, particularly in the hippocampus. The research builds upon previous findings indicating that CO2 levels impact respiration rate, blood flow, neuronal excitability, and seizure propensity.

The study hypothesizes that tissue acidification caused by changing CO2 levels may alter neuronal excitability by affecting extracellular adenosine and ATP concentrations. Both purines influence synaptic transmission in the hippocampus. The inhibitory effects of adenosine are significant in terminating seizures, maintaining postictal depression, and restoring metabolic equilibrium following seizure activity.

The research utilizes a combination of electrophysiology, an enzymatic adenosine biosensor, and two-photon pH imaging to investigate the relationship between CO2 levels in the brain and altered neural excitability. The study aims to determine whether purines, specifically adenosine and ATP, are responsible for CO2's profound effects on neural function in the hippocampus, potentially explaining global neurological effects of altered CO2 such as seizures and sedation.

RESULTS

This study investigates the impact of carbon dioxide (CO2) levels on neuronal excitability and adenosine release in the hippocampus, with potential implications for epilepsy and other neurological disorders. Key findings include:

  1. High CO2 levels (20%) inhibit ecto-ATPases, increasing ATP receptor activation and decreasing adenosine A1 receptor activation. This leads to a decrease in extracellular adenosine levels, which attenuates neuronal excitability.

  2. High CO2 levels cause a reversible attenuation of bursting activity in area CA3, mediated by increased extracellular adenosine and A1 receptor activation. Low CO2 levels (2%) increase burst frequency, an effect blocked by blocking both adenosine A1 receptors and ATP receptors.

  3. Blocking adenosine A1 receptors increases tonic firing, and high CO2 levels do not further attenuate bursting during adenosine A1 receptor blockade. This suggests that the effects of high CO2 on neuronal excitability are mediated by adenosine A1 receptors.

  4. The study also found that increasing potassium chloride decreases burst activity, while hypocapnia (2% CO2) leads to a decrease in extracellular adenosine levels and an increase in burst frequency. This increase in burst frequency can be blocked by combining the blockade of adenosine A1 receptors and ATP receptors.

The results suggest that CO2 plays a crucial role in regulating extracellular adenosine levels and neuronal excitability in the hippocampus, with potential implications for epilepsy initiation by hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia. Further research is necessary to confirm these findings and explore their physiological and clinical significance.

DISCUSSION

This scientific research paper published in Neuron explores the impact of adenosine on bursting activity in rat hippocampal neurons. The study used various methods such as confocal microscopy, enzymatic sensors, and tetanic stimulation to measure extracellular adenosine levels, pH levels, and bursting activity in rat hippocampal slices.

Key findings indicate that increased adenosine levels decrease the frequency of bursting, while blocking adenosine receptors has the opposite effect. The study also notes that CO2 levels influence both pH and adenosine sensitivity, which may impact results.
