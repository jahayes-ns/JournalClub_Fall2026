Final Summary

This scientific paper, led by Dr. Adrianne G. Huxtable, challenges the conventional wisdom that physiological variance among neonatal Sprague–Dawley rat litters is greater between litters than within litters. The research aimed to test this hypothesis using a retrospective analysis of body weight and breathing frequency data from three cohorts of rats.

Using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis, the study found that 40–75% of total variance in body weight originated within litters, while only 43% between-litter variance remained less than within-liter variance in the second cohort. Remarkably, for breathing frequency, nearly 100% of total variance originated within litters.

These findings challenge the assumption that littermates are more similar within themselves than to other litters and suggest that experimental designs should be based on specific research questions, outcome variables, rat genetics, and raw variance both within and between litters. The paper emphasizes the need for a re-evaluation of traditional methods using an entire litter as n=1 or select pups from each litter, as it may introduce bias in light of NIH directives to minimize animal use in research.

The authors propose using multiple pups per litter and applying a mixed-model ANOVA with litter as a random effect for more accurate results. They also provide formulas for calculating the design effect to adjust sample size calculations and confidence intervals for clustered data like litters.

However, limitations include the study's applicability to other lab rodents, particularly highly inbred or genetically engineered animals, and the small size of the nicotine-exposed cohort. The authors also note that within-litter heterogeneity has been underappreciated, with significant implications for study design in developmental biology research.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the long-standing debate regarding pseudo-replication in biomedical research using neonatal rodents, particularly in behavioral psychology, developmental biology, neuroscience, and toxicology communities. Researchers argue that when a treatment is applied to the mother during pregnancy, littermates are not independent due to shared perinatal environment, maternal influences, and genetic similarities. This leads to an artificially inflated sample size and 'pseudo-replication'.

The strictest interpretation suggests randomly selecting one male and one female from a litter, or averaging data from all males and females as one replicate. In recent years, journal policies emphasizing reproducibility and rigor have intensified this debate, with suggestions that mixed-effects models can account for within-litter and between-litter variation without collapsing all pups into a single data point.

However, the assumption that variance within a litter is greater than between litters is largely based on conjecture, as demonstrated by this study's analysis of physiological measures in outbred, Sprague–Dawley rats. The paper presents a retrospective analysis comparing within and between litter variance in body weight, nicotine exposure effects on body weight, and breathing frequency in control pups from independent litters. It aims to test the hypothesis that the variance in these physiological measures between litters is greater than within litters. The study is conducted by Dr. Adrianne G. Huxtable, a physiology researcher focusing on the neural control of breathing in newborn and premature infants.

METHODS

The study involved 409 neonatal Sprague-Dawley rats from 47 timed pregnant dams obtained from Charles River Laboratories or Envigo. All animals were handled under ethical approval from the IACUC at the University of Oregon and the University of Arizona, adhering to guidelines such as those of the American Physiological Society, National Institutes of Health, ARRIVE, and The Journal of Physiology.

Neonates were housed with their mothers and littermates under controlled conditions, and body weight was measured non-invasively on postnatal day 4 (P4). A third cohort was used for measuring breathing frequency using whole-body plethysmography at the University of Arizona. The data from previously published or unpublished studies were utilized in this research.

Statistical analyses employed an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to quantify the proportion of total variance attributable to between- and within-litter variability. Additionally, a mixed-model ANOVA with Welch's correction was applied to compare group differences. The ICC served as the primary outcome variable in this study.

RESULTS

The study analyzed body weight variability among 24 control rat litters, comprising a total of 260 pups (139 males and 121 females). ANOVA was used to test the null hypothesis that all group means are equal, revealing significant differences between litters with a W-score of 7.965 and P-value <0.0001. However, ANOVA did not provide information on the relative magnitudes of raw variance within vs. between groups.

To measure this, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was computed for total variability in body weight. The ICC indicated that 24.1% of the variation originated between-group and 73.9% within-group, with similar results found when data was stratified by sex (males: 26%, females: 27.3%).

The study also compared control and nicotine-exposed mothers' litters, revealing significant litter effects but no greater between-litter than within-litter variance. For breathing frequency in six litters of 11 pups each, ANOVA results showed no significant differences, with 100% of the variability due to within-litter differences. These findings suggest that between-litter variance in breathing frequency is substantially less than within-litter variance.

DISCUSSION

The discussed scientific paper challenges the assumption that variability in physiological outcomes is greater between litters than within litters in neonatal SD rats. Contrary to common practice, the authors find this assumption unsupported for body weight and breathing frequency in untreated and nicotine-treated pups.

The authors advocate for re-evaluating the use of treating an entire litter as n=1 or using select pups from each litter, as it may introduce bias, particularly in light of NIH directives to minimize animal use in research. They propose a method to determine the optimal number of pups per litter by measuring the outcome variable across multiple litters, calculating between- and within-litter variance, ICC, and design effect.

The paper also emphasizes the use of nested linear mixed models (LMMs) to address non-independence among littermates, but acknowledges that determining the optimal number of pups per litter requires knowing the between- and within-litter variance, which is rarely available before a study begins. The authors provide formulas for calculating the design effect to adjust sample size calculations and confidence intervals for clustered data like litters.

The authors highlight the need for a larger sample size (78 pups) due to higher within-litter variance compared to between-litter variance in their study involving neonatal SD rats. They suggest using multiple pups per litter and applying a mixed-model ANOVA with litter as a random effect for more accurate results.

Limitations include the study's applicability to other lab rodents, particularly highly inbred or genetically engineered animals, and the small size of the nicotine-exposed cohort. The authors also note that within-litter heterogeneity has been underappreciated, with significant implications for study design in developmental biology research.

In conclusion, the paper underscores the importance of considering the litter effect in experimental designs using neonatal SD rats and provides practical guidance for determining the optimal number of pups per litter based on the outcome variable being studied.
