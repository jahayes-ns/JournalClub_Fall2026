Final Summary

In the scientific paper titled "Early Microglial Response to Amyloid Plaques Drives Sleep Loss in Alzheimer's Disease," researchers from various institutions, including the University of Kentucky and Wake Forest University School of Medicine, investigate the link between sleep disruption and Alzheimer's disease (AD) in APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice at different stages of pathology. The study reveals that amyloid plaques selectively reduce non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, while aging reduces REM sleep.

The findings suggest that the emergence of amyloid plaques is the critical driver of sleep loss, and microglia expansion in plaque and non-plaque containing regions may contribute to this phenotype. Microglial depletion using Pexidartinib increased sleep by over 2 hours per day in APP/PS1 mice, implying that targeting microglia could improve both sleep quantity and quality. The study also identifies distinct Electroencephalography (EEG) signatures associated with amyloid plaques during wake and NREM sleep, suggesting that EEG could be a valuable functional biomarker for early disease detection and monitoring in presymptomatic AD.

In summary, the research demonstrates that an early microglial response to amyloid plaques leads to sleep loss in Alzheimer's disease, providing insight into the progression of Alzheimer's disease and potential targets for future therapeutic interventions aimed at improving sleep quality and quantity in presymptomatic AD patients.

PREAMBLE

Title: Early Microglial Response to Amyloid Plaques Drives Sleep Loss in Alzheimer's Disease

Authors: Nicholas J. Constantino, Riley E. Irmen, Matthew J. Lanning, Sierra M. Turner, Clair C. Ashley, Caitlin M. Carroll, Evan M. Neary, Dave Rubinow, J. Andy Snipes, Shannon L. Macauley

Affiliations: Department of Physiology and Neuroscience, University of Kentucky; Sanders Brown Center of Aging, University of Kentucky; Department of Psychiatry, Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, Wake Forest University School of Medicine; LifeCanvas Technologies, Cambridge, Massachusetts

Funding Agencies: BrightFocus Foundation, Coins for Alzheimer’s Research Trust, Cure Alzheimer’s Fund, NIGMS, NIH, NIH Center of Biomedical Research Excellence (COBRE) in CNS Metabolism

Published: 13 May 2026, DOI: 10.1002/alz.71579

The research article presents findings that demonstrate an early microglial response to amyloid plaques leads to sleep loss in Alzheimer's disease. The study was conducted by a team of researchers from the University of Kentucky, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, and LifeCanvas Technologies, with funding support from various agencies including BrightFocus Foundation, Coins for Alzheimer’s Research Trust, Cure Alzheimer’s Fund, NIGMS, NIH, and NIH Center of Biomedical Research Excellence (COBRE) in CNS Metabolism. The study's key findings reveal a connection between the early activation of microglia in response to amyloid plaques and sleep disruption, providing insight into the progression of Alzheimer's disease and potential targets for future therapeutic interventions.

ABSTRACT

The study investigates the link between sleep disruption and Alzheimer's disease (AD) in APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice at various stages of pathology. Sleep disruption, specifically non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep loss, was found to emerge with amyloid plaques and did not worsen with increased plaque burden. Amyloid plaques disrupted NREM but not REM sleep. Aging reduced REM sleep and eliminated sleep rebound. Microglia were identified as a causal, reversible driver of amyloid-associated sleep loss, suggesting that sleep and EEG-based metrics could serve as sensitive biomarkers of presymptomatic AD. Electroencephalography (EEG) signatures also distinguished amyloid pathology from aging as early biomarkers of disease.

BACKGROUND

The 'background' section of the scientific paper discusses the bidirectional relationship between Alzheimer's disease (AD) and sleep. Early AD pathology, specifically amyloid plaques, impairs sleep, while sleep loss increases the risk of developing AD. However, it is unclear how amyloid plaque burden contributes to sleep disruption and its severity.

The understanding of sleep disruption in AD comes primarily from studies on individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or established dementia, where multiple factors such as hyperphosphorylated tau, neurodegeneration, and other downstream processes coexist. Preclinical studies often rely on non-invasive sleep monitoring instead of electroencephalography (EEG), assess sleep at a single time point, or fail to consider aging, limiting insight into how amyloid pathology affects sleep macroarchitecture and EEG-derived metrics across disease progression.

The paper aims to define how amyloid plaques alter excitability and their relationship to sleep, clarify how amyloid pathology disrupts sleep, identify EEG-based biomarkers for disease progression, and explore the role of microglia in modulating sleep in AD.

Microglia are suggested to play a significant role in this context, as they modulate cortical excitability and sleep. In response to amyloid plaques, microglia increase and adopt reactive phenotypes, positioning them as potential contributors to functional deficits such as sleep loss.

The study conducted on APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice revealed that amyloid plaques selectively reduced non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, while aging reduced REM sleep. The findings suggest that amyloid plaque emergence is the critical driver of sleep loss and that microglia expansion in plaque and non-plaque containing regions may contribute to this phenotype. Depletion of microglia using Pexidartinib increased sleep by over 2 hours per day in APP/PS1 mice, implying that targeting microglia is sufficient to improve both sleep quantity and quality.

METHODS

The study aims to comprehend Alzheimer's disease mechanisms in mice and potential treatment targets. Methods include:

  1. Genetically modifying mice (6 or 18 months old) with characteristics resembling Alzheimer's disease.
  2. Behavioral tests, EEG recording, MRI scans, tissue collection, biochemical analysis, histological analysis, light-sheet microscopy, and RNA extraction for gene expression analysis.
  3. In this specific study, treated APP/PS1 mice were homogenized, and RNA was extracted using QIAzol, handheld homogenizer, chloroform, and a RNeasy spin column.
  4. qPCR was performed on target genes (Ilb1β, Tnfα, Cd68, Clec7a, P2ry12, Tmem119, Gfap, Apoe) compared to β-actin as a loading control. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 with p<0.05 as the significance threshold, and outliers were removed using the Grubbs test.

RESULTS

The research study investigates the impact of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and aging on sleep patterns and brain activity, focusing on the role of microglia.

Findings suggest that AD causes changes in EEG power, synchrony, and excitability associated with sleep loss, while aging impairs the regulation of consolidated sleep–wake states. In AD, amyloid plaques increase microglial density in cortical and hippocampal networks, particularly in the hippocampus of 18-month old mice. Aging also increases microglial density in the thalamus and hippocampus.

Tmem119+ microglia, associated with phagocytosis, increased in the cortex and hippocampus at 6 months in AD mice but decreased as plaque burden increased in older mice. Clec7a+ microglia, associated with inflammation, increased with further plaque burden in the cortex and hippocampus at 18 months. Aging selectively increased Clec7a+ microglia in the thalamus.

Microglial depletion using PLX3397 increased NREM and REM sleep, reducing wake time. The increase in NREM sleep was sustained after microglial repopulation but did not significantly increase REM sleep time compared to untreated AD mice. These findings suggest that microglia play a role in the sleep disturbances observed in AD, potentially through their influence on networks supporting NREM sleep.

The study also found that microglial depletion reduced inflammatory cytokines and homeostatic microglial markers, suggesting a less inflammatory milieu accompanying sleep restoration. Astrocytic processes expanded to fill the absence of microglia without increasing astrocyte numbers.

Overall, the results suggest that homeostatic microglia may be responsible for mediating sleep in APP/PS1 mice, a model for AD. Potential therapeutic strategies targeting microglia could be explored to treat sleep disturbances in AD.

DISCUSSION

In a study investigating the relationship between sleep disruption and Alzheimer's disease (AD), researchers used a model of cerebral amyloidosis (APP/PS1) to understand the impact of amyloid plaques and aging on sleep. The key finding was that amyloid plaques induce selective Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM) sleep loss, while sparing Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep, and this phenotype does not worsen with increasing plaque burden, suggesting an early, stable pathological sleep phenotype.

The study focused on the role of microglia in plaque-dependent sleep loss due to their reactivity in response to amyloid plaques. Increased cortical and thalamic microglial density was associated with reduced NREM and REM sleep, respectively. Microglial depletion restored over two hours of NREM sleep per day in APP/PS1 mice, demonstrating that reactive microglia are a major driver of NREM sleep loss associated with amyloid plaques.

The findings suggest that microglia contribute to sleep loss, especially as an early network-level response to amyloid plaques, and may be a target for therapies aimed at reversing sleep deficits in presymptomatic AD. The study also identified a distinct Electroencephalography (EEG) signature associated with amyloid plaques during wake and NREM sleep, suggesting that EEG could be a valuable functional biomarker for early disease detection and monitoring in presymptomatic AD.
