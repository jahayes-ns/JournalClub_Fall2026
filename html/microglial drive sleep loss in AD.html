Early microglial response to amyloid plaques drives sleep loss in Alzheimer’s disease

Constantino, Irmen, Lanning, Turner, Ashley, Carroll, Neary, Rubinow, Snipes, Macauley | 2026

Keywords: aging, amyloid plaques, amyloid-beta, microglia, sleep

Final Summary

This research study, published in 2026, investigated the relationship between microglial response to amyloid plaques and sleep loss in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The study was conducted by a team from the University of Kentucky, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, and LifeCanvas Technologies. The research focused on APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice at various stages of pathology, using sleep deprivation, electroencephalography/electromyography (EEG/EMG) recordings, quantitative EEG analysis, and whole-brain light-sheet microscopy to measure amyloid burden and microglial density.

Key findings include: 1. Amyloid plaques cause non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep loss that does not worsen with increased plaque burden. 2. Aging reduces REM sleep and eliminates sleep rebound. 3. Microglial expansion occurs in regions governing sleep–wake dynamics, both within and beyond plaque-bearing areas. 4. Microglia depletion restores over 2 hours of sleep per day without altering amyloid burden.

The study suggests that microglia are a reversible cause of amyloid-associated sleep loss, positioning sleep and EEG-based metrics as sensitive biomarkers for presymptomatic AD detection. The research could potentially lead to new therapeutic strategies for managing sleep disorders in AD patients.

Title: Early microglial response to amyloid plaques drives sleep loss in Alzheimer's disease

Authors: Nicholas J. Constantino, Riley E. Irmen, Matthew J. Lanning, Sierra M. Turner, Clair C. Ashley, Caitlin M. Carroll, Evan M. Neary, Dave Rubinow, J. Andy Snipes, Shannon L. Macauley

In this research article, published in 2026, the authors investigated the relationship between microglial response to amyloid plaques and sleep loss in Alzheimer's disease. The study was conducted at the University of Kentucky, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, and LifeCanvas Technologies.

The researchers found that an early microglial response to amyloid plaques in the brain is a key factor contributing to sleep disturbances observed in Alzheimer's disease. The study provides evidence for potential therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating this microglial response to alleviate sleep loss and potentially improve cognitive functioning in patients with Alzheimer's disease.

ABSTRACT

The study investigates the link between Alzheimer's disease (AD) and sleep disruption, focusing on APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice at various stages of pathology. Sleep deprivation, electroencephalography/electromyography (EEG/EMG) recordings, quantitative EEG analysis, and whole-brain light-sheet microscopy were used to measure amyloid burden and microglial density.

Key findings include: 1. Amyloid plaques cause non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep loss that does not worsen with increased plaque burden. 2. Aging reduces REM sleep and eliminates sleep rebound. 3. Microglial expansion occurs in regions governing sleep–wake dynamics, both within and beyond plaque-bearing areas. 4. Microglia depletion restores over 2 hours of sleep per day without altering amyloid burden.

The study suggests that microglia are a reversible cause of amyloid-associated sleep loss, positioning sleep and EEG-based metrics as sensitive biomarkers for presymptomatic AD detection.

BACKGROUND

The background section of the scientific paper discusses the bidirectional relationship between Alzheimer's disease (AD) and sleep. Amyloid plaques, an early pathological feature of AD, are associated with reduced sleep time, sleep fragmentation, and altered sleep efficiency. Sleep deprivation, in turn, increases amyloid-beta production and amyloid plaque burden, indicating a reciprocal relationship between amyloid pathology and sleep.

However, much of the understanding about sleep disruption in AD comes from clinical studies on individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or established dementia, where it is unclear how amyloid plaques contribute to sleep impairment prior to cognitive symptoms. This gap is compounded by preclinical studies that often rely on non-invasive sleep monitoring instead of electroencephalography (EEG), assess sleep at a single time point, or fail to consider aging.

The paper further explores the role of microglia in modulating sleep in AD. Microglia are known to regulate white matter integrity and neuronal excitability, but their role in modulating sleep is poorly understood. The study aims to examine how amyloid plaques disrupt sleep relative to aging and whether microglia drive this phenotype in APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice.

The findings suggest that amyloid plaques selectively reduce non–rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, while aging reduces REM sleep. Amyloid plaques induce hyperexcitability, cortical desynchrony, and network rigidity across sleep–wake states, while aging maintains cortical activity but destabilizes sleep–wake architecture. Microglia depletion using the CSFR1 inhibitor Pexidartinib increased sleep by over 2 hours per day in APP/PS1 mice, implicating microglia as a major contributor to plaque-related sleep loss.

METHODS

The study investigates the impact of drug PLX3397 on Alzheimer's disease in mice. Methods include behavioral testing (Morris water maze, open field test, elevated plus maze), electrophysiology, histology, immunofluorescence quantification, whole-brain light-sheet microscopy, and RNA extraction & qPCR. The goal is to understand PLX3397's effects on Alzheimer's progression for potential drug development.

The protocol involves treating APP/PS1 mice, with RNA extracted from tissue samples using a handheld homogenizer and QIAzol solution. Purification was done using a RNeasy spin column, and cDNA conversion followed. qPCR was performed on the cDNA for various target genes (Ilb1β, Tnfα, Cd68, Clec7a, P2ry12, Tmem119, Gfap, Apoe, β-actin). Statistical analysis used GraphPad Prism 10, with p<0.05 as the cutoff for significance and outliers removed using a Grubbs test.

RESULTS

The study investigates the effects of PLX3397-mediated microglial depletion on Alzheimer's disease (AD) mouse models. Key findings include:

  1. Microglial depletion reduced Iba1+ and Tmem119+ microglia counts, while Clec7a+ microglia remained unaffected. Proximity analysis showed a decrease in Iba1+ and Tmem119+ microglia within 50 µm of plaques, with no change in Clec7a+ microglia.

  2. Microglial depletion also led to a reduction of Iba1+ and Tmem119+ microglia beyond 5 µm from plaques, while Clec7a+ microglia remained unchanged. qPCR analysis showed reduced inflammatory cytokines Il1β and Tnfα, DAM marker Clec7a, and homeostatic microglial markers P2ry12 and Tmem119 in both the cortex and thalamus of APP/PS1 mice with PLX3397 treatment.

  3. After 10 days of microglial repopulation, time spent in NREM sleep remained elevated, resulting in approximately 1 hour and 40 minutes of additional sleep per day relative to untreated APP/PS1 mice, despite a complete repopulation of Iba1+ microglia.

  4. Microglial depletion altered astrocytes in PLX3397-treated APP/PS1 mice, with an increase in Gfap+ immunostaining (percent area) and no change in Gfap+ number (counts per mm2), suggesting that astrocytic processes expand to fill the absence of microglia without increasing the number of Gfap+ astrocytes.

In conclusion, microglial depletion reduces regional inflammatory signaling and may contribute to sleep rescue in APP/PS1 mice, though the role of altered astrocyte activity cannot be ruled out. This research could potentially lead to new therapeutic strategies for managing sleep disorders in AD patients.

DISCUSSION

The study aimed to understand the effects of amyloid plaques on sleep and the underlying mechanisms in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Using a model of cerebral amyloidosis (APP/PS1), the research found that amyloid plaques induce selective Non-REM (NREM) sleep loss while sparing REM sleep, and this phenotype does not worsen with increasing plaque burden. The non-progressive sleep disruption suggests that even moderate amyloid pathology is sufficient to establish a stable pathological sleep phenotype.

The study focused on the role of microglia in plaque-dependent sleep loss given their reactivity in response to amyloid plaques. Region-specific increases in microglia were observed across cortical and subcortical regions crucial for sleep regulation and sensory gating. Increased cortical microglia correlated with reduced NREM sleep due to amyloid plaques, while increased thalamic microglial density was associated with reduced REM sleep during normal aging.

Microglial depletion using CSF1R inhibitor PLX3397 restored over two hours of NREM sleep per day and eliminated the NREM sleep deficit in APP/PS1 mice, demonstrating that reactive microglia are a major driver of NREM sleep loss associated with amyloid plaques. Microglial depletion increased REM sleep, suggesting a role for microglia, particularly in thalamic circuits, in regulating REM sleep integrity. The study suggests that microglia are not only associated with but actively contribute to sleep loss, especially as an early, network-level response to amyloid plaques.

The findings have important implications for the early detection and staging of AD. Sleep disruption and altered cortical Electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns emerge with amyloid plaque formation, in the absence of tau pathology, neurodegeneration, and/or cognitive decline. The study positions EEG as a promising complementary biomarker for presymptomatic disease detection and monitoring. Integrating sleep and quantitative EEG measures into early AD frameworks may expand presymptomatic screening and improve evaluation of therapeutic efficacy during stages when intervention is most effective.
