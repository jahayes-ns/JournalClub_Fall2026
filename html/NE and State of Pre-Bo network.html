Final Summary

Title: Norepinephrine and Nitric Oxide Modulation of the PreBotanic Complex in Respiratory Rhythm Generation

This preprint study investigates the effects of Norepinephrine (NE) and Nitric Oxide (NO) on the preBotanic Complex (preBötC), a critical brainstem region for respiratory rhythm generation, using multi-electrode arrays (MEAs) and computational modeling.

The research question focuses on understanding how NE and NO modulate neuronal excitability, synchrony, and network dynamics within the preBötC. Key findings reveal that NE increases action potential frequency while decreasing the average action potential number during network bursts. NMDA receptor blockers reduce both action potential number and suppress the NE-driven increase in action potential frequency.

NO attenuates synchrony, decreasing bursting amplitude and frequency through activation of soluble guanylyl cyclase and cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG). Combined application of NA and NO leads to complex interactions, with overall effects on preBötC network dynamics dependent on their relative concentrations and specific ionotropic glutamate receptors being activated.

The study also highlights the potential role of NE and NO modulation in abnormal respiratory patterns associated with chronic hypoxia, offering insights for new therapeutic strategies for treating hypoxia-related respiratory disorders. However, further peer review is necessary as this is a preprint. The research will be available upon request, including experimental data, simulation code, and data processing code.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific study investigates the role of Norepinephrine (NE) on the preBotanic Complex (preBOTC) in rodents, focusing on its impact on inspiratory drive currents and potentials. The research uses brainstem slices from postnatal 8-13 day old CD-1 mice and employs various techniques such as electrophysiology, pharmacological manipulations, and modeling of calcium dynamics.

Experimental data was collected using multi-electrode arrays (MEAs) and computational data was generated from a Hodgkin-Huxley style conductance-based model. To investigate the effects of NMDA and Ca2+-activated non-specific cation (CAN) currents on network dynamics, the model parameters were manipulated.

The study analyzes neural synchrony using custom C++ simulation software, employing the exponential Euler method for numerical integration. Spike raster data are aggregated into 50-ms bins of spike counts to generate a vector time series, X(t), representing the spike count of each neuron at a given bin time. The overall synchrony of the population is characterized using a statistic derived from Golomb (2007), Masuda and Aihara (2004) and Harris et al. (2017).

The research includes experimental data, simulation code, and data processing code which will be made available upon request. The preprint is a bioRxiv preprint (not peer-reviewed) and was published on February 14, 2026.

RESULTS

This scientific study investigates the impact of norepinephrine (NE) on the preBotanic complex (preBötC) in the brainstem, essential for respiratory rhythm generation. The results show that NE has heterogeneous effects on neuronal excitability within the preBötC: it decreases the average action potential number during network bursts but increases action potential frequency. An NMDA receptor blocker (APV) further reduces both the action potential number and suppresses the NE-driven increase in action potential frequency.

NE shifts mean spike timing and peak action potential density closer to or ahead of the peak of the network burst, and these effects persist despite NMDAR loss. The study found no significant change in cycle skipping among active units during NE modulation but recruited previously silent units into participating in preBötC network bursts and increased firing frequency in the majority of inspiratory units.

The research utilized multi-electrode array (MEA) recordings to analyze individual unit behavior within the preBötC under baseline, during NE modulation, and following NMDAR blocking. The study found that NE affects unit excitability, reducing firing frequency in 24.3% of inspiratory units and silencing others. Changes in unit fidelity were observed, with some low-frequency (LF) units becoming high-frequency (HF) and vice versa.

To understand the role of enhanced NMDA receptor (NMDAR) activity in these changes, the researchers blocked NMDAR activity using APV, which decreased inspiratory unit fidelity. After NE modulation, blocking NMDAR activity reduced the percentage of preBötC network burst cycles that inspiratory units participate in, increased the probability of cycle skipping, and decreased the synchrony score of the networks, supporting the role of NMDAR in synchrony as predicted by an in silico model.

DISCUSSION

The paper discusses two molecules, Norepinephrine (NE) and Nitric Oxide (NO), and their effects on the pre-Botzinger Complex (preBötC), a brainstem region crucial for respiratory rhythm generation.

  1. NE enhances synchrony within the preBötC, increasing bursting amplitude and frequency via specific ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs). Blocking NMDAR reduces burst amplitude but not always frequency, suggesting a role in coordinating network synchronization.

  2. NO attenuates synchrony through activation of soluble guanylyl cyclase and cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG), decreasing bursting amplitude and frequency.

  3. The combined application of NA and NO leads to complex interactions, with overall effects on preBötC network dynamics depending on their relative concentrations and the specific iGluRs being activated.

  4. NA and NO modulate individual neuron bursting properties through distinct mechanisms.

The discussion section focuses on chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) and its impact on inspiratory activity at the level of the preBötC. CIH affects pacemaker and non-pacemaker neurons differently, leading to abnormal respiratory patterns. CIH alters synchronization of neuronal activity within the preBötC network, potentially contributing to breathing disorders associated with chronic hypoxia. The findings could lead to new therapeutic strategies for treating hypoxia-related respiratory disorders, although further peer review is necessary as this is a preprint.
