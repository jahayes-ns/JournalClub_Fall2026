A µ-opioid receptor superagonist analgesic with minimal adverse effects

Gomez, Ventriglia, Frangos, Sulima, Robertson, Sacco, Budinich, Giosan, Xie, Solis, Tischer, Bossert, Caldwell, Bonbrest, Essmann, Garçon-Poca, Choi, Noya, Limiac, Arce, Glatfelter, Robinson, Chen, Mullarkey, Brademan, Enten, Dunne, Quiroz, Schoenborn, Lee, Rais, Holt, Dannals, Shi, Hüttenhain, Ferré, Kiyatkin, Bonaventura, Shaham, Zachariou, Baumann, Skiniotis, Rice, Michaelides | 2026

Final Summary

The scientific paper investigates the novel synthetic opioids FNZ and its metabolite DFNZ, which exhibit strong affinity and activation of the mu-opioid receptor (MOR). The research question is to explore the unique spatiotemporal activation profiles of these compounds, their pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and effects on dopamine neurotransmission compared to morphine, fentanyl, and other opioids.

Methods used in the study include laboratory synthesis of FNZ and DFNZ, positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, [3H]FNZ and [3H]DFNZ brain uptake and distribution studies, behavioral tests for analgesia, motor, and thermoregulatory effects, antinociceptive assays in mice, mu-opioid receptor binding assays, radioligand competition binding assays, functional cell-based assays, mu-opioid receptor internalization assays, cell-based cAMP accumulation assays, and molecular dynamics simulations.

Key findings reveal that FNZ significantly increases the frequency and amplitude of fast dopamine transients compared to other drugs, while DFNZ does not. DFNZ increases dopamine transient duration over time but less than other drugs. DFNZ shows a preference for promoting slow dopamine transients and has limited capacity to stimulate dopamine neurotransmission compared to FNZ, morphine, and fentanyl. The qualitative differences in dopamine neurotransmission between FNZ and DFNZ may be due to pharmacodynamic differences at MOR–GAL1 heteromers.

The conclusions suggest that FNZ and its metabolite DFNZ could be promising therapeutic agents for targeting the mu-opioid receptor due to their unique spatiotemporal activation profiles. However, more research is needed to understand their pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profiles before they can be considered for clinical use. Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The combined preamble section of a scientific paper introduces two synthetic opioids, FNZ and its metabolite DFNZ. Both compounds exhibit strong affinity and activation of the mu-opioid receptor (MOR).

FNZ has a nitrobenzimidazole core with an ethylamine and a fluoroethyl group, and it forms salt bridges and hydrogen bonds with MOR residues. FNZ is rapidly metabolized in mouse liver microsomes into DFNZ, which retains MOR selectivity but shows divergent G-protein versus β-arrestin preference compared to FNZ.

In vitro kinetic bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assays reveal that both compounds have higher efficacies than DAMGO at Go, but the effect of DFNZ decreases significantly over time compared to DAMGO and FNZ. At β-arrestin 2, FNZ shows greater efficacy than DAMGO for the first 30 minutes, while DFNZ shows higher efficacies during this time and significantly greater efficacies over the full time course.

The cellular localization and trafficking profile of these compounds suggest that DFNZ leads to slower MOR internalization and endosomal trafficking compared to DAMGO. Overall, FNZ and its metabolite DFNZ could be promising therapeutic agents for targeting the mu-opioid receptor due to their unique spatiotemporal activation profiles.

The study also compares FNZ, DFNZ, morphine, and fentanyl in various aspects such as pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and effects on dopamine neuron activity. FNZ significantly increases the frequency and amplitude of fast (phasic) dopamine transients compared to other drugs, while DFNZ does not. DFNZ increases dopamine transient duration over time but less than other drugs. Overall, DFNZ shows a preference for promoting slow (tonic) over fast (phasic) dopamine transients and has limited capacity to stimulate dopamine neurotransmission compared to FNZ, morphine, and fentanyl.

The study suggests that the qualitative differences in dopamine neurotransmission between FNZ and DFNZ may be due to pharmacodynamic differences at MOR–GAL1 heteromers, which are critical to the weak dopaminergic effects of DFNZ in rats.

METHODS

The scientific paper describes various methods used in experiments related to mu-opioid receptors (MOR), focusing on the development of a new opioid drug called FNZ (and its metabolite DFNZ) for potential pain relief.

  1. Synthesis of FNZ and its metabolites - Laboratory synthesis of FNZ, including production of DFNZ as a major metabolite, with [3H]FNZ and [18F]FNZ used in PET imaging studies.
  2. Positron emission tomography (PET) - Use of [18F]FNZ in PET scans to study its distribution and metabolism in rats, with effects of the opioid antagonist naltrexone on FNZ binding discussed.
  3. [3H]FNZ and [3H]DFNZ brain uptake and distribution - Uptake of [3H]FNZ in rats and [3H]DFNZ in wild-type and PGP/BCRP-knockout mice, focusing on the distribution of these compounds in the brain.
  4. Analgesia, catalepsy, and hypothermia in rats - Behavioral study in rats to assess the analgesic, motor, and thermoregulatory effects of FNZ and DFNZ. Results show that both compounds produce analgesia, catalepsy (a motor impairment), and hypothermia (a decrease in body temperature).
  5. Antinociceptive assays in mice - Evaluation of FNZ's antinociceptive properties using the hot plate test on Oprm1-knockout mice, which lack functional opioid receptors and are therefore insensitive to most opioids. Results demonstrate that while FNZ is effective in wild-type mice, it has no effect in the knockout mice, suggesting that its action depends on functional opioid receptors.
