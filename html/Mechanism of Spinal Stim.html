Final Summary

In the study "Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation," Balaguer et al. (2025) investigate how spinal cord stimulation (SCS) restores motor control following paralysis in individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Contrary to prior assumptions, the research reveals that residual brain inputs do not facilitate SCS-mediated motor control but rather transform subthreshold SCS-generated excitatory postsynaptic potentials into action potentials, allowing for brain regulation and volitional rate control. The study also suggests that residual inhibitory drive can suppress unnecessary motor activity, expanding the functional range of SCS parameters, and highlights the limitation of SCS in severe injury cases due to a restricted set of functional stimulation parameters.

The researchers developed a model of the motoneuron membrane potential to investigate the integration of residual supraspinal excitatory drive and SCS pulses in enabling volitional motor control. The study found that participants could modulate firing to follow a sinusoidal profile during SCS, with higher force levels resulting in less pulse skipping, demonstrating fine control over force output. The study also showed that the stimulation amplitude required for voluntary production of force was close to the involuntary movement regime border, indicating a potential relationship between SCS and lesion severity.

Incorporating supraspinal inhibitory drive into the model expanded the voluntary regime and improved the ability to modulate motor neuron firing rate. Two participants with stroke from a clinical trial were able to inhibit SCS pulses, demonstrating their ability to volitionally inhibit suprathreshold SCS and expand the voluntary regime as predicted by the model. The research was supported by grants from the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, among others, and published on September 19, 2025, following revisions from December 9, 2024 to July 1, 2025.

PREAMBLE

In the study "Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation," Balaguer et al. investigate how spinal cord stimulation (SCS) restores motor control following paralysis. Contrary to prior assumptions, the research reveals that residual brain inputs do not facilitate SCS-mediated motor control but rather transform subthreshold SCS-generated excitatory postsynaptic potentials into action potentials. This entrainment of motoneuron firing allows for brain regulation and volitional rate control. The study also suggests that residual inhibitory drive can suppress unnecessary motor activity, expanding the functional range of SCS parameters, and highlights the limitation of SCS in severe injury cases due to a restricted set of functional stimulation parameters.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction discusses research on epidural electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS) and its effect on improving motor control in individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Clinical trials have shown that during SCS, humans with paralysis appear to regain voluntary control over previously paralyzed limbs, suggesting that SCS facilitates volitional movement rather than generating it. Studies have explored the neural pathways recruited by SCS, focusing on spinal circuits such as reflexes and central pattern generators, but without considering the contribution of residual supraspinal fibers.

The leading hypothesis is that SCS increases the excitability of spinal circuits and motoneurons, making them more responsive to supraspinal input. However, it's unclear how pulsatile stimulation could lead to membrane modulation of motoneurons, given the limitations in stimulation frequencies and membrane time constants.

The paper aims to investigate the integration of residual supraspinal excitatory drive and SCS pulses in the motoneuron membrane to enable volitional motor control. The authors have developed a model of the motoneuron membrane potential, testing their predictions against motor unit recordings in monkeys and humans with SCI and stroke. Preliminary results show that motoneuron action potentials can be triggered by subthreshold SCS pulses when there is residual supraspinal input, providing an alternative explanation for the facilitation of voluntary movement observed during SCS. The study also highlights a subset of SCS parameters enabling volitional motor control via combined residual excitatory and inhibitory action, with the size of this functional parameter space shrinking with lesion severity.

RESULTS

The study explores the modulation of force during spinal cord stimulation (SCS) and the impact of lesion severity, supraspinal inhibitory drive, and voluntary inhibition on the effectiveness of SCS.

  1. Motor units in SCS showed clear peaks at multiples of SCS interpulse intervals for all force levels. Participants could still modulate firing to follow a sinusoidal profile despite high SCS entrainment, with higher force levels resulting in less pulse skipping. These findings suggest that humans can finely control force output during SCS by changing the number of skipped pulses.

  2. The study found that the stimulation amplitude required for voluntary production of force was close to the involuntary movement regime border, indicating a potential relationship between SCS and lesion severity. The biophysical model showed that increasing lesion severity reduced the size of the voluntary movement regime, which was confirmed in a monkey experiment by shrinking the range of effective SCS parameters for voluntary movement.

  3. Incorporating supraspinal inhibitory drive into the model expanded the voluntary regime by turning action potentials triggered by supra-threshold SCS into subthreshold EPSPs, thereby enlarging the voluntary regime and improving the ability to modulate motor neuron firing rate.

  4. Lastly, two participants with stroke from a clinical trial were able to inhibit SCS pulses, demonstrating their ability to volitionally inhibit suprathreshold SCS and expand the voluntary regime as predicted by the model.

DISCUSSION

The research paper "Neural basis for volitional control during spinal cord stimulation" explores the mechanism by which the central nervous system integrates artificially induced activity to restore voluntary control over motoneuron firing during Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS). A computational model was developed to understand how neural firing rate can be modulated by natural activity, even when highly entrained to pulsatile artificial stimulation. The study suggests that this mechanism likely applies to other pulsatile neurostimulation technologies.
