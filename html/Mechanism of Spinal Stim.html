Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation

Balaguer, Prat-Ortega, Ostrowski, Borda, Verma, Yadav, Sorensen, de Freitas, Ensel, Donadio, Liang, Ho, Damiani, Grigsby, Fields, Gonzalez-Martinez, Gerszten, Fisher, Weber, Pirondini, Capogrosso | 2025

Final Summary

Title: Neural Mechanisms of Voluntary Motor Control Restoration with Spinal Cord Stimulation in Paralysis

This study by Balaguer et al., published on September 19, 2025, investigates the neural mechanisms behind restoring voluntary motor control following paralysis using spinal cord stimulation (SCS). The research challenges previous hypotheses that SCS facilitates residual supraspinal inputs to motoneurons by tonically increasing their excitability. Instead, it reveals that residual supraspinal inputs modulate the motoneuron's membrane potential and convert subthreshold SCS pulses into suprathreshold action potentials, thereby entraining motoneuron activity to SCS.

Despite this entrainment, residual supraspinal inputs can control motoneuron firing rates by determining the number of subthreshold SCS pulses transformed into action potentials, allowing for volitional modulation of motor output within a restricted set of SCS parameters. The study also predicts that residual supraspinal inhibitory drive could silence unwanted suprathreshold motoneuron activity, expanding the functional range of SCS parameters. However, the functional stimulation parameters are found to be limited by lesion severity, indicating an inherent constraint in SCS effectiveness for severe injuries.

The research was conducted on monkeys and humans with spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Key findings include highly entrained motor units that could still modulate firing rates, the requirement of stimulation amplitudes close to the involuntary movement regime border for voluntary production of force, and the potential expansion of the effective SCS parameter range through supraspinal inhibition. However, lesion severity reduced the size of the voluntary regime, with inhibition improving the ability to modulate motoneuron firing rate and execute fine motor control tasks.

The authors declare interests in spinal cord stimulation patents and affiliations with Reach Neuro, a company developing such technologies for stroke. Future work should focus on modeling long-term circuit changes for improved clinical efficacy. The study offers a promising step towards understanding the mechanisms enabling the recovery of voluntary motor control with SCS.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the scientific paper discusses the impact of epidural electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS) on motor control in individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Clinical trials have shown that SCS can facilitate voluntary movement, allowing paralyzed limbs to be controlled, although it does not generate movement itself. Mechanistic investigations have focused on the neural pathways recruited by SCS and the involvement of spinal circuits, but have neglected the contribution of residual supraspinal fibers. The leading hypothesis is that SCS increases the excitability of spinal circuits, particularly motoneurons, making them more responsive to supraspinal input. However, this hypothesis has not been experimentally tested.

The paper aims to study how residual supraspinal excitatory drive and SCS pulses interact in the motoneuron membrane to enable volitional motor control. The authors developed a model of the motoneuron membrane potential and tested their predictions against motor unit recordings in monkeys and humans with SCI and stroke. The results show that residual supraspinal inputs can trigger motoneuron action potentials, becoming entrained to the stimulation interpulse interval. These findings refute the hypothesis that SCS facilitates residual supraspinal inputs by tonically increasing motoneuron excitability and offer an alternative explanation for the observation of SCS-facilitated voluntary movement. The study is a first step towards understanding the mechanisms enabling the recovery of voluntary motor control with SCS.

RESULTS

The scientific paper discusses the modulation of force during spinal cord stimulation (SCS). Key findings include:

  1. Highly entrained motor units in SCS showed clear peaks at multiples of the SCS interpulse intervals for all force levels, despite high SCS entrainment, participants could still modulate firing to follow a sinusoidal profile. Higher force levels led to less pulse skipping by shifting the Inter-Stimulus Interval (ISI) histogram toward smaller values.

  2. The stimulation amplitude required to enable voluntary production of force was close to the involuntary movement regime border in some participants. Increasing lesion severity reduced the size of the voluntary movement regime, with higher SCS amplitudes potentiating muscle activity only in less severe lesions.

  3. Supraspinal inhibitory drive could enlarge the effective SCS parameter range by effectively silencing unwanted motoneuron activity and expanding the voluntary regime into the involuntary suprathreshold movement regime. Lesion severity still reduced the voluntary regime size, but inhibition increased the ability to modulate motoneuron firing rate and execute fine motor control tasks.

  4. Two participants with stroke were able to inhibit SCS pulses, thereby expanding the voluntary regime as predicted by the model. This was demonstrated by suppressing unwanted flexor reflexes induced by SCS during voluntary extension of their arm.

DISCUSSION

This scientific research paper explores the integration of artificially induced spinal cord stimulation (SCS) with natural neural activity to restore voluntary control over motoneuron firing during SCS for stroke patients. The study finds that highly entrained neural firing rates can still be modulated by natural activity, and suggests setting SCS slightly above threshold and training participants to sculpt motoneuron firing rate.

However, as lesion severity increases, the effective parameter regime for volitional control of spinal motoneurons shrinks, limiting SCS efficacy in severe paralysis cases. The study's limitations include only considering excitatory monosynaptic connections and not quantitatively predicting SCS parameters or motoneuron firing rates. Future work should focus on modeling long-term circuit changes for improved clinical efficacy.

The authors of the paper, led by L.E.F., have declared interests in spinal cord stimulation patents and affiliations with Reach Neuro, a company developing such technologies for stroke. The study was published on September 19, 2025, after revisions between December 9, 2024, and July 1, 2025.
