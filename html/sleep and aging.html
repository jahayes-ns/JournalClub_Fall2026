Inverted U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and phenotypic age in US adults: a population-based study

You, Chen, Liu, Zhang, Wang, Yang, Liu, Ma | 2024

Keywords: Sleep, Phenotypic age, Exercise, US population, Cross-sectional study

Final Summary

Title: Inverted U-shaped relationship between sleep duration, exercise, and phenotypic age in US adults

In this study, researchers investigate the relationship between sleep duration, exercise, and biological aging (phenotypic age) among a large sample of US adults using data from eight cycles of NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) from 2005 to 2020. The study found an inverted U-shaped association between sleep duration and phenotypic age, suggesting both short and long sleep durations may accelerate biological aging. Regular exercise was identified as a lifestyle intervention that can mitigate the negative effects of short sleep on aging, but high-volume exercise might increase inflammation for individuals with short sleep.

The study utilized weighted linear regression models and log-likelihood ratio tests to examine the association between sleep duration and phenotypic age while considering various confounding variables, including age groups, sex groups, race or ethnicity groups, marital status, poverty income ratio, education level, body mass index, smoke group, alcohol drink group, exercise group, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular diseases. Phenotypic age was calculated using ten age-linked variables such as chronological age, albumin, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte percent, mean red cell volume, red blood cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count.

While the study has strengths, including using nationally representative data, utilizing phenotypic age, and accounting for confounding factors, it has limitations such as cross-sectional data, self-reported sleep duration, lack of consideration for sleep quality or medication usage. The findings highlight the importance of maintaining optimal sleep habits for promoting healthy aging, with future research needed to establish causality and explore sleep quality's role in this relationship. Additionally, the study suggests a need for further exploration into the relationship between napping habits, split sleep schedules, and sleep fragmentation. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained, and data can be accessed through the CDC's NHANES website.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

Title: Inverted U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and phenotypic age in US adults

In this study, the authors investigate the trend of sleep duration in US adults and its relationship with phenotypic age. Phenotypic age was calculated based on biomarkers from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Sleep information was self-reported by participants. The researchers used weighted multivariable linear regression modeling to analyze the data, exploring a potential non-linear relationship using a restricted cubic spline model.

Key findings: 1. A declining proportion of individuals with insufficient and markedly deficient sleep time was observed since the 2015–2016 cycle. 2. Participants with extreme short (β = 0.582) and long sleep (β = 0.694) were positively associated with phenotypic age using the fully adjusted model. 3. The dose–response relationship between sleep duration and phenotypic age showed that long sleep duration could benefit from regular exercise activity, while short sleep duration with more exercise tended to have higher phenotypic age. 4. An inverted U-shaped relationship was found between short and long sleep durations and phenotypic age. 5. The study sheds light on the complex relationship between sleep and healthy aging, providing practical recommendations for promoting healthy sleep habits to help individuals live longer, healthier lives.

METHODS

Summary: This paper analyzes the association between sleep duration and phenotypic age using data from eight cycles of NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) from 2005 to 2020. A total of 48,762 participants were included in the trend analysis, while 13,569 participants with sleep data and phenotypic age were analyzed for the association between sleep duration and phenotypic age. The exposure variable was self-reported sleep duration categorized into long (≥8 hours), normal (7–<8 hours), short (6–<7 hours), and extreme short (<6 hours) sleep. Phenotypic age, a superior prognostic indicator for health, was calculated using ten age-linked variables such as chronological age, albumin, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte percent, mean red cell volume, red blood cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count. Covariates included age groups, sex groups, race or ethnicity groups, marital status, poverty income ratio, education level, body mass index, smoke group, alcohol drink group, exercise group, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular diseases. Statistical analyses were conducted using weighted linear regression models and log-likelihood ratio tests to examine the association between sleep duration and phenotypic age, considering various confounding variables and performing dose–response and stratified analyses. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained, and data can be accessed through the CDC's NHANES website.
