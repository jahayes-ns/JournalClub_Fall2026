The Small Molecule Nobiletin Targets the Molecular Oscillator to Enhance Circadian Rhythms and Protect against Metabolic Syndrome

He, Nohara, Park, Yong-Sung Park, Guillory, Zhao, Garcia, Koike, Chen Lee, Takahashi, Yoo, Chen | 2016

Final Summary

The study investigates the compound Nobiletin (NOB), a naturally occurring substance found in citrus peels, as a potential treatment for metabolic disorders by enhancing circadian clock function. In vitro testing on fibroblasts revealed NOB's ability to boost circadian clock amplitude, and subsequent experiments on diet-induced obese mice demonstrated that NOB improved metabolic function, increased energy expenditure, and locomotor activity in a Clock gene-dependent manner.

The study discovered that retinoid acid receptor-related orphan receptors (RORs) are direct targets of NOB, and the beneficial effects of NOB on metabolic disorders require these receptors. Microarray analysis revealed that NOB modulates the expression of genes related to metabolic regulation, with preliminary evidence suggesting that it may activate RORgt and bind directly to RORa and RORg, improving glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity.

The research highlights NOB's potential therapeutic benefits in treating metabolic disorders associated with diminished circadian amplitude, although further studies are required for validation and exploration of its full potential. The study was led by Z.C., funded by multiple grants, and published in Cell Metabolism in April 2016 after revisions in February 2016.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a high-throughput screening, researchers identified Nobiletin (NOB), a naturally occurring compound found in citrus peels, as a molecule that enhances circadian clock amplitude. This discovery was made using fibroblasts expressing PER2::Luc, a bioluminescent marker of the circadian clock.

When administered to diet-induced obese mice, NOB significantly improved metabolic function and increased energy expenditure and locomotor activity in a Clock gene-dependent manner. The study also found that the beneficial effects of NOB on metabolic disorders in db/db mutant mice require the clock.

In diet-induced obese mouse liver, NOB boosted clock protein levels and triggered significant gene expression remodeling. Retinoid acid receptor-related orphan receptors were identified as direct targets of NOB, suggesting a pharmacological intervention that strengthens circadian rhythms to combat metabolic diseases through the circadian gene network.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction summarizes that living organisms have an intrinsic circadian clock system to anticipate and exploit daily environmental changes, with mammals having a hierarchical organization led by the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN). The cell-autonomous molecular oscillator is composed of interlocked feedback loops, with the core loop consisting of positive (CLOCK/BMAL1 or NPAS2/BMAL1) and negative (PER1/2 and CRY1/2) arms. This system regulates metabolism, as both metabolites and metabolic gene expression exhibit circadian oscillations. Circadian misalignment in humans can cause metabolic disturbances such as glucose intolerance and hyperlipidemia. Recent studies have explored the manipulation of circadian rhythms to ameliorate the metabolic syndrome, including time-restricted intake of high-fat diet and pharmacological approaches involving clock-modulating small molecules. In this study, a natural compound, Nobiletin, is identified as enhancing the circadian clock and providing protection against metabolic syndrome in a Clock gene-dependent manner. The direct targets of NOB are identified as retinoid acid receptor-related orphan receptors (RORs), nuclear receptors functioning in the stabilization loop of the molecular oscillator.

RESULTS

The study investigates the effects of a compound named NOB on energy homeostasis and behavior in mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD). Key findings include:

  1. Improved circadian clock gene expression and oscillations, particularly in the liver, leading to restored metabolic output gene expression.
  2. Microarray analysis revealed that 251 and 56 genes showed altered expression patterns due to HFD, which were reversed by NOB. These NOB-responsive genes are enriched for functions related to metabolic regulation.
  3. Many of the NOB-responsive genes have promoter occupancy by core clock proteins, notably REV-ERBs and RORs.
  4. Preliminary evidence suggests that NOB may activate RORgt, although further validation was not conducted in this study.
  5. In a separate analysis, it was found that NOB binds directly to RORa and RORg with higher affinity for RORg, improving glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and reducing blood glucose levels in HFD-fed mice.
  6. Lipid levels were also decreased by NOB treatment in the blood and liver of HFD-fed mice, leading to improvements in liver health.
  7. Functional assays demonstrated that NOB activates RORs, particularly RORa/g, which are essential for enhancing NOB's activity on Bmal1 transcription.

In summary, the study suggests that NOB improves metabolic outcomes and may have therapeutic potential in mice fed a high-fat diet through direct binding to and activation of ROR proteins. Further research is needed to validate and explore the potential of NOB as a treatment for metabolic disorders.

DISCUSSION

The study discovers a natural compound called NOB that strengthens the circadian clock and safeguards against metabolic syndrome. A high-throughput chemical screen was conducted using mouse fibroblast cells to identify circadian clock modulators, followed by treatments of male mice on a high-fat diet with either vehicle or NOB.

Metabolic analyses, microarray analysis of liver tissues, and molecular and biochemical assays were carried out to understand the interaction between NOB and specific receptors (RORs). Data was presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was determined using ANOVA tests. The findings indicate that NOB protects against metabolic syndrome in a clock-dependent manner, potentially influencing future applications for various diseases linked to diminished circadian amplitude.

The research, led by Z.C., was supported by multiple grants including those from The Welch Foundation, American Heart Association, NIH, and others.
