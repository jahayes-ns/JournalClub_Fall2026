Final Summary

The Nature Medicine study explores the feasibility of cervical epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for improving upper limb motor function in individuals with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis. Over a four-week period, seven participants underwent SCS implantation and demonstrated significant improvements in strength (+32% MVC), reduced spasticity, and increased Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) points on average by +6.6 compared to baseline. Hand/finger movements improved in participants with residual corticospinal connectivity.

While both short-term studies show promising results for SCS as a potential treatment option, further research is required to establish its long-term effectiveness and safety, as well as the determinants of responsiveness to SCS, particularly spared sensory function. The study employs various assessment techniques for motor function, muscle activity, spasticity, sensory perception, corticospinal excitability, brain activation patterns, quality of life, pain, and mood. Data availability is on the Database Archive of the BRAIN Initiative, with preliminary findings suggesting that personalized calibration of SCS parameters can lead to improvements in arm function and strength. The clinical trial identifier for this study is NCT04512690.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This Nature Medicine paper is a feasibility study on the use of cervical epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for improving upper limb motor function in individuals with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis. The study involved seven participants with profound motor deficits (Fugl-Meyer Assessment scores ranging from 15 to 35). Over a four-week period, each participant was implanted with two SCS leads in the cervical spinal cord. No serious adverse events occurred during the study.

Under SCS ON, immediate improvements were observed in motor function regardless of impairment severity, with an average increase of +32% strength and +5.6 FMA points. Three participants with residual corticospinal connectivity to finger muscles showed improved hand/finger movement. Despite limited motor activity (8.6 hours), participants demonstrated an average improvement of +6.6 FMA points compared to baseline, and spasticity decreased in all participants.

The study suggests that cervical SCS is safe, feasible, and effective for chronic post-stroke hemiparesis, indicating its potential as a fully implantable neuroprosthetic solution for assisting upper limb function in daily living. Further research is needed to determine the determinants of responsiveness to SCS, particularly spared sensory function. The clinical trial identifier is NCT04512690.

RESULTS

In two separate studies, the effects of Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) on motor impairment due to post-stroke hemiparesis were investigated.

  1. The first study found that SCS led to immediate and significant improvements in strength, arm dexterity, reduced abnormal synergies, and increased scores on FMA and ARAT scales. These functional gains were observed after just 2 weeks of intervention with no serious adverse events reported.

  2. In the second study, published in Nature Medicine, ten participants underwent SCS implantation for up to 4 weeks. SCS increased maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) strength by an average of 32%, selectively activating appropriate spinal levels. SCS also significantly reduced muscle tone and improved spasticity in most participants, with baseline sensory function being a stronger determinant of responsiveness to SCS compared to initial motor impairment.

Hand and finger improvements were predominantly observed as assistive effects of SCS, but the effects on functional motor performance were task-dependent, with no improvements in complex multi-joint movements despite increased strength during MVC tasks. This suggests that SCS modulates supraspinal neural drive in a task-specific manner.

Both studies suggest that SCS may be a promising treatment option for individuals with post-stroke hemiparesis, improving motor function and reducing spasticity. However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and determine the long-term effects of SCS on motor impairment in this population.

DISCUSSION

The study on spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for stroke rehabilitation shows significant improvements in motor function, hand movements, and spasticity for individuals with both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. However, the small sample size and short time period limit the validity of results. SCS demonstrates potential as a neuroprosthetic technology, with optimal lead placement and fine-tuning of SCS parameters crucial for meaningful effects. Future research should focus on long-term effectiveness and safety, larger populations, and longer study durations to further confirm these findings. METHODS

The study summarized in the excerpt investigates the effects of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) on upper limb motor function in chronic stroke patients. Participants underwent SCS surgery followed by various tests to assess their upper limb motor function before and after treatment.

The primary outcome measure was hand-opening ability, but additional tests included measurements of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) force during isolated movements, grip strength, arm kinematics during a reach-and-pull task using a robotic augmented reality exoskeleton system, and more. The study found significant improvements in upper limb strength and dexterity after SCS treatment.

Safety measures included the documentation of adverse events, reporting to the data safety and monitoring board (DSMB), and adherence to IRB directives. Spinal selectivity index was developed to identify the most targeted spinal levels by SCS parameters used during MVC tasks.

The methods section of the paper employs various assessment techniques for motor function, muscle activity, spasticity, sensory perception, corticospinal excitability, brain activation patterns, quality of life, pain, and mood. Assessments include Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), Motor Assessment Scale (MAS), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET), and several questionnaires.

The study utilizes three types of tasks: strength tasks using the HUMAC isokinetic system, reaching tasks using the KINARM exoskeleton robot, and other functional tasks. Statistical analysis involved bootstrapping at both participant and group levels using MATLAB R2022b. Data availability is on the Database Archive of the BRAIN Initiative, and all software used to produce figures will be available upon request.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

The study investigates the impact of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) on arm kinematics during reach and pull phases, as well as upper-extremity motor function using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA).

The research focuses on individuals with upper limb motor impairments, aiming to enhance movement function and strength by personalizing SCS parameters based on individual recruitment curves. Significant improvements in at least one joint were observed for each participant during SCS ON.

Figures demonstrate the placement of SCS leads, spinal segment maps, and clinical configurations. They also show the calibration process through recruitment curves and associated activation profiles. Maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) values were measured, with SCS found to enhance strength significantly.

Arm kinematics during reach and pull phases displayed improvements for some targets under SCS ON, as shown in Extended Data Figures 5-7. The TRI/BIC ratio increased with SCS ON, while no difference was observed in TRI/BR. A significant negative correlation was found between the percentage change in TRI/BIC and log dimensionless jerk, but not for TRI/BR.

The FMA assessment was used to evaluate the effects of SCS on upper-extremity motor function, with web plots showing normalized changes in FMA subcomponent scores across different timepoints. Assistive effects (change in FMA scores between SCS ON and OFF conditions) and therapeutic effects (change in FMA scores over time for SCS OFF condition) were noted.

Preliminary findings suggest that personalized calibration of SCS parameters can lead to improvements in arm function and strength, but further studies are needed to confirm and expand upon these results.
