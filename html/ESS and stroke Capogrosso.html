Spinal cord stimulation for upper limb motor function in people with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis: a feasibility trial

2026

Final Summary

This study investigates the feasibility and safety of cervical epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) as a potential neuroprosthetic solution for chronic post-stroke upper limb hemiparesis. Seven participants with profound motor deficits were implanted with SCS leads in their cervical spinal cord for four weeks, resulting in immediate motor function improvements, an average +32% strength, and +5.6 FMA points under SCS ON, despite performing only 8.6 hours of motor activity during the study. Participants demonstrated significant improvements in clinical assessments (ARAT) and functional living tasks, with reduced spasticity in all participants. The study found no serious adverse events and improvements in motor function for all participants by an average of +6.6 FMA points at the end of the study compared to baseline. Preliminary analysis suggests that spared sensory function may be a determinant of responsiveness to SCS, providing preliminary evidence for the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of cervical SCS for chronic post-stroke hemiparesis. The clinical trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04512690.

Methods used include surgical procedures guided by fluoroscopy and neuromonitoring systems, electrophysiological assessments such as MEP, SSEP, hand-opening ability, MRI, EMG, fMRI, TMS, a robotic augmented reality exoskeleton system to measure arm kinematics during reach-and-pull tasks, intraoperative fluoroscopy to determine the position of SCS leads along the spinal cord, development of a selectivity index, various assessment tools like ARAT, BBT, MAS, and simulation of daily living tasks for evaluating motor function and spasticity, surface EMG recordings to monitor muscle activity during movements, and statistical analysis using bootstrapping methods in MATLAB R2022b and SPSS Statistics version 29. The study suggests that SCS could be a feasible neuroprosthetic technology for treating movement dysfunctions and spasticity in post-stroke patients, but further research is needed to fully understand the effectiveness and safety of this intervention.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This study explores the feasibility and safety of cervical epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) as a potential neuroprosthetic solution for chronic post-stroke upper limb hemiparesis. Seven participants with profound motor deficits were implanted with SCS leads in their cervical spinal cord for four weeks, resulting in immediate motor function improvements (average +32% strength and +5.6 FMA points) under SCS ON, despite performing only 8.6 hours of motor activity during the study. Three participants who had residual corticospinal connectivity to finger muscles showed improved hand/finger movement with SCS. The study found no serious adverse events and improvements in motor function for all participants by an average of +6.6 FMA points at the end of the study compared to baseline, with spasticity decreasing in all participants. Preliminary analysis suggests that spared sensory function may be a determinant of responsiveness to SCS, providing preliminary evidence for the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of cervical SCS for chronic post-stroke hemiparesis. The clinical trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04512690.

RESULTS

The study explores the impact of Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) on motor function and spasticity in post-stroke hemiparesis patients. Key findings include:

  1. SCS enhanced maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) force by 32%, particularly for participant SCS04, with appropriate spinal activation crucial for strength improvement.

  2. SCS led to immediate reductions in muscle tone and significant decreases in Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) scores, suggesting a potent therapeutic effect on spasticity.

  3. Baseline sensory function was a stronger predictor of SCS responsiveness compared to initial motor impairment. However, hand and finger improvements were predominantly seen in participants with residual finger movement or MEP positive status.

  4. Participants demonstrated significant improvements in clinical assessments (ARAT) and functional living tasks under SCS ON, with participant SCS07 exhibiting a two-fold improvement in the Box and Block Test.

  5. SCS showed benefits on arm reach kinematics, particularly in straightness and smoothness metrics. The triceps brachii and biceps myoelectric activity were amplified under SCS ON, while brachioradialis activity was suppressed.

  6. Reduced co-contraction between the triceps and antagonist muscles (brachioradialis and biceps) under SCS ON contributed to improved movement smoothness.

  7. The motor function benefits of SCS were maximized when SCS was activated, as follow-up assessments decreased relative to week 4 under the SCS ON condition. The study suggests that SCS may be a promising treatment for post-stroke hemiparesis, offering immediate improvements in strength and dexterity, and potential long-term therapeutic effects. However, further research is necessary to fully understand the effectiveness and safety of this intervention.

DISCUSSION

The study explores the use of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for post-stroke rehabilitation, finding it effective in improving motor function, hand movements, and reducing spasticity in stroke patients. The optimization process for SCS parameters was practical for clinical use. However, the small sample size, absence of a control group, focus on specific patient groups, and lack of long-term data are noted as limitations.

The study found an average improvement of +5.14 on the FMA motor scale after 4 weeks in post-stroke hemiparesis patients, suggesting potential for SCS in a broad stroke population. The absence of a control group and participants' awareness of stimulation conditions were addressed by testing different contact configurations. Future research should address these limitations with larger populations, longer time periods, direct comparisons with controls, and real-world arm use assessments.

In conclusion, the results suggest that SCS could be a feasible neuroprosthetic technology for treating movement dysfunctions and spasticity in post-stroke patients. For further details, refer to the online content available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04435-1.

METHODS

The combined text from the scientific papers discusses the use of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for treating stroke-induced paralysis and upper limb motor impairments due to neurological conditions.

Methods used in the studies include: 1. Surgical procedures guided by fluoroscopy and neuromonitoring systems. 2. Electrophysiological assessments such as MEP, SSEP, hand-opening ability, MRI, EMG, fMRI, TMS. 3. Robotic augmented reality exoskeleton system to measure arm kinematics during reach-and-pull tasks. 4. Intraoperative fluoroscopy to determine the position of SCS leads along the spinal cord. 5. Development of a selectivity index to quantify the association between the spinal levels targeted by SCS and corresponding improvements in upper limb strength during MVC tasks. 6. Various assessment tools like ARAT, BBT, MAS, and simulation of daily living tasks for evaluating motor function and spasticity. 7. Surface EMG recordings to monitor muscle activity during movements. 8. Statistical analysis using bootstrapping methods in MATLAB R2022b and SPSS Statistics version 29.

The studies found that SCS improved upper limb strength and dexterity in individuals with neurological conditions, particularly when the stimulation was delivered at spinal levels corresponding to the targeted muscles during MVC tasks. However, more research is needed to confirm these findings and optimize treatment protocols. Anonymized data from the studies can be found on the Database Archive of the BRAIN Initiative, and software used to produce figures will be made available upon request to the corresponding authors.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

The study investigates the impact of Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) on arm kinematics and upper-extremity motor function in chronic stroke patients. Seven participants received SCS with two leads targeting specific spinal cord areas.

Adverse events were reported in three participants, but no serious ones occurred. The SCS parameters were calibrated based on muscle recruitment curves, improving strength, kinematic metrics, and muscle synergies during specific tasks.

During the reach phase, SCS led to increased agonist-antagonist decoupling, as indicated by an increased TRI/BIC ratio with a significant negative correlation between percentage change in TRI/BIC and log dimensionless jerk. However, there was no significant correlation for TRI/BR.

Arm kinematics improved significantly during both reach and pull phases under SCS ON compared to OFF conditions, as shown in Extended Data Figures 5 and 6. The Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) was used to assess upper-extremity motor function, with assistive effects observed across different timepoints (pre-implant, week 2, and week 4).

The study suggests that SCS may be a promising treatment for chronic stroke patients, but further research is needed to optimize SCS parameters, assess long-term safety, and evaluate its efficacy in improving motor function.
