Final Summary

This scientific paper published in Nature Metabolism investigates the role of hyperglycosylation, specifically N-glycosylation, in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The study used spatial metabolomics, lipidomics, and glycomics to examine the impact of altered glycosylation on AD homeostasis in both transgenic mouse models and post-mortem human samples.

The research findings suggest that excessive N-glycosylation contributes to the conserved phenotype of brain hyperglycosylation in AD, and reducing this excess improves social recognition memory in AD mouse models. Genetic knockdown of glycan biosynthetic enzymes showed improved cognitive outcomes, while glucosamine supplementation worsened behavior in these mice. A retrospective analysis of electronic health records revealed that glucosamine supplementation was associated with accelerated AD progression and reduced survival in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia.

The study highlights the importance of targeting glycan metabolism as a potential therapeutic strategy for Alzheimer's disease, emphasizing the need for further investigation into cell-type-specific spatial information and pharmacological interventions targeting the hexosamine pathway in animal models. A large-scale, double-blind clinical trial is recommended to determine the impact of glucosamine on AD progression at the population level.

The methods used included metabolomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, and glycoproteomics analyses, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analysis, behavioral testing, primary neuron culture, and a retrospective cohort study using electronic health records data. The research provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying cognitive processes like social recognition memory and dementia-related conditions.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The scientific paper published in Nature Metabolism identifies hyperglycosylation as a key driver of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Researchers used spatial metabolomics, lipidomics, and glycomics to investigate the impact of hyperglycosylation on AD homeostasis. The study found that increased glycan biosynthesis contributes to the conserved phenotype of brain hyperglycosylation in both transgenic AD mouse models and post-mortem human AD samples.

Genetic knockdown of glycan biosynthetic enzymes improved cognitive outcomes in AD mice, while oral glucosamine supplementation worsened their behavior. A retrospective analysis of electronic health records showed that glucosamine supplementation was associated with accelerated AD progression and worsened survival in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. The findings suggest that hyperglycosylation may play a critical role in the pathology of AD, making glycan metabolism an actionable target for potential therapeutic strategies against Alzheimer's disease.

RESULTS

The research summarized investigates the impact of altered glycosylation, specifically N-glycosylation, on Alzheimer's disease (AD) in mice. The study found that reducing excessive N-glycosylation improves social recognition memory in AD mouse models, suggesting it could be a potential therapeutic target for dementia-related memory decline.

The researchers used two methods to reduce glycosylation: suppressing phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) and using an OST inhibitor called NGI-1. Both methods showed improvements in social recognition memory compared to control mice. Contrary to these findings, glucosamine supplementation, known to increase brain glycans, exacerbated social memory deficits in AD mouse models.

In real-world evidence from a cohort of patients, the researchers found that glucosamine use was associated with adverse outcomes in dementia patients, particularly those with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). Ten-year all-cause survival curves for ADRD patients using glucosamine showed reduced survival compared to non-users. The cumulative incidence of transition from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to Alzheimer's disease over time revealed a higher conversion rate among glucosamine users compared with matched non-users.

The study highlights the importance of identifying medications like glucosamine in electronic health records (EHR) to better understand their potential effects on patient outcomes. However, it is important to note that a 2026 study published in Nature Metabolism found no impairment of social recognition memory in wild-type mice after glucosamine supplementation, suggesting a normal brain may have resilience mechanisms that mitigate the metabolic effects of glucosamine supplementation.

DISCUSSION

The study utilized MALDI–MSI-based spatial multiomics to discover a hyperglycosylation phenotype in Alzheimer's Disease (AD) brains. Both the 5xFAD and PS19 mouse models of AD showed increased N-glycans across vulnerable brain regions, suggesting that hyperglycosylation is a shared metabolic signature of neurodegeneration rather than being confined to a single driver mutation. The study also found a reprogramming of UDP-GlcNAc metabolism in AD, favoring expansion of complex glycosylation at the expense of cytosolic and extracellular glycan branches.

Targeting hyperglycosylation as a strategy against dementia was tested through three approaches: shRNA against PGM3, a small-molecule inhibitor of OST, and glucosamine supplementation. Both modalities reduced N-glycan levels in the AD mouse brains and improved memory performance. However, glucosamine usage was associated with worse survival in patients with AD/ADRD across real-world EHR analysis, suggesting that glucosamine may be a negative driver of neurodegeneration.

The study underscores the broader potential of MALDI imaging and spatial biology in AD research, advocating for further investigation into cell-type-specific spatial information through emerging technologies and the development of pharmacological interventions targeting the hexosamine pathway in animal models as a promising avenue for therapeutic exploration. Additionally, it emphasizes the urgent need for a large-scale, double-blind clinical trial to determine the impact of glucosamine on AD progression at the population level.

METHODS

The combined methods used in this study involve:

  1. Metabolomics, Proteomics, Transcriptomics, and Glycoproteomics analyses using LC-MS/MS and database search algorithms for identification of metabolites, proteins, and glycopeptides in brain tissue samples.

  2. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analysis to visualize specific proteins (e.g., GFAP, 6E10, Thr231) using fluorescence microscopy and quantitative analysis with HALO software. Antibody pre-adsorption assays were conducted for antibody validation.

  3. Behavioral testing to assess social memory in mice through exposure to novel conspecifics and recording interactions using DeepLabCut, an open-source deep learning platform.

  4. Primary neuron culture for studying cellular processes in vitro.

  5. Retrospective cohort study using EHR data from 2012 to 2024 to identify individuals with a diagnosis of MCI or AD and related dementias, and glucosamine users through a natural language processing pipeline.

  6. Survival and transition analyses were performed on the identified cohort using Kaplan–Meier analysis, propensity score matching, and subgroup analyses for all ages and those >50 years.

The research investigates changes in metabolism, protein expression, and gene expression associated with specific biological processes (e.g., glycosylation) in the brain, as well as assessing social memory in mice using a behavioral test. Additionally, it involves a retrospective cohort study to analyze survival and transition rates of individuals diagnosed with MCI or AD and related dementias, with a focus on glucosamine use. The methods used aim to provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying cognitive processes like social recognition memory and dementia-related conditions.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

The extended data figures of a Nature Metabolism paper explore the impact of the hexosamine pathway on brain N-glycans, social memory, astrocytosis, and amyloid plaque burden in mouse models. Experiments involved genetic knockdown of PGM3 (phosphoglucomutase 3) and pharmacological inhibition using NGI-1, as well as glucosamine supplementation.

Key findings include: - PGM3 knockdown reduces brain N-glycan levels and improves social memory in PS19 mice (Extended Data Fig. 6) - Minimal impact on brain N-glycans and social memory in WT mice with hexosamine pathway modulation or glucosamine supplementation (Extended Data Fig. 7) - No induction of astrocytosis or Aβ deposition in WT mice with glucosamine treatment or PGM3 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 8) - No changes in astrocytosis or amyloid plaque burden in 5xFAD mice with hexosamine pathway modulation (Extended Data Fig. 9)

The findings suggest potential therapeutic benefits for Alzheimer's disease through targeting the hexosamine pathway, specifically improving social memory and reducing brain N-glycan levels. However, further research is required to confirm these results and determine effective approaches for modulating this pathway as a treatment strategy. The paper acknowledges no significant impact of NGI-1 was observed in the cortex and hippocampus, based on data from five animals in each group. The findings were derived using a two-tailed t-test and presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.), with no substantial P value differences reported.
