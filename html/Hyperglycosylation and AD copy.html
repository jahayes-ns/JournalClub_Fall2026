Hyperglycosylation is a metabolic driver of Alzheimer’s disease

Liu, Ribas, Medina, Nielsen, Clarke, Ma, Mueller, Plasencia, Sheer, Simpson, Soto, Sudderth, Cai, Cantrell, Colpaert, Shedlock, Wu, Young, Kooser, Chen, Huang, He, Alonge, Guo, Bian, Vander Kooi, Gentry, Sun, Quinones, Son, Azadi, Deberardinis, Prokop, Allison, Yang, Chen, Huang, He, Guo, Gao, Kooi, Gentry, & Sun | 2026

Final Summary

This scientific paper published in Nature Metabolism investigates the role of hyperglycosylation in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The research question focuses on understanding if increased glycan biosynthesis causes brain hyperglycosylation, a conserved phenotype in AD, and its impact on cognitive impairments.

The study used spatial metabolomics, lipidomics, and glycomics analysis in transgenic AD mouse models and post-mortem human AD samples. Two key findings emerged: first, targeting phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) or using NGI-1, an OST inhibitor, reduced brain N-glycan abundance and improved cognitive outcomes in AD mice. Second, glucosamine supplementation increased glycosylation and exacerbated social memory deficits in AD mice, while having no impact on reactive astrocytes or β-amyloid plaques.

A retrospective analysis of electronic health records showed that glucosamine supplementation was associated with accelerated AD progression and reduced survival. The conclusions suggest hyperglycosylation as a potential target for dementia-related memory decline, establishing a causal role for excessive glycosylation in AD-related neurocognitive deficits.

The study utilized various techniques including Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) for glycopeptide analysis, RT-PCR and digital PCR for DNA/RNA quantification, glycoproteomics for identifying N- and O-linked glycans, behavioral testing for social memory assessment, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry analysis.

The research underscores the need for a well-designed clinical trial to systematically evaluate the impact of glucosamine on cognitive decline in patients with dementia, while emphasizing the potential risks associated with excessive glycan biosynthesis in disease advancement. The study also highlights the broader potential of MALDI imaging and spatial biology in AD research.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

This scientific paper, published in Nature Metabolism, investigates the role of hyperglycosylation in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The research team identified brain hyperglycosylation as a driver of AD through spatial metabolomics, lipidomics, and glycomics analysis in transgenic AD mouse models and post-mortem human AD samples.

The study demonstrated that increased glycan biosynthesis causes the conserved phenotype of brain hyperglycosylation in AD. Genetic knockdown of glycan biosynthetic enzymes improved cognitive outcomes in AD mice, while oral glucosamine supplementation worsened them.

A retrospective analysis of electronic health records from patients with AD showed that glucosamine supplementation was associated with accelerated AD progression and reduced survival. The results suggest that hyperglycosylation is a pathological driver of AD, highlighting glycan metabolism as an actionable target in the fight against AD. The research underscores the importance of metabolic impairments in early disease pathology and emphasizes potential risks associated with excessive glycan biosynthesis in disease advancement.

RESULTS

The study investigates hyperglycosylation in Alzheimer's disease (AD) brains and its impact on cognitive impairments. Researchers found an increase in N-glycan levels in mouse models of AD, particularly in the cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus. They identified two potential approaches to reduce glycosylation: 1. Targeting phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) with lentivirus via stereotaxic injection. After a two-week intervention period, mice showed improved social memory. 2. Using NGI-1, an OST inhibitor, which decreased brain N-glycan abundance and improved social recognition performance compared to vehicle controls.

Glucosamine supplementation increased glycosylation and exacerbated social memory deficits in AD mice, while having no impact on reactive astrocytes or β-amyloid plaques. The study also found that glucosamine use was more common in ADRD cohorts compared to MCI cohorts, with reduced survival rates in ADRD patients using glucosamine and a higher conversion rate from MCI to AD among glucosamine users. These findings suggest hyperglycosylation as a potential target for dementia-related memory decline and establish a causal role for excessive glycosylation in AD-related neurocognitive deficits.

DISCUSSION

The study utilizes MALDI-MSI-based spatial multiomics to uncover a hyperglycosylation phenotype in Alzheimer's Disease (AD) brains, a finding supported by two mouse models of AD. Hyperglycosylation suggests a reprogramming of UDP-GlcNAc metabolism favoring expansion of complex glycosylation at the expense of cytosolic and extracellular glycan branches. The study suggests that targeting the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway could be a viable therapeutic strategy for improving cognitive function in AD, independent of addressing neuroinflammation or amyloid clearance. However, glucosamine supplementation, which is widely used and varies in quality, was found to be associated with worse survival in patients with AD/ADRD but not in MCI populations. The findings emphasize the need for a well-designed clinical trial to systematically evaluate the impact of glucosamine on cognitive decline in patients with dementia. The study underscores the broader potential of MALDI imaging and spatial biology in AD research, advocating for further investigation into the impact of hyperglycosylation on neuronal function, glial response, blood–brain barrier, and glymphatic systems.

METHODS

The methods section of the scientific paper describes several techniques used for various analyses:

  1. Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) is used for glycopeptide analysis in proteomics to determine chemical composition.
  2. RT-PCR and digital PCR are used to amplify and quantify DNA or RNA sequences related to glycosylation enzymes.
  3. Glycoproteomics identifies and quantifies various N- and O-linked glycans in brain tissue.
  4. Behavioral testing is used for social memory testing on mice.
  5. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analysis are used to study proteins related to astrocytes, Alzheimer's disease, and glycosylation in brain tissue.

Specific procedures include: - Tissue processing for H&E staining, immunofluorescence, and quantitative analysis - Antibody validation through an antibody pre-adsorption assay - Primary neuron culture from C57BL/6J mouse embryos - Retrospective cohort study using EHR data for patient categorization and glucosamine use - Survival and transition analyses with Kaplan–Meier analyses and propensity score matching - Quantification and statistical analysis of all animals and data points, assuming normal distribution but not formally testing it.

The paper also includes a reporting summary and raw MALDI datasets and Proteomics datasets are accessible online.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

The combined acknowledgements section of the scientific paper in Nature Metabolism details research on brain N-glycans and their connection to Alzheimer's disease (AD). Key findings include:

  1. N-glycan levels remain unchanged in wild-type mice after modulating the hexosamine pathway or glucosamine supplementation, but are reduced in PS19 mice following PGM3 knockdown and associated with improved social memory.
  2. Astrocytosis and Aβ deposition remain unaltered by modifying the hexosamine pathway or glucosamine treatment in wild-type and AD models.
  3. The novel compound NGI-1 significantly impacts targeted biological processes, as shown in various experiments detailed in figures created using BioRender.

The study was conducted on five animals per group, with data presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). Statistical analysis involved a two-tailed t-test to determine P values for comparisons between groups.
