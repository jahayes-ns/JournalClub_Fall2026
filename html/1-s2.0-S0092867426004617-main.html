Final Summary

The research by Belliveau et al., published in Cell (2026), reveals Galvanin as an electric-field sensor crucial for directed migration in various rapidly moving cells like immune and epithelial cells. The study was conducted to identify proteins that directly sense electric fields, playing a role in cellular navigation beyond chemotaxis.

Through experiments with HL-60 neutrophils, T cells, and zebrafish keratocytes, it was found that Galvanin is required for electric-field guided migration. Upon exposure to electric fields, Galvanin localizes at the front and rear of cells, changes its spatial patterns of cellular protrusion and retraction, and exhibits an exponential decay along the electric field vector with a net charge of -18e. The intracellular domain of Galvanin regulates subsequent directed movement response.

Key findings suggest that a highly negatively charged construct can rescue directed migration in the knockout cell line, indicating that a high net negative ectodomain charge is sufficient for Galvanin-mediated electrotaxis. However, further studies are necessary to fully understand the molecular mechanisms underlying Galvanin's role in electrotaxis, including its implications for cell signaling and other biological processes.

In conclusion, this research reveals Galvanin as a biophysical electric-field sensor that plays a crucial role in directed migration in various cell types, providing additional directional information for wound repair and immune cell function. The study suggests multiple mechanisms mediate electrotaxis across different cell types and contexts.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study identifies Galvanin (TMEM154) as an electric-field sensor that links electrical cues to directed migration in rapidly moving cells, including immune and epithelial cells. The researchers found that the protein is required for electric-field-guided migration and that its relocalization occurs upon exposure to electric fields, leading to changes in spatial patterns of cellular protrusion and retraction. This suggests Galvanin acts as a direct sensor of electric fields, transducing spatial information about a cell's electrical environment to the intracellular migratory apparatus to support directed cell migration. The study was conducted by Belliveau et al., 2026, and published in Cell on June 25, 2026.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper discusses the importance of directed cell migration, focusing on chemotaxis and electrotaxis (also known as galvanotaxis). While much is understood about chemotaxis, less is known about how cells sense other spatial information in their environment, such as gradients in temperature, pH, or matrix stiffness. The paper also explores the role of wound-associated electric fields in directing cell migration towards injuries, and the complexity in understanding cellular electric-field detection due to diversity in electrotactic behavior across different cell types, electric-field magnitudes, and modes of cell movement. It notes that while several intracellular signaling components known to be involved in chemotaxis have also been shown to contribute to electrotaxis, the molecular mechanism of electric-field detection remains elusive. The paper aims to discover cell-surface proteins that act directly as electric-field sensors in rapidly moving cells.

RESULTS

The research paper discusses the role of the protein Galvanin in electrotaxis (directional movement towards an electric field) across various cell types, including HL-60 neutrophils, T cells, and zebrafish keratocytes.

In HL-60 neutrophils, Galvanin is crucial for directed migration during electrotaxis but not in chemotaxis. In T cells, Galvanin contributes to cathodal electrotaxis. In zebrafish keratocytes, knockout cells showed reduced directional bias compared to wild-type cells under electric field exposure. The study suggests Galvanin may be important for skin injury and wound healing, as well as immune cell function.

Key findings about the behavior of Galvanin under electric fields include: 1. Galvanin localizes at the front and rear of cells during electric cue exposure. 2. Galvanin exhibits an approximate exponential decay along the electric field vector, with an estimated net charge of -18e. 3. The effective diffusion coefficient for Galvanin-GFP is 0.25 μm^2/s under immobilization conditions. 4. The intracellular domain of Galvanin regulates subsequent directed movement response. 5. A highly negatively charged construct, replacing the ectodomain with a negatively charged XTEN linker and GFP, could rescue directed migration in the knockout cell line, indicating that a high net negative ectodomain charge is sufficient for Galvanin-mediated electrotaxis.

Further studies are needed to fully understand the molecular mechanisms underlying Galvanin's role in electrotaxis, including its implications for cell signaling and other biological processes.

DISCUSSION

The paper identifies Galvanin, a membrane protein, as an electric-field sensor that mediates cathode-directed cell migration. Loss of Galvanin expression disrupts directed migration in response to an electric field. Galvanin's rapid electrophoretic redistribution enables cells to quickly repolarize within minutes of exposure to an electric field, providing additional directional information for wound repair. Galvanin accumulates at the anodal side of individual cells and may steer cell migration by locally activating retraction or inhibiting protrusion. Future work will focus on understanding how Galvanin interacts with and regulates actomyosin activity and signaling pathways. The study suggests that multiple mechanisms mediate electrotaxis across different cell types and contexts, with Galvanin providing a biophysical mechanism for electric field sensing that is translated into polarized signaling responses. The paper also discusses the limitations of the study, such as potential bias in gene knockdowns affecting adhesion, indirect changes in cellular glycosylation, and underestimation of the inferred net charge due to electroosmotic fluid flow at the cell surface.
