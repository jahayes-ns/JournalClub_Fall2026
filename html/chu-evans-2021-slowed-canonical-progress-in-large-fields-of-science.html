Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The paper by Johan S. G. Chua and James A. Evans argues that an excess of published papers in a scientific field can lead to stagnation rather than progress. They propose that the deluge of new papers may deprive reviewers and readers of the cognitive slack required to fully recognize and understand novel ideas, and competition among many new ideas may prevent the gradual accumulation of focused attention on a promising new idea. The authors provide data supporting these predictions, showing that in large scientific fields, citations flow disproportionately to already well-cited papers, and newly published papers are unlikely to disrupt existing work or become highly cited. These findings suggest that policy measures may be needed to push scientific fields into new areas of study to avoid stagnation.

RESULTS

The study found that in larger scientific fields, the most-cited papers become durably dominant, capturing a disproportionately higher share of citations. This inequality increases with field size, with the top 0.1% most-cited papers collecting up to 16.7% of total citations when a field has 100,000 published papers per year. The bottom 50% least-cited papers decrease in share as the field grows larger.

As fields grow, canons crystallize, with the ranking and identity of the most-cited papers becoming more stable. The most-cited papers maintain their number of citations year over year in large fields, while other papers' citation counts decay. The probability of a paper ever reaching the top 0.1% most cited in its field decreases when it is published in the same year as many others.

When papers in large fields do become most cited, it is rarely through a process of local diffusion and preferential attachment. Instead, they shoot quickly to the top, inconsistent with a cumulative process where scholars discover new work by reading references cited in others' work. Most papers published in the same year as many others build on, rather than disrupt, existing literature. The probability of disruptive papers decreases as field size increases.

These findings support the predictions of the authors' theory, but alternative explanations cannot be ruled out completely. The study also finds that veteran scholars change their citation patterns as a field grows, with the field's size when an article is published having a much stronger effect on their citation behavior than the field size at the time they entered the field.

DISCUSSION

The study discusses the implications of a rapidly increasing number of scientific publications and their potential impact on fundamental progress in large scientific fields. The more-is-better approach to science may ironically hinder progress due to insufficient time for new ideas to be evaluated against old ones. This issue is exacerbated by the proliferation of journals, blurring journal hierarchies, and the online accessibility of articles.

Suggested solutions include establishing a clearer hierarchy of prestigious journals, reward systems that value fewer, deeper, more novel contributions, adopting a measure of novelty, and revising graduate training to encourage appreciation for novel ideas less rooted in established canon. While these changes may be challenging to implement, they could help push scholarship towards more innovative frontiers.

The study utilizes data from the Web of Science dataset, analyzing papers published between 1960 and 2014 across 241 subjects. The annual count of citations received by a paper from newly published papers in the same subject was the main variable of interest. The results suggest that papers published in years with many others in their subject tend to develop existing ideas rather than introduce disruptive new ones.
