Slowed canonical progress in large fields of science

Chu, Evans | 2021

Final Summary

The study by Johan S. G. Chu and James A. Evans investigates the impact of an increasing number of scientific publications on progress within various academic fields. Contrary to popular belief, the authors argue that a surge in new papers can impede progress by overwhelming reviewers and readers, hindering recognition and understanding of novel ideas. This deluge may lead to the entrenchment of top-cited papers, preventing new work from gaining prominence.

Based on an analysis of 1.8 billion citations among 90 million papers across 241 subjects between 1960 and 2014, key findings suggest that as fields grow larger, the most-cited papers become durably dominant, garnering disproportionately higher shares of citations. These top-cited papers form a "canon" that becomes more stable with larger field size. The study also reveals that papers published in large fields tend to build on existing literature rather than disrupt it, and that veteran scholars change their citation patterns as a field grows, citing canonical articles more often when many other papers are being published.

The authors propose several solutions to combat these issues: establishing a clearer hierarchy of prestigious journals, rewarding fewer, deeper, and more novel contributions, developing a measure of novelty for evaluating papers, revamping graduate training, and examining progress at lower subdisciplinary levels with more precise methods for classifying papers. These changes could help push scholarship toward more innovative frontiers, although implementing them across large fields may be challenging.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The paper by Johan S. G. Chu and James A. Evans explores the potential impact of an increasing number of published scientific papers on progress within various academic fields. Contrary to the assumption that more publications lead to faster scientific advancement, the authors argue that an overwhelming influx of new papers can actually hinder progress by depriving reviewers and readers of the cognitive capacity needed to fully recognize and understand novel ideas. This deluge may result in the entrenchment of top-cited papers, preventing new work from rising into the most-cited canon of a field.

The findings suggest that the current focus on quantity in scientific productivity may inhibit fundamental progress, especially as the annual volume of publications within each field continues to grow. To combat this issue, the authors propose policy measures aimed at restructuring the production, dissemination, consumption, and reward of scientific work to facilitate attention for promising, novel ideas.

Predictions based on these arguments include a higher likelihood of new citations being directed towards already well-cited papers, an ossification of the most-cited papers list, and decreased probability of a new paper becoming highly cited or disruptive. The study examines 1.8 billion citations among 90 million papers across 241 subjects to support these predictions.

RESULTS

The study analyzes citation patterns in the Web of Science dataset across various fields. It finds that as fields grow larger, the most-cited papers become durably dominant, garnering disproportionately higher shares of citations. These top-cited papers maintain their number of citations year over year while others decay. The largest fields have a Gini coefficient of citation shares around 0.5, as large as income inequality in some countries.

The most-cited papers also form a "canon" that becomes more stable with larger field size. When many papers are published, the most-cited papers see no decline in their citations, while less-cited papers lose a significant portion of their citations annually. The probability of a paper ever reaching the top 0.1% most cited in its field decreases when it is published in the same year as many others.

Most papers published in large fields build on existing literature rather than disrupting it, and papers that do disrupt tend to have a muted impact in larger fields. The study's findings align with the theory's predictions but cannot rule out other causal explanations, such as the passage of time and maturing of fields. The analysis also reveals that veteran scholars change their citation patterns as a field grows, citing canonical articles more often when many other papers are being published.

DISCUSSION

The study suggests that the rapid publication of scientific papers may hinder progress in large scientific fields due to insufficient consideration of new ideas against old ones, and a focus on quantity over quality. This issue is exacerbated by the proliferation of journals and blurring of journal hierarchies.

To address these concerns, the paper proposes several potential solutions:

  1. A clearer hierarchy of prestigious journals that publish less canon-centric work could foster disruptive scholarship.
  2. Reward systems that value fewer, deeper, more novel contributions and eschew quantity measures could reduce the deluge of papers and promote innovative work.
  3. A measure of novelty could provide a helpful guide for evaluating papers, grant applications, and scholars.
  4. Revamped graduate training to better appreciate less canonical ideas.
  5. Examining progress at lower subdisciplinary levels may require more precise methods for classifying papers.

The study uses the Web of Science dataset, analyzing 90 million papers published between 1960 and 2014. The analysis shows that papers published in years with many others in their subject tend to develop existing ideas rather than introduce disruptive new ones, and that papers published in the same year as many others in their subject are unlikely to be highly disruptive.

The paper acknowledges that these suggestions may not be easy to implement across large fields but could help push scholarship toward more novel frontiers.
