Slowed canonical progress in large fields of science

Chua, Evans | 2021

Keywords: scientific progress, durable dominance, entrepreneurial futility, science policy, science of science

Final Summary

Title: Quantity vs Quality in Scientific Publications: A Study on the Impact of Increasing Publication Volume on Progress in Large Fields

This paper by Johan S. G. Chua and James A. Evans argues that an increase in the number of scientific papers published each year in large fields can lead to stagnation rather than advancement due to cognitive overload and a competition among many new ideas. The study reveals key findings such as increased dominance of already well-cited papers, a more stable list of most-cited papers over time (canon crystallization), decreased probability for a paper to become highly cited in large fields, and fewer disruptive papers published as volume increases.

The authors discuss concerns about the current state of science due to an overemphasis on quantity over quality, suggesting potential policy measures like a clearer journal hierarchy, reward systems valuing novel contributions, revamped graduate training, and adopting a measure of novelty in evaluations and assessments to push fields into new areas of study. The findings call attention to the need for more precise methods to examine lower levels and raise concerns about missed opportunities for fertile new paradigms due to overworked areas of study.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The paper by Johan S. G. Chua and James A. Evans argues that an increase in the number of scientific papers published each year in large fields can lead to stagnation rather than advancement. This is due to a deluge of new papers depriving reviewers and readers of the cognitive slack required to fully recognize and understand novel ideas, and competition among many new ideas preventing the gradual accumulation of focused attention on a promising new idea. The authors provide data supporting these predictions, showing that when the number of papers published per year in a scientific field grows large, citations flow disproportionately to already well-cited papers, the list of most-cited papers ossifies, newly published papers are unlikely to ever become highly cited, and new papers become unlikely to disrupt existing work. The findings suggest that progress in large scientific fields may be slowed, trapped in an existing canon, and call for policy measures to shift how scientific work is produced, disseminated, consumed, and rewarded to push fields into new areas of study.

RESULTS

The study reveals several key findings about the distribution of citations in scientific fields as they grow larger:

  1. The most-cited papers become increasingly dominant and entrenched atop the citation distribution, capturing a disproportionate number of citations. This leads to increased inequality in attention. For instance, when Electrical and Electronic Engineering published 10,000 papers annually, the top 0.1% most-cited papers accounted for 1.5% of total citations, growing to 5.7% with 100,000 published papers per year.

  2. As fields grow larger, the ranking and identity of the most-cited papers become more stable over time (canon crystallization). The most-cited papers maintain their citation counts year over year, while all other papers’ citation counts decay.

  3. The probability of a paper ever reaching the top 0.1% most cited in its field decreases when it is published in the same year as many others, regardless of the field or time period.

  4. Most papers published in large fields build on existing literature rather than disrupting it. The likelihood of publishing disruptive papers drops significantly with increasing publication volumes, and even when a paper is disruptive, its impact is muted in larger fields.

These results support the predictions made by the authors' theory, although alternative explanations such as the passage of time and maturing of fields cannot be completely ruled out. The study also finds that veteran scholars change their citation patterns as a field grows in size.

DISCUSSION

The paper discusses concerns about the current state of science, particularly in large fields, due to an overemphasis on quantity over quality. Rapid publication rates may prevent new ideas from being thoroughly considered against old ones and hinder fundamental progress. The proliferation of journals and blurring of journal hierarchies exacerbate this problem.

Policy prescriptions like limiting annual publications or closing research institutions are impractical due to career implications, and simply reducing paper quantity may deter the publication of innovative ideas. Instead, suggestions include:

  1. A clearer hierarchy of journals dedicating pages to less canonically rooted work to foster disruptive scholarship.
  2. Reward and promotion systems that value fewer, deeper, more novel contributions at prestigious institutions.
  3. Adopting a measure of novelty vis-a-vis the canon for paper evaluations, grant applications, and scholar assessments.
  4. Revamped graduate training to encourage appreciation of uncomfortable novelty in ideas less rooted in established canon.

The study uses data from the Web of Science and focuses on fields and large subfields, acknowledging the need for more precise methods for examining lower levels. The findings suggest that while progress may still occur, increased research effort and money are now required to achieve similar scientific gains, raising concerns about missed fertile new paradigms due to overworked areas of study.
