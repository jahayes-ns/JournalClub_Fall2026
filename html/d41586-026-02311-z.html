The 'preamble (combined)' section of the scientific paper discusses the field of Biophotonics, which studies light emissions by living organisms, particularly biophotons from metabolic processes within cells, especially mitochondria. Applications include diagnostic uses such as tumor detection and assessing donated organ health, with cancer cells potentially emitting distinct biophoton patterns.

Research also explores biophoton emissions as a form of cell-to-cell communication, though evidence is not always conclusive due to weak signals standing out from background noise. Biophotons are also suggested to play roles in various biological processes, including red-light therapies for aging skin, joint pain, and neurodegenerative disorders.

A novel hypothesis proposes that brain cells may communicate using internally generated photons, or biophotons, along neuronal axons guided by the myelin sheath's higher refractive index, potentially enabling faster communication and aspects of learning and thought difficult to explain through electrical signaling alone. The theory suggests quantum effects could occur, with photons entangling with other atoms or electrons for information transmission.

Researchers propose testing this theory by silencing or genetically modifying photoreceptors in the brain or introducing extra background light and examining their impact on learning. Alison Sweeney from Yale University supports the idea that brains might have evolved to use internally generated light for communication. Efforts are being made to simplify technology for studying biophotons, increase awareness about their relevance, and gain acceptance within the scientific community. The challenge lies in addressing skepticism from some researchers who dismiss the existence of biophotons without proper investigation.
