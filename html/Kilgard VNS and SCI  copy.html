Closed-loop vagus nerve stimulation aids recovery from spinal cord injury

Kilgard, Epperson, Adehunoluwa, Swank, Porter, Pruitt, Gallaway, Stevens, Gillespie, Arnold, Powers, Hamilton, Naftalis, Foreman, Wigginton, Hays, Rennaker

Final Summary

The scientific paper discusses two studies investigating the effectiveness of neuromodulation therapy, specifically closed-loop vagus nerve stimulation (CLV) and continuous lung-stimulation vibratory (CLSV) therapy, in improving arm and hand function for individuals with spinal cord injuries.

Both studies showed significant improvements in motor function compared to sham stimulation. The first study reported an average increase of 936g in pinch force and 28.5N cm in wrist torque, while the second study demonstrated a 23% reduction in disability levels as measured by the Graded Redefined Assessment of Strength, Sensation, and Prevention (GRASSP).

However, both studies suggest that more research is needed to confirm long-term effects, determine efficacy in larger studies, and target additional muscles. Both therapies were found to be safe with no serious device-related adverse events or unexpected failures.

The studies also highlight the potential of Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS) paired with rehabilitation for individuals with upper limb motor impairment due to ischemic stroke, but further research is necessary to confirm these findings and determine long-term safety and efficacy.

DISCUSSION

The text you've provided appears to be an academic article discussing a clinical trial for Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS) in conjunction with rehabilitation for individuals suffering from upper limb motor impairment due to ischemic stroke.

The study aimed to determine the safety and efficacy of VNS when paired with rehabilitation therapy. The trial was a randomized, blinded, pivotal device trial involving 216 participants who were randomly assigned to receive either active VNS or sham stimulation.

The primary endpoint was upper limb motor function, as measured by the Fugl-Meyer Assessment Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) score. Secondary endpoints included other functional assessments and adverse events.

After 12 weeks of treatment, the active VNS group demonstrated significant improvement in upper limb motor function compared to the sham stimulation group. The improvements were more prominent in participants who received higher doses of stimulation.

The study concluded that VNS paired with rehabilitation may be an effective intervention for individuals with upper limb motor impairment following ischemic stroke. However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and to determine the long-term safety and efficacy of this treatment approach.

The text also discusses several related topics, such as optimizing dosing of VNS, patterns and predictors of functional recovery from traumatic spinal cord injury, and ethical considerations in conducting clinical trials for neurological injuries.

METHODS

The study is a double-blinded, randomized, sham-controlled early feasibility trial investigating the safety and effectiveness of a miniaturized Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS) device during high-intensity physical therapy for individuals with spinal cord injury.

The 19 participants underwent implantation, received individualized rehabilitation regimens, and were randomized to receive either active or sham stimulation in two phases, followed by active stimulation for all participants during the next 18 sessions.

The primary outcome measure was the GRASSP (Grade of Arm Function and Sensibility Test), with a clinically significant improvement set as a greater than four-point increase. The study found improvements in hand and wrist strength, speed, and range of motion across exercises, with untrained arm GRASSP scores significantly improved compared to baseline after 18 sessions of active VNS.

The number of VNS events delivered was not correlated with baseline or change in GRASSP score. Closed-loop VNS did not affect cardiovascular function. The study also reported adverse events, all classified as mild and related to the study.

Predicted GRASSP scores were derived using a linear regression model based on American Spinal Injury Association (AIS) and three subtotals of GRASSP. Extended data tables provide information about participant characteristics and reasons for discontinuation, adverse events, and predicted GRASSP score formula.
