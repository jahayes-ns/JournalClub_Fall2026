Final Summary

The study investigates the effectiveness of Closed-Loop Vagus Nerve Stimulation (CLV) combined with rehabilitation for individuals with chronic spinal cord injuries (SCI), aiming to enhance upper limb function recovery. In a double-blinded, randomized, sham-controlled early feasibility trial involving 19 implant surgeries, CLV showed significant improvements in pinch force, wrist torque, and upper limb motor function as measured by the Grasp Ability Scale for Upper Limb (GRASSP) and Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function (JTHF) scores.

The study found that CLV resulted in an average 23% disability reduction and demonstrated a medium-sized effect size over equivalent rehabilitation with sham stimulation. The researchers also developed a linear regression model to predict GRASSP scores using AIS, GRASSP subtotals: strength, palm sensation, and dorsal sensation. The therapy was safe with minimal adverse events and reduced voice alteration rates compared to conventional VNS for epilepsy. Future prospectively powered studies are needed to confirm its efficacy.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study presented is a research on Closed-Loop Vagus Nerve Stimulation (CLV) for individuals with chronic spinal cord injuries (SCI). It combines intensive rehabilitation and real-time vagus nerve stimulation delivered via a miniaturized implant to enhance recovery of upper limb function.

In the study, CLV demonstrated significant improvements in pinch force and wrist torque, with average gains of 393% and 152%, respectively. The therapy was personalized for each participant, and exercise difficulty was adjusted regularly.

During a randomized controlled trial (RCT), CLVS showed a medium-sized effect size over equivalent rehabilitation with sham stimulation. On average, the eight responders lost 23% of their disability as measured by the Grasp Ability Scale for Upper Limb (GRASSP).

The study concluded that CLV is safe and effective for improving hand and wrist strength, speed, and range of motion in individuals with chronic SCI. However, a prospectively powered study is needed to confirm its efficacy. The study involved 19 implant surgeries and resulted in minimal adverse events and reduced voice alteration rates compared to conventional VNS for epilepsy. No significant changes in heart rate or blood pressure were observed between active and sham stimulation, nor any instances of autonomic dysreflexia in individuals with SCI.

DISCUSSION

This text appears to be an academic article about a study on the use of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) paired with rehabilitation for stroke recovery. The study was conducted as part of the VNS-REHAB trial, a randomized, blinded, pivotal, device trial.

The main findings of the study are that VNS combined with rehabilitation led to significant improvements in upper limb motor function and motor impairment compared to sham VNS or no intervention. The study also found that certain parameters, such as the intensity and duration of VNS stimulation, may play a role in maximizing the effects of this treatment for stroke recovery.

The article discusses several previous studies on VNS for stroke and other neurological conditions, and highlights some potential limitations and future directions for research in this area. It also addresses concerns about the safety of VNS and its potential impact on cardiovascular function.

Overall, the study suggests that VNS may be a promising treatment option for improving upper limb motor function after stroke, with further research needed to optimize dosing and understand the underlying mechanisms of action. The article also emphasizes the need for greater diversity in clinical trials to ensure that findings are applicable to diverse patient populations.

METHODS

This study is a double-blinded, randomized, sham-controlled early feasibility trial for a miniaturized Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS) device in individuals with cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). The trial involved 18 sessions of rehabilitation, with half the participants receiving active VNS and the other half sham stimulation. Afterward, all participants underwent additional 18 sessions of rehabilitation with active VNS.

The primary outcome measure was a greater than four-point increase in GRASSP (a clinician-administered assessment of upper limb function and impairment). The study found improvements in hand and wrist strength, speed, range of motion, and Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function (JTHF) scores.

The researchers also developed a linear regression model using AIS, GRASSP subtotals: strength, palm sensation, and dorsal sensation to predict GRASSP scores in MATLAB. The model is summarized as Predicted GRASSP = (AIS x 4.9) + (-0.39 * Strength) - (0.29 * PalmSensation) + (0.24 * DorsalSensation) + 9.2.

Participant satisfaction with the therapy was high at the end of the study, and all reported adverse events were mild and related to the study. Detailed metrics for all participants can be found in the supplemental data file.
