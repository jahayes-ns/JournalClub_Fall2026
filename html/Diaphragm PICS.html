Final Summary

Title: Estimation of Persistent Inward Currents Contribution to Inspiratory Motoneuron Firing in Humans

Research Question: To investigate the role of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) in prolonging firing in human inspiratory muscles, specifically the diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles during both quiet and voluntary breathing.

Methods: The study employed linear mixed-effect models, false discovery rate analysis, and adapted 'self sustained firing duration' to analyze motor unit activity from multiple participants during quiet and voluntary breathing. Motor units that continued firing into the expiratory phase were compared between the diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles (1st, 3rd, and 5th) to estimate PIC contributions.

Key Findings: During quiet breathing, a higher proportion of diaphragmatic motor units continued firing during expiration compared to 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostals, suggesting potential differences in PIC impact between muscles. In voluntary breathing, no significant differences were found for the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles, but a significant difference was detected for the 5th parasternal intercostal muscle compared to both 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostals.

Conclusions: The study suggests that PICs play a greater role in driving diaphragm motoneuron activity during quiet breathing compared to parasternal intercostals, but their contribution may vary across different inspiratory muscles. These findings enhance understanding of respiratory muscle control and encourage further research to validate, estimate, or modulate possible PIC contributions in humans and animal models.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The paper titled 'Estimation of persistent inward currents contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing in humans' published in The Journal of Physiology, explores the role of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) in controlling breathing muscles. The study was conducted by Ricardo N.O. Mesquita and colleagues from multiple institutions in Sweden, Australia, and Finland.

The research focused on alpha motoneurons, which control contraction in breathing muscles. PICs, a flow of calcium and sodium ions into the motoneuron, are crucial for starting, amplifying, and sustaining motoneuron activity. The team recorded individual motor unit activity in inspiratory muscles during both quiet and voluntary breathing in humans.

The findings suggest that diaphragm motor units show firing patterns consistent with PIC contribution, whereas parasternal intercostal motor units do not. This indicates that PICs play a greater role in driving the activity of diaphragm motoneurons compared to the parasternal intercostals. The study reveals unexpected differences in how the nervous system controls each breathing muscle, suggesting that PICs do not contribute equally across all breathing muscles.

ABSTRACT

The study investigates the role of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) in the prolongation of firing in human inspiratory muscles, specifically the diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles. During quiet and voluntary breathing, motor unit activity was analyzed to estimate PIC contributions. Results suggest that diaphragm motor units exhibited a significant role of PICs due to a higher magnitude of firing prolongation compared to parasternal intercostal muscles, indicating a potential differential contribution of PICs across inspiratory muscles. This study enhances understanding of respiratory muscle control and encourages future research on validating, estimating, or modulating possible PIC contributions in humans and animal models.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction discusses the control of respiratory motoneuron firing and the role of persistent inward currents (PICs) in shaping this behavior. While PICs are known to be crucial in limb motoneurons, their contribution to human respiratory motoneuron firing remains unclear. Although animal data suggest minimal PIC involvement in diaphragmatic motoneuron firing amplification, the diversity of non-linear firing patterns that PICs can produce warrants further investigation in both diaphragm and intercostal inspiratory motor units in humans to better understand inspiratory motor control. The paper aims to examine whether motor unit activity recorded from major human inspiratory muscles exhibits firing patterns indicative of prolongation of firing induced by motoneuronal PICs, and if this potential PIC contribution differs between quiet (automatic) and voluntary breathing conditions.

METHODS

The study investigates the contribution of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) to inspiratory motor neuron firing in different muscles, including diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles. Methods employed include linear mixed-effect models, false discovery rate analysis, and a technique adapted from 'self sustained firing duration' to calculate inspiration-to-expiration motor unit firing duration ratios.

Statistical analyses were conducted in R, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Data is collected from multiple participants through a repeated measures approach. Motor unit data during quiet and voluntary breathing were analyzed using polynomial functions and correlated motor unit pairs were identified.

The study found that during quiet breathing, 84.0% of motor units were fitted with a 5th degree polynomial, while 61.1% were fitted during voluntary breathing. Correlated motor unit pairs were more prevalent in the parasternal intercostal muscles compared to the diaphragm.

Linear mixed-effect models and Z tests for independent proportions were used to compare PIC activity between quiet and voluntary breaths, and across different muscles respectively. Exploratory repeated measures correlations were computed to investigate associations between various parameters such as F values, recruitment thresholds, peak smoothed firing rates, and time between recruitments of control and test units.

RESULTS

In this study, 248 breaths were analyzed across four muscles (diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles 1st, 3rd, and 5th) during both quiet and voluntary breathing. During quiet breathing, no significant differences were detected between the muscles in terms of F scores, which estimate persistent inward currents (PICs). The diaphragm was the only muscle with a mean F score significantly greater than 1 Hz, while all muscles had F scores significantly greater than 0 Hz. When examining the proportion of motor units that continued to fire into the expiratory phase (duration ratio < 1), the diaphragm exhibited a significantly higher proportion than the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles, but not different to the 5th.

During voluntary breathing, no significant differences in F scores were observed between the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles when comparing matched motor unit pairs between conditions. However, a significant difference was found for the 5th parasternal intercostal muscle, which exhibited lower duration ratios compared to both the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles.

In both quiet and voluntary breathing, no significant correlations were found between recruitment time differences and F scores for any of the muscles. The diaphragm had a significantly higher proportion of motor units that continued to fire into the expiratory phase compared to the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles during quiet breathing. DISCUSSION

The discussion section of the study focuses on the challenges and implications of estimating Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing during short-duration breathing tasks. Key findings include:

  1. Breath durations shorter than typical ramp contraction times may underestimate PIC activation.
  2. Slower activation and deactivation of PICs compared to other voltage-gated ion channels were observed, potentially affecting their full activation in the study.
  3. Uncertainty remains about whether PICs fully deactivate between breaths, impacting motoneuron intrinsic excitability.
  4. Minimum time intervals between recruitment of control and test units were not employed due to short-duration breathing tasks.
  5. No significant associations were found between recruitment time differences and F scores in the analyses.
  6. Future studies could investigate motor unit activity during prolonged breaths or isometric contractions for full PIC activation.
  7. A higher proportion of diaphragmatic motor units continued firing during expiration compared to 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostals, suggesting potential differences in PIC impact between muscles.
  8. The study provides novel insights into PIC contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing, but further research is needed for confirmation and refinement. Future studies could explore sex differences and the role of PICs in parasternal intercostal muscles during non-respiratory ipsilateral rotations.
  9. New methodological paradigms could advance understanding of PICs in respiratory motor output.

METHODOLOGY

The methodology section of the paper discusses the investigation into the role of Phasically Active Units (PICs) in controlling firing output in human respiratory motoneurons. The study builds on previous research (Gorassini et al., 1998, 2002a, 2002b; Binder et al., 2020; Crone et al., 1988; Eken & Kiehn, 1989; Hounsgaard et al., 1988; Lee & Heckman, 1996; Schwindt & Crill, 1980; Li et al., 2004) that established self-sustained firing as a hallmark of PIC activity.

The study aims to determine whether the firing of these motor units is sustained by ongoing excitatory input or by strong intrinsic motoneuron excitability, such as subthreshold oscillations of sodium PICs. To achieve this, additional methodological approaches will be developed for investigating the role of PIC channels in various behaviors like sneezing, coughing, breath-holds, trunk rotations, vocalization, swallowing, or vomiting.

The research also aims to establish whether PICs contribute to inspiratory motoneuron firing and if interventions known to modulate PIC activity in spinal motoneurons could be effective for targeting respiratory motor output. The findings suggest that intrinsic motoneuron excitability contributes to the control of firing output in human respiratory motoneurons, with a significant contribution from motoneuronal PICs during quiet breathing in diaphragmatic motor units but not as evident in 1st, 3rd, and 5th parasternal intercostal motor units.

Future research should explore the mechanisms driving these variations in non-linear firing patterns and their functional implications for respiratory control in health and disease.
