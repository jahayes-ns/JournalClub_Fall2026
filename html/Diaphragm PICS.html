Estimation of persistent inward currents contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing in humans

Mesquita1,2,3, Gandevia4,5, Taylor5,6, Hudson7, Butler4,5 | 2026

Final Summary

The study 'Estimation of persistent inward currents contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing in humans', published in The Journal of Physiology, investigates the role of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) in controlling respiratory muscle contractions during both quiet and voluntary breathing. The research focused on alpha motoneurons in the diaphragm and parasternal intercostals.

Using motor unit activity data, the study found that diaphragm motor units exhibited firing patterns consistent with PIC contribution, indicating a greater role of PICs in driving the activity of diaphragm motoneurons compared to parasternal intercostals. No such effect was observed in parasternal intercostal muscles. The findings suggest a differential contribution of PICs across different inspiratory muscles and advance our understanding of respiratory muscle control mechanisms.

The study employed pharmacological interventions, repeated measures, linear mixed-effects models, and descriptive statistics to analyze the data. However, limitations arose in the analysis of 3rd and 5th parasternal intercostal muscles due to insufficient test units. The findings offer insights into the role of PICs in motor neuron function but require further research for complete understanding and potential application in treating respiratory disorders.

Future studies should investigate PIC's role during post-inspiratory activity, expiration, its impact on lung volumes and respiratory mechanics, corroborate or refine findings using interventions that elicit inhibitory inputs onto motoneurons, explore new methodological paradigms, and study PICs in parasternal intercostal muscles during ipsilateral rotations and non-respiratory activities.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

The study 'Estimation of persistent inward currents contribution to inspiratory motoneuron firing in humans' published in The Journal of Physiology explores the role of persistent inward currents (PICs) in controlling the contraction of breathing muscles. The research focused on alpha motoneurons, which send signals to these muscles. PICs, a sustained flow of calcium and sodium ions into the motoneuron, help initiate, amplify, and sustain motoneuron activity.

The researchers recorded the activity of individual motor units in inspiratory muscles during both quiet and voluntary breathing in humans. They found that diaphragm motor units exhibited firing patterns consistent with PIC contribution, suggesting a greater role of PICs in driving the activity of diaphragm motoneurons compared to parasternal intercostals. The findings suggest that PICs do not contribute equally to the control of all breathing muscles, implying unexpected differences in how the nervous system controls each breathing muscle.

ABSTRACT

The study investigates persistent inward currents (PICs) and their role in prolonging firing of motoneurons in human inspiratory muscles during involuntary (quiet) and voluntary breathing. The analysis was conducted on motor unit (MU) activity from the diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles.

Results showed that diaphragm MUs exhibited a significant PIC contribution, with higher firing hysteresis scores compared to other muscles. On the other hand, no such effect was observed in parasternal intercostal muscles. The study suggests a differential contribution of PICs across different inspiratory muscles and advances our understanding of respiratory muscle control mechanisms.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of this scientific paper outlines a study aimed at understanding the role of Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) in respiratory motoneuron firing, specifically during breathing. While PICs are known to play a significant role in shaping the firing behavior of limb motoneurons, their contribution to human respiratory motoneuron firing remains unexplored.

The paper discusses that while there is evidence for the presence of persistent calcium channels in feline phrenic motoneurons, PICs appear to contribute minimally to the amplification of synaptic inputs in diaphragmatic motoneuron firing. However, the diversity of non-linear firing patterns that PICs can produce, along with their potential presence in other respiratory motoneurons, provides a rationale for estimating PICs in both diaphragm and intercostal inspiratory motor units in humans.

The study plans to investigate the evidence for PICs in respiratory motoneurons during their typical firing behavior (i.e., during breathing). The paired motor unit technique could potentially be used on existing data during breathing to yield in vivo estimates of PIC contributions and advance our understanding of the neural mechanisms regulating respiratory muscle control. The study aims to determine if motor unit activity recorded from major human inspiratory muscles exhibits firing patterns indicative of prolongation of firing induced by motoneuronal PICs, and whether this potential PIC contribution differs between quiet (automatic) and voluntary breathing conditions.

METHODS

The study investigates the role of potassium channels (PICs) in dendrites during breathing-induced motor neuron firing in rats. The researchers used pharmacological interventions to manipulate PIC function while recording motor neuron activity.

Data analysis methods included a repeated measures approach for selecting suitable motor unit pairs and a linear mixed-effects model to minimize bias. Findings show that PIC activity significantly modulates the firing rate of inspiratory motor neurons, with many cases exceeding 1 Hz. This suggests PICs may regulate motor neuron excitability during breathing, potentially impacting respiratory rhythm and pattern generation.

The study also analyzed motor unit data during quiet and voluntary breathing using various methods, including descriptive statistics, polynomial fits, linear mixed-effect models, Z tests for independent proportions, and exploratory repeated measures correlations. However, limitations arose in the analysis of 3rd and 5th parasternal intercostal muscles due to insufficient test units. The findings offer insights into the role of PICs in motor neuron function but require further research for complete understanding and potential application in treating respiratory disorders.

RESULTS

In this study, 248 breaths were examined across four muscles (diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles 1st, 3rd, 5th) under quiet breathing conditions. The diaphragm was the only muscle with a mean frequency greater than 1 Hz, while all muscles showed frequencies significantly greater than 0 Hz. There were no significant differences in motor unit firing frequencies between muscles during quiet breathing. The average time difference between recruitment of control and test units ranged from 0.24 to 0.47 seconds across the four muscles, with no significant correlations found between these times and motor unit firing frequencies.

The diaphragm exhibited a higher proportion of motor units that continued to fire into the expiratory phase compared to the parasternal intercostal muscles, with statistically significant differences observed when comparing the diaphragm to the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles.

During voluntary breathing, no significant differences in motor unit firing frequencies were found between the 1st and 3rd parasternal intercostal muscles compared to quiet breathing. However, duration ratios were significantly higher during voluntary breathing for both the 3rd and 5th parasternal intercostals, suggesting an earlier recruitment of motor unit firing activity relative to the onset of inspiration.

DISCUSSION

The study investigates Persistent Inward Currents (PICs) contribution to motor unit firing in human respiratory muscles, particularly the diaphragm and parasternal intercostal muscles during quiet breathing and voluntary breaths.

Key findings suggest that PICs may have a less prominent role in inspiring motoneurons compared to limb motoneurons, with lower and more variable magnitudes observed in parasternal intercostal motoneurons. No significant differences were found between quiet and voluntary breaths.

The study used an indirect method called 'F' to estimate PICs. Challenges and limitations include the method's applicability across different motoneuron pools, underactivation of calcium PIC channels due to short breath durations, and uncertainty about full PIC deactivation between breaths.

Future research is proposed to investigate PIC's role during post-inspiratory activity, expiration, and its impact on lung volumes and respiratory mechanics. Further studies should corroborate or refine findings using interventions that elicit inhibitory inputs onto motoneurons, monoaminergic drugs, studies examining aging, and larger, sex-balanced samples to determine sex-related differences in PIC contribution to respiratory motoneuron firing. Lastly, the paper suggests exploring new methodological paradigms and studying PICs in parasternal intercostal muscles during ipsilateral rotations and non-respiratory activities.

METHODOLOGY

The methodology section of this scientific paper explores the role of intrinsic motoneuron excitability in controlling firing output in human respiratory motor neurons. The study utilizes phasically active units (PICs) as a focus, which are known for their self-sustained firing, as established by previous research.

The paper investigates whether the firing of these motor units is sustained by ongoing excitatory input or strong intrinsic motoneuron excitability, such as subthreshold oscillations of sodium PICs. Additionally, it suggests the need for further methodological approaches to investigate the role of PIC channels in various behaviors like sneezing, coughing, breath-holds, trunk rotations, vocalization, swallowing, or vomiting.

The paper concludes that intrinsic motoneuron excitability contributes to the control of firing output in human respiratory motor neurons, although this contribution is heterogeneously expressed across inspiratory muscles. The findings suggest that PICs contribute significantly to the prolonged firing of diaphragmatic motor units during quiet breathing but not as evidently in the 1st, 3rd, and 5th parasternal intercostal motor units during both quiet and voluntary breaths. Future research is recommended to explore the mechanisms driving these variations in non-linear firing patterns and their functional implications for respiratory control in health and disease.
