Final Summary

In the study 'Neural mechanisms underlying the recovery of voluntary control of motoneurons after paralysis with spinal cord stimulation' (Balaguer et al., 2025), researchers aimed to understand how spinal cord stimulation (SCS) facilitates voluntary movement in individuals with spinal cord injury and stroke. The study revealed that residual supraspinal inputs modulate motoneuron membrane potentials, allowing subthreshold SCS pulses to transform into action potentials and entrain motoneuron activity to the stimulation. This entrainment allows for brain control of firing rates by regulating the number of transformed subthreshold SCS pulses.

The research team developed a computational model of the motoneuron membrane potential, which was tested against motor unit recordings, and found that motoneuron action potentials can be entrained to the stimulation interpulse interval by SCS pulses in the presence of residual supraspinal inputs. These findings refute the hypothesis that SCS increases tonic motoneuron excitability but offer an alternative explanation for facilitating voluntary movement with SCS.

The study also found that only a subset of SCS parameters enables volitional motor control, and this functional parameter space shrinks with lesion severity. Furthermore, supraspinal inhibitory drive can enlarge the effective SCS parameter range, but lesion severity still reduces its size. In humans with corticospinal lesions, participants were able to inhibit unwanted flexor reflexes induced by SCS during voluntary arm extension, demonstrating their ability to volitionally inhibit suprathreshold SCS.

The study's findings have potential implications for treating neurological disorders like spinal cord injuries, where SCS could help restore motor function. The research proposes that participants can leverage residual supraspinal inhibition to control motoneuron firing rate by minimizing unwanted SCS activity and maximizing it through training. However, the study's limitations include focusing on immediate SCS effects and not accounting for long-term changes due to physical training.

INTRODUCTION

This scientific paper introduces research focused on understanding how epidural electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS) facilitates voluntary movement in individuals with spinal cord injury and stroke. Clinical trials have shown that SCS enables people with paralysis to control previously paralyzed limbs, but the exact mechanism behind this phenomenon remains unclear.

Existing research has explored neural pathways recruited by SCS and the involvement of movement-generating spinal circuits such as reflexes, synergies, and central pattern generators. However, these studies have not considered the contribution of residual supraspinal fibers, which are crucial for voluntary control.

The leading hypothesis is that SCS increases the excitability of spinal circuits, making motoneurons more responsive to supraspinal input. This hypothesis suggests that pulsatile stimulation could lead to membrane modulation of motoneurons, but it has not been tested experimentally.

Prior work has established that SCS directly activates sensory afferents in the dorsal roots and dorsal columns, creating short-duration excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) to spinal motoneurons. However, these EPSPs are too brief to significantly depolarize the motoneuron membrane without high stimulation frequencies.

This paper aims to study how residual supraspinal excitatory drive and SCS pulses interact in the motoneuron membrane to enable voluntary motor control. The researchers developed a model of the motoneuron membrane potential, tested its predictions against motor unit recordings, and found that motoneuron action potentials can be entrained to the stimulation interpulse interval by SCS pulses in the presence of residual supraspinal inputs. These findings refute the hypothesis that SCS increases tonic motoneuron excitability but offer an alternative explanation for facilitating voluntary movement with SCS. The study also reveals that only a subset of SCS parameters enables volitional motor control and that this functional parameter space shrinks with lesion severity.

RESULTS

The study investigated the modulation of force during spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in humans and the impact of lesion severity and supraspinal inhibitory drive on the effective range of SCS parameters.

Key findings: 1. Highly entrained motor units showed clear peaks at multiples of the SCS interpulse intervals for all force levels, yet participants could modulate firing to follow a sinusoidal profile. Higher force levels led to less pulse skipping by shifting the ISI histogram toward smaller values. 2. The stimulation amplitude required to enable voluntary production of force was close to the involuntary movement regime border, and increasing the stimulation amplitude led to involuntary motor responses. 3. A biophysical model showed that increasing lesion severity reduced the size of the voluntary movement regime, with higher SCS amplitudes needed for voluntary movement in severe lesions. 4. In a monkey, low SCS amplitudes did not potentiate muscle activity produced by ICS, whereas higher—but still subthreshold—SCS amplitudes potentiated muscle activity (voluntary movement regime). 5. Supraspinal inhibitory drive could enlarge the effective SCS parameter range, transforming action potentials triggered by supra-threshold SCS into subthreshold EPSPs and expanding the voluntary regime into the involuntary suprathreshold movement regime. However, lesion severity still reduced the voluntary regime size. 6. In humans with corticospinal lesions, participants were able to inhibit unwanted flexor reflexes induced by SCS during voluntary arm extension, demonstrating their ability to volitionally inhibit suprathreshold SCS.

DISCUSSION

The scientific research article 'Recovery of Voluntary Control Over Motoneurons During Spinal Cord Stimulation' published in Neuron explores the integration of artificially induced activity to restore voluntary control over motoneuron firing during spinal cord stimulation (SCS).

The study used a computational model, revealing that highly entrained neural firing rates can still be modulated by natural activity. This mechanism may apply to other pulsatile neurostimulation technologies. The findings have potential implications for treating neurological disorders like spinal cord injuries, where SCS could help restore motor function.

The research proposes that participants can leverage residual supraspinal inhibition to control motoneuron firing rate by minimizing unwanted SCS activity and maximizing it through training. However, the study's limitations include focusing on immediate SCS effects and not accounting for long-term changes due to physical training.
