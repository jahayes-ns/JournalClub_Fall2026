ABSTRACT

The scientific paper discusses the impact of spinal cord injury (SCI) on the brain-spinal cord axis, which generates movement by combining motor primitives into complex behaviors. SCI disrupts this neuroaxis, impairing both locomotion and other behaviors in the full repertoire. Traditional methods for quantifying recovery after SCI focus on isolated aspects of movement, particularly those involving locomotion, and exclude non-locomotor actions like grooming or pausing.

To better understand SCI recovery, researchers adapted a machine-learning framework called motion sequencing (MoSeq) to segment spontaneous behavior into sub-second "syllables" (actions like locomotion, rearing, grooming). Using this approach, they found that SCI alters the composition of both locomotor and non-locomotor behaviors across a range of injury severities. The changes in behavior sequences are primarily shaped by time post-injury, indicating a shared temporal structure to recovery.

Syllable-level descriptions revealed hidden heterogeneity among animals assigned identical BMS scores, uncovering new insights into SCI recovery. Compressing repertoire-wide behavior into a single metric also uncovered clusters of coevolving locomotor and non-locomotor behaviors, positioning SCI recovery as repertoire-level changes under a disrupted brain-spinal cord axis where adaptive strategies emerge from constrained access to motor primitives and their flexible assembly.

RESULTS

This study analyzes the behavior of mice with spinal cord injuries (SCIs) using a method called "syllable-based descriptions." The method captures unique biomechanical signatures of locomotion and reveals hidden heterogeneity across animals during recovery.

The analysis involved categorizing specific behaviors, or "syllables," into normal, injured, or paralyzed based on mouse behavior. A diffusion map was created to summarize the variation in behavior across recovery stages. Five clusters of behavioral syllables were identified: Full Paralysis, Severe Paraparesis, Intermediate Paraparesis, Easy Normal, and Challenging Normal.

The study found that repertoire-level descriptions provide a more complete account of functional status compared to traditional methods like the Biersky and Mendelsohn Scale (BMS). The recovery score, derived from syllable usages, demonstrated better linear model fits than BMS scores. The study included 71 mice, with statistical tests performed on the data but not presented in this summary.

The findings suggest that recovery after SCI might involve selective access to functionally achievable subsets of behavioral solutions under physical constraints related to injury severity. The method could have implications for understanding neurological consequences of spinal cord injuries and informing rehabilitation strategies.
