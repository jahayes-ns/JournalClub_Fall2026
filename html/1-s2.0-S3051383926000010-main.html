Tanycytic degeneration impairs tau clearance and contributes to Alzheimer’s disease pathology

Sauve´, Martinez-Go´ mez, Mbouamboua, Ternier, Nampoothiri, Dupre´, Garcia, Couralet, Dewisme, Lebouvier, Danis, Rasika, Dhenain, Kim, Ciofi, Bue´ e, Landrieu, Pasquier, Lilamand, Paquet, Giacobini, Barbry, Maurage, Nogueiras, Schwaninger, Prevot | 2026

Final Summary

The study 'Tanycytic degeneration impairs tau clearance and contributes to Alzheimer’s disease pathology' by Prevot et al. investigates the role of specialized hypothalamic cells, known as tanycytes, in clearing tau protein associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD). The research question focuses on understanding the clearance mechanism of tau from the brain and its implications in AD pathophysiology.

The methods employed include experiments using animal and cellular models, along with patient tissue analysis. Findings reveal that tanycytes transport tau from the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to the blood via the hypothalamic median eminence, and when vesicular transport in tanycytes is blocked, CSF-to-blood tau efflux is hindered, worsening AD pathology.

In AD patients, plasma-to-CSF ratios of total and p181 tau are reduced, suggesting a clearance deficit in tanycytes. Furthermore, postmortem analysis of tanycytes from AD patient brains showed significant fragmentation and transcriptomic alterations, particularly in genes related to vesicular transport, which could explain this clearance deficiency.

Overall, the study highlights the involvement of tanycytic dysfunction in human AD pathophysiology and provides evidence for a brain-to-blood tanycytic shuttle, with far-reaching implications for understanding and potentially treating AD.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction outlines the significant public health concern posed by Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other age-related cognitive disorders, such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD). The hallmarks of AD pathogenesis include the accumulation of amyloid beta peptide (Aβ) and microtubule-associated protein tau in the brain, leading to neuronal dysfunction and death. While these proteins are cleared under physiological conditions, their impaired clearance contributes to the neuronal pathology in AD. Tau is found in increased concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with AD, and it is suggested that the CSF plays a role in its multistep clearance from the brain. However, the rapid kinetics of tau's clearance from the CSF to the blood remain unclear, suggesting the existence of alternative clearance pathways.

A recent single-cell transcriptomic study has identified peculiar ependymoglial cells called tanycytes in the hypothalamus as hotspots for age-related changes in gene expression. Tanycytes are situated between the CSF and the pituitary portal circulation, serving as a bridge between these two compartments. They express tight junction complexes that seal their cell bodies together at the ventricular wall, acting as gatekeepers to the hypothalamus and actively shuttling blood-borne signals into the CSF. The study investigates whether tanycytes are also capable of shuttling molecules in the reverse direction, specifically tau from the CSF to the blood, and explores the relevance of these cells for the pathophysiology of AD and other dementias using animal and cellular models, postmortem brains from patients with AD and FTD, and blood and CSF samples from two independent AD patient cohorts.

RESULTS

The combined results from various scientific studies discuss the role of tanycytes, brain cells found in the hypothalamus, in Alzheimer's disease (AD).

In AD patients, tanycytes exhibit pathology such as cytoskeleton fragmentation or beading, which is more frequent in the infundibulum and internal zone of the median eminence. This pathology was not observed in patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD).

A study published in Cell Press Blue found significant differences in tanycyte morphology between AD patients and control subjects, with tanycytes in AD patients being degraded and disorganized, showing an increase in fragmented areas. This study also revealed that cellular stress response pathways were activated in tanycytes of AD patients, leading to the triggering of cell death mechanisms.

The findings suggest that significant tanycytic changes occur in AD, extending beyond the observed beading phenotype and hinting at a global dysregulation of tanycyte function. The studies highlight the importance of understanding tau clearance and tanycyte function in neurodegenerative diseases like AD.

Regarding tau protein clearance, another study found that the ratio of tau levels in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is significantly higher in AD patients compared to control subjects, suggesting a clearance deficit for tau in AD. This deficit was independent of blood-brain barrier alterations.

In summary, the studies suggest that tanycytic pathology could be a feature of neurodegenerative disorders like AD, potentially interfering with tau efflux from the CSF and contributing to its accumulation in the brain. The findings emphasize the need for further research into tanycyte function and its role in managing neurodegenerative diseases associated with tau protein accumulation.

DISCUSSION

This discussion section of a scientific paper focuses on research about tau protein, a key component in neurofibrillary tangles found in Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients. The study investigates the role of tanycytes, brain cells lining the ventricular system and hypothalamus, in tau protein clearance.

Findings from animal models show that tanycytes transport tau proteins from the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to other parts of the brain for breakdown and removal. However, in AD patients, tanycytes demonstrate degeneration and fragmentation, potentially impairing their clearance ability.

Human brain data showed a lower ratio of CSF-to-plasma tau protein in AD patients compared to controls, suggesting a specific tau clearance deficit. This finding was consistent across two different patient cohorts diagnosed based on cognitive and biological criteria.

The study also revealed degeneration and fragmentation of tanycytes in the hypothalamus of AD patients at various ages and disease stages, hinting at their potential role in the pathogenesis rather than a consequence of tau overload.

Further research is needed to understand the role of tanycytes in neurodegenerative diseases and their potential as therapeutic targets. The study employed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) to analyze gene expression profiles in human tanycytes, gaining insights into their functions.

Additionally, the research team investigated the internalization of 2N4R Tau and Tau-565 by primary tanycytes using methods like immunocytochemistry, western blotting, a primary tanycytic Tau-565 release assay, live imaging, and fluorescence microscopy. Key findings show both 2N4R Tau and Tau-565 can be internalized by primary tanycytes, with the internalization process possibly involving endocytosis and autophagy. Moreover, Tau-565 release from primary tanycytes was found to be significantly higher than 2N4R Tau. Quantification and statistical analysis were performed to confirm these findings.
