Tanycytic degeneration impairs tau clearance and contributes to Alzheimer’s disease pathology

Sauve´, Martinez-Go´ mez, Mbouamboua, Ternier, Nampoothiri, Dupre´, Garcia, Couralet, Dewisme, Lebouvier, Danis, Rasika, Dhenain, Kim, Ciofi, Bue´ e, Landrieu, Pasquier, Lilamand, Paquet, Giacobini, Barbry, Maurage, Nogueiras, Schwaninger, Prevot | 2026

Final Summary

In a recent study led by Vincent Prevot, researchers found that specialized hypothalamic cells called tanycytes play a crucial role in clearing tau protein accumulation associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD). Using animal models, cellular models, and patient tissue samples, the team demonstrated that these cells act as a bridge between the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood, shuttling tau from the CSF to the blood. In AD patients, dysfunctional tanycytes contribute to impaired tau clearance, leading to its buildup in the brain. The study's findings suggest that targeting these cells could potentially aid in understanding and addressing the underlying mechanisms of Alzheimer's disease.

Research methods included immunohistology, single-nucleus RNA sequencing, light-sheet microscopy, ELISA techniques such as immunocytochemistry, western blotting, primary tanycytic Tau-565 release assay, and live imaging. The paper was published in March 2026 after revisions in January 2026.

Section Summaries

PREAMBLE

In a recent study led by Vincent Prevot, the authors have found that specialized hypothalamic cells called tanycytes play a crucial role in clearing tau protein accumulation associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD). Tanycytes bridge the brain's cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood by shuttling tau from the CSF to the blood, preventing its buildup.

In AD patients, these cells are fragmented and dysfunctional. The research team demonstrated this using animal models, cellular models, and patient tissue samples. In mice, blocking tanycytic vesicular transport reduces the tau clearance from the brain to the blood, exacerbating tau pathology.

In AD patients, plasma-to-CSF ratios of total and p181 tau are reduced, indicating a deficit in tau clearance. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing revealed significant transcriptomic alterations in vesicular transport genes within tanycytes from postmortem AD patient brains. These findings suggest that the dysfunction of these cells contributes to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease, with far-reaching implications for understanding and potentially targeting the underlying mechanisms of this debilitating condition.

INTRODUCTION

In the introduction of this scientific paper, the authors discuss Alzheimer's disease (AD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) as significant public health concerns in aging societies. The hallmarks of AD pathogenesis include the accumulation of amyloid beta peptide (Aβ) and microtubule-associated protein tau, leading to neuronal dysfunction and death. These proteins are released from cells and cleared from the brain under normal conditions, but their impaired clearance contributes to AD pathology.

Tau is found in increased concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of AD patients, where it exists as soluble forms with post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and truncation. The CSF is thought to be an intermediary destination in the multistep clearance of tau from the brain. However, while CSF-borne tau can travel to the blood, the cellular mechanisms responsible for this transfer remain incomplete or inconsistent with the rapid kinetics of its clearance.

Recent research suggests that hypothalamic regions near the third ventricle, particularly ependymoglial cells called tanycytes, are involved in age-related changes in gene expression and may play a role in brain-to-blood transfer. Tanycytes are located between the CSF and the pituitary portal circulation and act as a bridge between the two, regulating the transport of molecules. The authors aim to investigate whether tanycytes can transport tau from the CSF to the blood and explore their relevance for the pathophysiology of AD and other dementias using animal and cellular models, postmortem brains from patients with AD and FTD, and blood and CSF samples from two independent AD patient cohorts.

RESULTS

The combined results of the scientific papers can be summarized as follows:

  1. A study investigates the role of tanycytes in clearing tau proteins from the brain to the blood, suggesting their importance in tau efflux under physiological conditions and potential contribution to the onset and progression of tauopathies like Alzheimer's disease when disrupted.

  2. Another study focuses on tanycytic pathology in neurodegenerative disorders, specifically AD and FTD. It found that tanycytes undergo a unique form of pathology in AD characterized by fragmentation or beading of their cytoskeleton, potentially interfering with tau efflux from the cerebrospinal fluid and contributing to its accumulation in the brain.

  3. In Alzheimer's Disease (AD) patients, tanycytes were found to be degraded and disorganized compared to controls. This fragmentation involved the entire tanycytic process and led to the upregulation of genes related to cellular stress response pathways, deregulation of tanycytic transport mechanisms, and cell death pathways.

In summary, these studies highlight the crucial role of tanycytes in tau clearance and their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease due to their fragmentation and dysregulation in AD patients.

DISCUSSION

In a scientific research paper, researchers from various institutions studied the role of tanycytes, a specific type of brain cell in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The study was conducted using methods like immunohistology, single-nucleus RNA sequencing, and light-sheet microscopy.

The researchers found that tanycytes in the hypothalamus take up tau efficiently, but in AD brains, they undergo morphological changes such as fragmentation and beading. This could indicate a potential causative role for these changes in tau accumulation in the brain. The study also revealed that genes involved in tanycytic transport and metabolic control are dysregulated in AD, possibly due to cellular stressors like metal ions, hypoxia, or oxidative stress.

The authors propose that these stressors may induce a cascade of events leading to the fragmentation and death of tanycytes, further contributing to the accumulation of pathological tau in the brain. The importance of tanycytic integrity for healthy cognitive aging is emphasized.
